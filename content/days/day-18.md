# Introduction: Build to Learn, Not to Ship

Many companies make this mistake:

```text id="3lqvpk"
Idea
 ↓
6 Months Development
 ↓
Launch
 ↓
Users Don't Care
```

Instead, successful teams work like this:

```text id="zw6bl8"
Idea
 ↓
Prototype
 ↓
Experiment
 ↓
Learn
 ↓
Improve
 ↓
Build
```

The purpose of experimentation is not to prove that your idea is correct.

The purpose is to **learn as quickly and cheaply as possible.**

---

# What Is a Prototype?

A prototype is an early representation of a product or feature.

Its purpose is to:

- Explore ideas
- Test usability
- Validate assumptions
- Collect customer feedback

A prototype is **not** the final product.

---

# Why Product Managers Use Prototypes

Prototypes help answer questions like:

- Do users understand this solution?
- Can users complete the task?
- Does this solve the problem?
- Is the interaction intuitive?

Instead of debating internally, teams can observe real users.

---

# Benefits of Prototyping

## Faster Learning

Instead of spending months building, you can test ideas in days.

---

## Lower Cost

Changing a prototype is much cheaper than changing production software.

---

## Better Collaboration

Designers, engineers, and stakeholders can discuss something tangible instead of abstract ideas.

---

## Reduced Risk

Problems are discovered before development.

---

# Fidelity in Prototypes

Prototype fidelity refers to how realistic the prototype is.

There are two common categories:

- Low-Fidelity
- High-Fidelity

---

# Low-Fidelity Prototypes

Simple representations of an idea.

Examples:

- Paper sketches
- Whiteboard drawings
- Wireframes
- Basic clickable flows

Purpose:

Explore concepts quickly.

Advantages:

- Fast
- Cheap
- Easy to modify

Disadvantages:

- Limited realism

---

# High-Fidelity Prototypes

Close to the final product.

Examples:

- Figma prototypes
- Interactive mockups
- Clickable mobile designs

Advantages:

- Realistic experience
- Better usability testing

Disadvantages:

- More time to create

---

# When to Use Each

## Early Discovery

Use:

Low-fidelity

Reason:

Ideas change frequently.

---

## Before Development

Use:

High-fidelity

Reason:

Validate detailed interactions.

---

# Prototype vs MVP

These terms are often confused.

---

# Prototype

Purpose:

Learn.

Characteristics:

- Not production-ready
- May not contain real functionality
- Used for testing ideas

---

# MVP (Minimum Viable Product)

Purpose:

Deliver value while learning.

Characteristics:

- Real product
- Used by real customers
- Contains only essential functionality

---

Example:

Investment education feature

Prototype:

A clickable design showing educational screens.

MVP:

A simple working education module available to real users.

---

# What Is an Experiment?

An experiment is a structured way to test an assumption.

Every experiment begins with uncertainty.

Example:

Assumption:

> Beginner investors need educational guidance.

Experiment:

Test a simple onboarding tutorial.

---

# Hypothesis-Driven Product Development

Good experiments begin with hypotheses.

Template:

> We believe that [solution] for [user] will improve [metric]. We'll know we're right if [success criteria].

Example:

> We believe that adding investment tutorials for beginner investors will increase first-trade completion by 15%.

---

# Anatomy of a Good Experiment

Every experiment should define:

## Goal

What are you trying to learn?

---

## Hypothesis

What do you believe?

---

## Target Users

Who will participate?

---

## Success Metric

How will you measure success?

---

## Time Frame

How long will the experiment run?

---

# Types of Product Experiments

---

# 1. Usability Testing

Purpose:

Can users complete tasks?

Example:

Observe users trying to open a trading account.

Questions:

- Where do they hesitate?
- Where do they become confused?

---

# 2. Prototype Testing

Purpose:

Validate design before development.

Users interact with a prototype while explaining their thoughts.

---

# 3. Fake Door Test

Purpose:

Measure demand.

Example:

Display a new menu item:

> AI Portfolio Advisor

When clicked:

> "Coming Soon"

Measure click rate.

High clicks indicate strong interest.

---

# 4. Landing Page Experiment

Purpose:

Validate customer interest.

Create a landing page describing a feature.

Measure:

- Sign-ups
- Email registrations
- Waitlist joins

---

# 5. Concierge MVP

Instead of software, humans provide the service manually.

Example:

Before building AI investment recommendations,

A financial advisor creates recommendations manually.

Learn customer expectations before automation.

---

# 6. Wizard of Oz MVP

Looks automated.

Actually performed manually behind the scenes.

Customer believes software performs the task.

The team learns without full implementation.

---

# 7. A/B Testing

Compare two alternatives.

Example:

Version A:

Traditional onboarding.

Version B:

Guided onboarding.

Compare:

- Activation
- Completion
- Retention

---

# Choosing the Right Experiment

Ask:

## What assumption is most risky?

Example:

Will customers use the feature?

A Fake Door test may be enough.

---

Can users understand the design?

Prototype testing is appropriate.

---

Does the feature improve conversion?

Run an A/B test.

---

# Experiment Metrics

Choose metrics before running the experiment.

Examples:

## Activation Rate

Percentage of users completing onboarding.

---

## Conversion Rate

Percentage of users completing the desired action.

---

## Task Success Rate

Percentage of users completing a task without help.

---

## Time on Task

How long does completion take?

---

## Error Rate

How often do users make mistakes?

---

## Customer Satisfaction

Collected through surveys after testing.

---

# Example: LinkedIn

Problem:

Users don't complete their profiles.

---

Hypothesis:

Providing AI profile suggestions will improve completion.

---

Experiment:

Prototype AI suggestions.

Observe:

- Do users understand them?
- Do users accept them?

---

Success Metrics:

- Profile completion rate
- User satisfaction
- Time required

---

# Example: Stock Trading Platform

Problem:

New investors hesitate before their first trade.

---

Possible Experiment:

Prototype:

Guided first investment.

Observe:

- Confidence
- Task completion
- Questions asked

---

Possible MVP:

Release the guided flow to 5% of new users.

Compare:

- First-trade completion
- Retention
- Customer satisfaction

---

# Experiment Design Checklist

Before every experiment ask:

✔ What assumption are we testing?

✔ What evidence will support or reject it?

✔ How many users do we need?

✔ What metric defines success?

✔ What decision will we make afterward?

---

# Common Experiment Mistakes

## Mistake #1

Testing multiple assumptions at once.

Keep experiments focused.

---

## Mistake #2

Running experiments without success criteria.

Define metrics first.

---

## Mistake #3

Ignoring negative results.

Failed experiments create valuable learning.

---

## Mistake #4

Building before testing.

Learn first.

Build later.

---

## Mistake #5

Stopping experiments too early.

Collect enough evidence before making decisions.

---

# Interview Question

## Question

Why are prototypes important in Product Management?

## Strong Answer

Prototypes allow Product Managers to validate ideas before investing in development. They help teams gather customer feedback, test usability, reduce risk, and improve solutions quickly and inexpensively.

---

Idea:

Add a "Guided Investment Assistant."

---

# Key Takeaways

1. Prototypes help teams learn before building.
2. Low-fidelity prototypes are ideal for early exploration.
3. High-fidelity prototypes validate detailed interactions.
4. MVPs deliver real value while continuing to learn.
5. Every experiment should test one key assumption.
6. Define success metrics before starting.
7. Product Managers optimize learning speed, not development speed.

# PM Reflection

Think about your current product.

Ask yourself:

- Which assumptions are we making without evidence?
- Could we test this idea in one week instead of one quarter?
- What is the smallest experiment that could help us learn?
- Are we building software, or are we building knowledge?

The best Product Managers do not try to eliminate uncertainty.

They reduce uncertainty through rapid learning.
