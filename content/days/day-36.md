# 1. What Is Agile?

**Agile** is an approach to product development that emphasizes:

- Iterative development
- Customer feedback
- Continuous learning
- Collaboration
- Adaptability
- Frequent delivery of value

The core idea is:

> **Build something, learn from it, adapt, and continue.**

Instead of trying to predict everything before development begins, Agile accepts that requirements and understanding will evolve.

---

# 2. Why Was Agile Created?

Traditional software development often followed a sequential process:

```text
Requirements
    ↓
Design
    ↓
Development
    ↓
Testing
    ↓
Launch
````

The problem was that customers might not see the product until very late.

By then, the team might discover:

> "This isn't what customers actually needed."

Changing direction could be expensive.

Agile approaches try to shorten this feedback loop.

---

# 3. Waterfall vs. Agile

## Waterfall

```text
Plan everything
      ↓
Build everything
      ↓
Test everything
      ↓
Launch
      ↓
Learn
```

## Agile

```text
Plan
 ↓
Build small increment
 ↓
Test
 ↓
Release / Review
 ↓
Learn
 ↓
Adapt
 ↓
Repeat
```

The fundamental difference is how each approach handles uncertainty and change.

---

# 4. Agile Does Not Mean "No Planning"

A common misconception is:

> "Agile means we don't plan."

That's incorrect.

Agile involves planning at multiple levels:

* Product strategy
* Roadmap
* Release
* Sprint
* Daily work

The difference is that Agile recognizes:

> **Plans are based on assumptions and may need to change when new information appears.**

---

# 5. The Agile Manifesto

The Agile Manifesto emphasizes four values.

### 1. Individuals and interactions

over

> processes and tools

### 2. Working software

over

> comprehensive documentation

### 3. Customer collaboration

over

> contract negotiation

### 4. Responding to change

over

> following a plan

This does **not** mean the items on the right are useless.

It means the items on the left are valued more highly.

# 6. Agile Principles

Some important Agile principles include:

* Deliver valuable software frequently.
* Welcome changing requirements.
* Work closely with customers.
* Build around motivated individuals.
* Prefer direct communication.
* Working software is an important measure of progress.
* Maintain a sustainable pace.
* Continuously improve.
* Keep things simple.
* Regularly reflect and adjust.

The overall theme is:

> **Learn quickly and continuously improve.**

# 7. What Is Scrum?

**Scrum** is a specific framework for implementing Agile product development.

Agile is a broader philosophy and set of principles.

Scrum is one framework that provides:

* Roles
* Events
* Artifacts
* Rules
* A development cadence

Other Agile approaches include:

* Kanban
* Extreme Programming (XP)
* Lean
* Shape Up

---

```text

     ↓
Sprint
     ↓
Increment
     ↓
```

# 9. Scrum Roles

Scrum defines three accountabilities:

1. Product Owner
2. Scrum Master
3. Developers

Notice that Scrum doesn't formally define a "Product Manager" role.

A Product Manager may exist alongside Scrum or may perform some Product Owner responsibilities depending on the organization.

---

# 10. Product Owner

The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing the value of the product resulting from the work of the Scrum Team.

Typical responsibilities include:

* Product goal
* Product backlog
* Backlog ordering
* Clarifying requirements
* Communicating product priorities
* Working with stakeholders

The Product Owner should understand:

> What should the team work on next, and why?

---

# 11. Scrum Master

The Scrum Master helps the team effectively use Scrum.

Typical responsibilities include:

* Facilitating Scrum events
* Helping remove impediments
* Coaching the team
* Improving collaboration
* Supporting Scrum adoption
* Helping the organization understand Scrum

The Scrum Master is not simply:

> "The person who schedules meetings."

---

# 12. Developers

In Scrum, "Developers" means everyone on the Scrum Team who creates the product increment.

This can include:

* Software engineers
* QA specialists
* Data specialists
* Other technical contributors

The key idea is:

> The team collectively creates the product increment.

---

# 13. Product Manager vs. Product Owner

This is an important interview topic.

A **Product Manager** is typically responsible for broader product outcomes, such as:

* Product strategy
* Market understanding
* Customer problems
* Business goals
* Product vision
* Roadmap
* Prioritization

A **Product Owner** in Scrum focuses heavily on:

* Product backlog
* Backlog ordering
* Sprint-level product decisions
* Clarifying work for the development team

In some companies:

> One person performs both roles.

In others:

> They are separate roles.

---

# 14. Product Backlog

The **Product Backlog** is an ordered list of work that may be needed to improve the product.

It can contain:

* Features
* User stories
* Bugs
* Technical work
* Research
* Experiments
* Improvements

The backlog is not simply:

> A list of everything anyone has ever requested.

It should reflect current product priorities.

---

# 15. Backlog Ordering

The Product Owner is responsible for ordering the backlog in Scrum.

The highest-priority items should generally be:

* Valuable
* Important
* Sufficiently understood
* Ready for development

The exact prioritization method can vary.

Possible factors:

* Customer value
* Business impact
* Strategic importance
* Risk
* Urgency
* Dependencies
* Cost
* Regulatory requirements

---

# 16. Product Backlog vs. Sprint Backlog

### Product Backlog

> Everything that may need to be done.

### Sprint Backlog

> The work selected for the current Sprint plus the team's plan for delivering it.

Example:

```text
PRODUCT BACKLOG

Feature A
Feature B
Feature C
Bug D
Experiment E
Feature F
Feature G
```

Sprint team selects:

```text
SPRINT BACKLOG

Feature B
Bug D
Experiment E
```

---

# 17. What Is a Sprint?

A **Sprint** is a fixed period during which the team works to create a valuable product increment.

A Sprint is commonly:

> 1–4 weeks.

Many Scrum teams use:

> 2-week Sprints.

The important principle is:

> A Sprint creates a predictable opportunity for the team to inspect and adapt.

---

# 18. Sprint Goal

Every Sprint should have a meaningful goal.

Bad:

> Complete 12 tickets.

Better:

> Enable new customers to complete the first step of onboarding without contacting support.

A Sprint Goal focuses the team on:

> **Why this work matters.**

---

# 19. Sprint Planning

Sprint Planning answers two major questions:

### Why?

> What is the goal of this Sprint?

### What?

> What work can help achieve that goal?

The team then creates a Sprint Backlog.

---

# 20. Sprint Planning Example

Suppose the product goal is:

> Improve insurance claim completion.

Sprint Goal:

> Reduce friction in document submission.

Potential Sprint work:

* Design new document checklist
* Implement checklist API
* Implement upload validation
* Add analytics events
* Improve upload error states

The work should contribute to the Sprint Goal.

---

# 21. Daily Scrum

The **Daily Scrum** is a short event for Developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt their plan.

A common traditional format asks:

1. What did you do yesterday?
2. What will you do today?
3. What blockers do you have?

This format can work, but it isn't the fundamental purpose.

The real purpose is:

> **Inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan.**

---

# 22. Sprint Review

At the end of the Sprint, the team reviews the product increment with stakeholders.

The team can discuss:

* What was built
* What was learned
* What changed
* Customer feedback
* Product metrics
* What should happen next

The Sprint Review is not simply:

> A demo meeting.

It should be an opportunity for product inspection and adaptation.

---

# 23. Sprint Retrospective

The Retrospective focuses on the team's process.

The team asks:

* What went well?
* What didn't go well?
* What should we change?
* What should we continue?
* What experiment should we run next?

The goal is:

> **Continuous improvement.**

---

# 24. Sprint Review vs. Retrospective

This distinction is important.

### Sprint Review

Focus:

> **Product**

Questions:

* What did we build?
* What did customers/stakeholders think?
* What should we do next?

### Retrospective

Focus:

> **Team/process**

Questions:

* How did we work?
* What slowed us down?
* How can we improve?

---

# 25. Backlog Refinement

**Backlog refinement** is the ongoing activity of improving and clarifying upcoming backlog items.

The team may:

* Clarify requirements
* Break down large items
* Identify dependencies
* Discuss edge cases
* Estimate work
* Remove outdated items
* Reprioritize items

The goal is to make upcoming work sufficiently understood.

---

# 26. Definition of Ready

**Definition of Ready (DoR)** is a team's agreed set of conditions for when a backlog item is sufficiently prepared for development.

For example:

* Problem is understood.
* User story is clear.
* Acceptance criteria exist.
* Designs are available when needed.
* Dependencies are known.
* Major questions are answered.

DoR is not an official mandatory Scrum artifact.

It is a team practice.

---

# 27. Definition of Done

**Definition of Done (DoD)** describes the conditions that must be met for work to be considered complete.

Example:

A feature is Done when:

* Code is implemented.
* Code is reviewed.
* Automated tests pass.
* QA is complete.
* Analytics are implemented.
* Documentation is updated if required.
* The feature is deployed.

The exact definition varies by team.

---

# 28. Done vs. "Code Complete"

This is an important distinction.

> Code is written.

does not necessarily mean:

> The product work is Done.

A feature might still need:

* Testing
* Monitoring
* Documentation
* Security review
* Deployment
* Analytics
* Release configuration

The Definition of Done creates a shared standard.

---

# 29. User Stories

A common format:

> As a [user], I want [capability], so that [benefit].

Example:

> As a job seeker, I want to save jobs so that I can review them later.

User stories are useful because they keep the conversation focused on user value.

But a user story is not necessarily enough detail to build something.

It may need:

* Acceptance criteria
* Designs
* Business rules
* Edge cases
* Technical details

---

# 30. Story Splitting

Large stories should often be split into smaller pieces.

Bad:

> Build the entire payment system.

That's too large.

Better:

1. Display payment options.
2. Add payment method.
3. Submit payment.
4. Show payment confirmation.
5. Handle failed payment.
6. Store transaction status.
7. Allow payment retry.

Smaller work items improve planning and learning.

---

# 31. Vertical Slicing

A useful way to split work is **vertical slicing**.

Instead of:

```text
Frontend
Backend
Database
QA
```

for one huge feature, try to create a small end-to-end slice.

For example:

> User can pay using one payment method.

That slice may include:

* Frontend
* Backend
* Database
* Payment integration
* Testing

It delivers a small but complete capability.

---

# 32. Horizontal vs. Vertical Slicing

### Horizontal

```text
Frontend
   ↓
Backend
   ↓
Database
```

Each layer is developed separately.

### Vertical

```text
Small customer capability
        ↓
Frontend + Backend + Database
```

The team delivers an end-to-end capability.

Vertical slices often make it easier to deliver value incrementally.

---

# 33. Estimation

Agile teams often estimate work to help with planning.

Common approaches include:

* Story Points
* T-shirt sizes
* Ideal days
* Hours

Estimation should help answer:

> How much work can we reasonably take on?

It should not be treated as a guarantee.

---

# 34. Story Points

Story Points are a relative measure of effort or complexity.

For example:

```text
1 point
2 points
3 points
5 points
8 points
13 points
```

A 5-point story is not necessarily:

> 5 days.

It simply represents more relative effort/uncertainty than a 3-point story.

---

# 35. Why Fibonacci Numbers?

Teams often use:

> 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13...

The increasing gaps reflect increasing uncertainty.

For example:

A small story may clearly be:

> 2 points.

A complex story may be:

> 13 points.

The team recognizes:

> We don't have enough precision to distinguish exactly between 11 and 12.

---

# 36. Planning Poker

**Planning Poker** is a collaborative estimation technique.

Process:

1. Product Manager explains the story.
2. Team discusses it.
3. Each person privately selects an estimate.
4. Everyone reveals their estimate.
5. Large differences are discussed.
6. Team estimates again.

The goal isn't mathematical precision.

The discussion often reveals:

> Hidden assumptions and misunderstandings.

---

# 37. Why Estimates Differ

Suppose:

Developer A:

> 3 points

Developer B:

> 8 points

That difference is useful.

Maybe Developer B knows:

> The external API has a major limitation.

Now the team has discovered a risk.

Therefore:

> **Estimation discussions can be a discovery mechanism.**

---

# 38. Velocity

**Velocity** is the amount of estimated work a team completes during a Sprint.

For example:

```text
Sprint 1 → 20 points
Sprint 2 → 23 points
Sprint 3 → 21 points
Sprint 4 → 22 points
```

Average:

> Approximately 21–22 points.

This can help with forecasting.

---

# 39. Velocity Is Not a Productivity Score

A common mistake is:

> "Team A completed 30 points. Team B completed 20. Team A is better."

Not necessarily.

Story Points are team-specific.

A 5-point story for one team may be a 3-point story for another.

Velocity should primarily help a team:

> **Forecast its own capacity.**

---

# 40. Estimation Anti-Pattern

Never tell the team:

> "You completed 20 points last Sprint. You must complete 25 this Sprint."

This encourages:

* Inflating estimates
* Gaming the metric
* Reducing quality
* Avoiding difficult work
* Focusing on points rather than value

The goal is not:

> Maximize Story Points.

The goal is:

> Maximize product value sustainably.

---

# 41. Burndown Chart

A **Burndown Chart** shows remaining work over time.

Example:

```text
Remaining Work
|
|\
| \
|  \
|   \
|    \
|     \____
|
+----------------
     Time
```

It can help visualize whether a Sprint is progressing as expected.

But it doesn't necessarily tell you:

> Whether the product is creating value.

---

# 42. Burnup Chart

A **Burnup Chart** shows completed work against total scope.

This can be useful when scope changes.

Example:

```text
Work
|
|           Total Scope
|        /------------
|      /
|    /
|  / Completed
| /
+----------------
       Time
```

A burnup chart can make scope changes more visible than a burndown chart.

# 44. Roadmap vs. Sprint Plan

### Roadmap

Focuses on:

> Where are we going?

Time horizon:

> Months / quarters.

### Sprint Plan

Focuses on:

> What will we accomplish now?

Time horizon:

> Usually 1–4 weeks.

A roadmap should not become a giant list of Sprint tickets.

---

# 45. Agile Roadmaps

An Agile roadmap should communicate:

* Direction
* Outcomes
* Priorities
* Approximate timing
* Strategic themes

It shouldn't necessarily promise:

> Every feature will ship on an exact date.

A useful roadmap can be outcome-oriented.

Instead of:

> Q3: Build AI chatbot.

Use:

> Q3: Reduce customer support dependency through self-service.

The solution can evolve.

---

# 46. Agile and Prioritization

Agile doesn't tell you exactly how to prioritize the backlog.

You still need product prioritization frameworks.

Examples:

* RICE
* WSJF
* Value vs. Effort
* MoSCoW
* Opportunity Scoring
* Cost of Delay

The important principle is:

> Agile helps you execute iteratively; it does not automatically tell you what is most valuable.

---

# 47. Agile Does Not Solve Bad Product Strategy

A team can be extremely Agile and still build the wrong thing.

For example:

```text
Excellent Scrum
      ↓
Fast development
      ↓
Frequent releases
      ↓
Wrong product
```

Agile improves the development process.

It doesn't replace:

* Product strategy
* Customer discovery
* Prioritization
* Product vision
* Business understanding

---

# 48. Agile Does Not Mean "Ship Everything Quickly"

The goal isn't:

> Ship more features.

The goal is:

> **Deliver valuable outcomes quickly and learn continuously.**

Sometimes the right Agile decision is:

> Don't build it.

---

# 49. Agile and Product Discovery

Discovery and delivery work together.

```text
DISCOVERY
Understand the problem
        ↓
Identify opportunity
        ↓
Validate solution
        ↓
        DELIVERY
        ↓
Build
        ↓

        ↓

        ↓
```

Discovery helps determine:

> What should we build?

Delivery helps determine:

> How do we build and release it effectively?

# 50. Agile and Product Management

A Product Manager in an Agile team often spends time on:

### Before development

* Understand customer problems
* Analyze data
* Define opportunities
* Prioritize
* Write requirements
* Collaborate with design and engineering

### During development

* Clarify requirements
* Make trade-offs
* Remove ambiguity
* Manage scope
* Monitor progress
* Make product decisions

### After release

* Analyze results
* Gather feedback
* Measure outcomes
* Decide what to do next

# 51. The PM Should Not Become a Ticket Manager

A common failure mode is:

```text
Engineer asks question
      ↓
PM answers
      ↓
Engineer builds
      ↓
PM writes another ticket
      ↓
Engineer builds
```

The PM becomes an administrative layer.

A strong PM instead focuses on:

* Customer value
* Product outcomes
* Prioritization
* Strategy
* Learning
* Decision-making

---

# 52. Stakeholder Requests in Agile

Suppose a stakeholder says:

> "Add a new export button."

Don't immediately create a ticket.

Ask:

* What problem does this solve?
* Who needs it?
* How often?
* What are they doing today?
* What's the business impact?
* Is there a simpler solution?

The request is an input.

It isn't automatically the requirement.

---

# 53. Scope Changes During a Sprint

Suppose halfway through a Sprint, the CEO asks:

> "Can we add this feature immediately?"

The team should evaluate:

* Is it urgent?
* Does it affect the Sprint Goal?
* What work would need to be removed?
* Is the request actually more valuable than current work?

Agile doesn't mean:

> "Everything can change at any time."

It means:

> Changes should be handled deliberately based on value and context.

---

# 54. Sprint Goal vs. Scope

The Sprint Goal provides focus.

The exact tasks can sometimes change if the team discovers a better way to achieve the goal.

For example:

### Sprint Goal

> Improve document upload completion.

Initially planned:

* Feature A
* Feature B
* Feature C

During development, the team discovers:

> Feature B isn't necessary.

The team can adapt while preserving the Sprint Goal.

---

# 55. Product Increment

The Scrum Team should produce a usable **Increment** during the Sprint.

The increment should meet the Definition of Done.

This means:

> Work should be in a usable state.

It doesn't necessarily mean:

> It must immediately be released to all customers.

Release timing depends on the product and organization.

---

# 56. Continuous Delivery vs. Scrum

A team can use Scrum and release:

* Every Sprint
* Multiple times per Sprint
* Continuously

Scrum does not require:

> One release per Sprint.

The important distinction is:

> Sprint cadence and release cadence don't have to be identical.

Useful Agile metrics can include:

### Delivery

* Lead time
* Cycle time
* Deployment frequency
* Throughput

### Reliability

* Change failure rate
* Defect rate
* Mean time to recovery

* Adoption

# 58. Output vs. Outcome in Agile

A team can have excellent delivery metrics:

* 95% Sprint completion
* High velocity
* Fast cycle time

But product outcomes might be poor.

Example:

> Feature adoption = 2%.

Therefore:

> **Delivery efficiency and product effectiveness are different things.**

A strong PM monitors both.

# 59. Common Agile Mistakes

## Mistake 1: Treating Scrum as a Set of Meetings

Scrum is not:

> Planning + Daily + Review + Retro.

The purpose is empirical product development.

---

## Mistake 2: Optimizing Velocity

Velocity is a forecasting tool, not a business objective.

---

## Mistake 3: Treating the Backlog as a Graveyard

Don't keep thousands of outdated tickets.

Regularly remove or archive irrelevant items.

---

## Mistake 4: No Product Goal

Without a product goal, the team may optimize individual features instead of product outcomes.

---

## Mistake 5: No Customer Feedback

A team can technically follow Scrum while rarely talking to customers.

That's Agile theater.

---

## Mistake 6: Huge Stories

If work cannot reasonably fit into a Sprint, break it down.

---

## Mistake 7: Carrying Work Forward Every Sprint

Constant carryover may indicate:

* Poor estimation
* Large stories
* Dependencies
* Interruptions
* Unclear requirements
* Unrealistic commitments

Investigate the underlying cause.

---

# 60. Agile Maturity

You can think of Agile maturity in stages.

### Level 1 — Process

> We run Sprints.

### Level 2 — Delivery

> We reliably deliver increments.

### Level 3 — Feedback

> We use customer and product data.

### Level 4 — Outcomes

> We optimize for measurable product outcomes.

### Level 5 — Continuous Learning

> Strategy, discovery, delivery, and measurement continuously influence each other.

The goal isn't simply:

> "Become better at Scrum."

The goal is:

> **Become better at creating customer and business value.**

# 65. PM Interview Questions

## Question 1: What is Agile?

Agile is an approach to product development based on iterative delivery, customer feedback, collaboration, continuous learning, and adapting to change.

---

## Question 2: What's the difference between Agile and Scrum?

Agile is a broader set of principles and values. Scrum is a specific framework for applying iterative and empirical product development.

---

## Question 3: What's the difference between Product Owner and Product Manager?

The Product Owner is a formal Scrum accountability focused heavily on maximizing product value through backlog management and ordering. A Product Manager typically has broader responsibilities around product strategy, customers, market, business outcomes, and product direction. In some organizations one person performs both roles.

---

## Question 4: What is a Sprint Goal?

A Sprint Goal is the objective that gives the Sprint a coherent purpose and helps the team focus its work on a meaningful outcome.

---

## Question 5: What is the difference between Sprint Review and Retrospective?

The Sprint Review focuses on the product and stakeholder feedback. The Retrospective focuses on how the team worked and how the process can improve.

---

## Question 6: What are Story Points?

Story Points are a relative measure used by a team to estimate the effort, complexity, and uncertainty of work. They are not directly equivalent to hours or days.

---

## Question 7: Should velocity be used to compare teams?

No. Velocity is team-specific and should primarily be used to help a team forecast its own future capacity.

---

## Question 8: What would you do if stakeholders continuously add requests during a Sprint?

I would first understand the urgency and value of the request. If it is important enough to interrupt the Sprint, I would work with the team and stakeholders to understand what existing work should be removed or whether the request should be prioritized for a future Sprint. The goal is to protect focus while still responding appropriately to genuinely urgent changes.

# 66. Key Takeaways

1. **Agile is an approach to building products through iterative learning and adaptation.**
2. Agile does not mean "no planning."
3. Scrum is one framework for implementing Agile product development.
4. Scrum defines three accountabilities: Product Owner, Scrum Master, and Developers.
5. Product Managers may work alongside Product Owners or perform similar responsibilities depending on the organization.
6. The Product Backlog contains potential product work.
7. The Sprint Backlog contains work selected for the current Sprint.
8. A Sprint should have a meaningful Sprint Goal.
9. Sprint Planning determines the Sprint Goal and selected work.
10. Daily Scrum helps Developers inspect progress and adapt their plan.
11. Sprint Review focuses on the product and stakeholder feedback.
12. Sprint Retrospective focuses on improving the team's way of working.
13. Backlog refinement improves upcoming work.
14. Definition of Ready can help teams prepare work, but it is not a mandatory Scrum artifact.
15. Definition of Done establishes a shared standard for completed work.
16. User stories describe user needs but often require additional detail.
17. Vertical slicing helps teams deliver small, end-to-end capabilities.
18. Story Points are relative estimates, not units of time.
19. Velocity should be used for forecasting, not as a productivity target.
20. Agile does not automatically solve prioritization or product strategy.
21. Agile delivery should remain connected to customer problems and product outcomes.
22. The goal is not to maximize the number of tickets completed.
23. The goal is to **maximize valuable outcomes while continuously learning and adapting**.

# 67. The PM Mental Model

A strong Agile Product Manager should constantly connect:

```text
CUSTOMER PROBLEM
       ↓
PRODUCT OUTCOME
       ↓
PRIORITY
       ↓
SPRINT GOAL
       ↓
TEAM WORK
       ↓
PRODUCT INCREMENT
       ↓
CUSTOMER FEEDBACK
       ↓
DATA
       ↓
LEARNING
       ↺
```

If the team is busy but you cannot explain:

> "What customer or business outcome is this work supposed to improve?"

then something is probably wrong.

---

# 68. Final Principle

Agile is not about:

> **Doing more work faster.**

It is about:

> **Learning and delivering value faster.**

A team that builds 20 features per quarter but creates little customer value is not necessarily Agile in the way that matters.

A team that continuously:

* Understands customer problems
* Prioritizes valuable opportunities
* Builds small increments
* Gets feedback
* Measures outcomes
* Learns
* Adapts

is much closer to the real purpose of Agile product management.
