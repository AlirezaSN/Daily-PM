# Day 96: Product Innovation & Emerging Technologies

# Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Distinguish incremental, adjacent, disruptive, and radical innovation.
- Evaluate emerging technologies based on customer value rather than novelty.
- Understand technology readiness, adoption curves, and market timing.
- Identify product opportunities created by technological change.
- Use structured experiments to validate uncertain technology-driven ideas.
- Balance short-term product delivery with long-term innovation.
- Build an innovation portfolio and allocate resources across different time horizons.
- Evaluate the feasibility, desirability, viability, and risks of emerging technologies.
- Understand how AI, blockchain, automation, and new infrastructure can change product possibilities.
- Create a practical innovation strategy for a fintech or digital product.

# 1. What Is Product Innovation?

Product innovation is the process of creating or significantly improving products, services, experiences, or business models to deliver meaningful value.

Innovation does not necessarily require inventing new technology.

A company can innovate by:
- Solving an existing problem in a fundamentally better way.
- Making a previously expensive service affordable.
- Removing unnecessary steps from a customer journey.
- Combining existing technologies into a new experience.
- Serving a customer segment that existing products overlook.
- Changing how customers discover, purchase, or use a service.
- Creating a new business model around an existing capability.

For example, online insurance purchasing is not a new technology. However, a product that helps customers understand complicated coverage options, compare exclusions, and confidently select a suitable policy may represent meaningful product innovation.

The innovation lies in making a difficult decision easier, not necessarily in inventing an algorithm.

## 1.1 Innovation versus invention

**Invention** is the creation of something new.

**Innovation** is the successful application of an idea to create value.

An invention can be technically impressive without solving a meaningful problem. An innovation connects an idea to an outcome that customers or the business value.

Consider a new AI model that can generate highly detailed financial explanations.

The invention is the model capability.

Possible innovations include:
- Helping customers understand transaction fees.
- Explaining portfolio risk in accessible language.
- Reducing the time support agents spend resolving routine questions.
- Helping compliance teams review documents more efficiently.

The same technology can produce many potential innovations, but not every application will be valuable.

## 1.2 The Product Manager's role

Product Managers do not need to predict every important technology trend. They need to understand how technological changes affect customer problems and business opportunities.

Their responsibilities include:

1. Identifying important problems and unmet needs.
2. Understanding which new capabilities may help solve them.
3. Evaluating desirability, feasibility, and viability.
4. Testing uncertain assumptions.
5. Prioritizing investments.
6. Coordinating delivery across functions.
7. Measuring actual outcomes.
8. Deciding whether to scale, modify, pause, or stop an initiative.

The central principle is:

**Start with the opportunity, evaluate the technology, and validate the outcome. Do not start with a technology and assume a valuable product will follow.**

# 2. Types of Innovation

Different types of innovation involve different levels of uncertainty, investment, and organizational change.

## 2.1 Incremental innovation

Incremental innovation improves an existing product without fundamentally changing its core business model.

Examples:
- Improving onboarding completion.
- Reducing payment failures.
- Making a portfolio dashboard easier to understand.
- Improving search filters in a merchant platform.
- Reducing the number of steps required to purchase insurance.

Incremental innovation is often easier to evaluate because the product, users, and business model are already understood.

It is especially useful when the team has clear evidence about customer friction and measurable opportunities for improvement.

However, excessive focus on incremental improvements can cause a company to miss major market shifts.

## 2.2 Adjacent innovation

Adjacent innovation applies existing capabilities to a new customer segment, use case, channel, or market.

Examples:
- A payments company launching invoicing tools for small businesses.
- A crypto exchange adding recurring investment functionality.
- An insurance comparison service expanding from individual customers to small employers.
- A merchant platform extending from Instagram sellers to other small online businesses.

Adjacent innovation can be attractive because the company can reuse existing assets while learning about a new opportunity.

The key question is whether the existing capabilities actually transfer to the new context.

A successful consumer product does not automatically translate into a successful enterprise product. Enterprise customers may require permissions, audit trails, procurement processes, and service-level commitments.

## 2.3 Disruptive innovation

Disruptive innovation is commonly associated with offerings that begin by serving overlooked or less-demanding customers, often through a simpler, more accessible, or more affordable proposition, and may later challenge established providers.

It is not simply a synonym for a major technological breakthrough.

For example, a financial service might initially target customers who are poorly served by traditional providers because existing solutions are too expensive or complicated.

The product may begin with a narrower feature set and lower cost structure, then improve over time.

A new technology does not automatically make a product disruptive. Its market position, customer segment, business model, and competitive trajectory matter.

## 2.4 Radical innovation

Radical innovation introduces a substantially new capability, experience, or business model.

Examples might include:
- A new computing capability that enables previously impractical workflows.
- A financial service that fundamentally changes how a particular transaction is executed.
- A new interaction model that replaces much of a traditional software interface.

Radical innovation often involves high uncertainty because customer behavior, technical feasibility, and business economics may all be unclear.

It can produce significant opportunities, but teams should avoid assuming that the scale of the technological change determines the scale of the customer value.

## 2.5 A practical comparison

| Innovation type | Main change | Typical uncertainty | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incremental | Existing product improves | Lower | Reduce payment failure rate |
| Adjacent | Existing capabilities enter a new context | Medium | Offer payments to a new merchant segment |
| Disruptive | A new offering challenges established market patterns | High and context-dependent | Serve overlooked customers with a simpler model |
| Radical | A substantially new capability or model | Often high | Enable a previously impractical financial workflow |

These categories are not rigid. An initiative may combine multiple types.

# 3. The Innovation Opportunity: Start with Customer Problems

Technology-led innovation often fails because teams become fascinated by a capability before identifying a valuable use case.

A more reliable process begins with customer problems and works toward a suitable technological solution.

## 3.1 Find important unmet needs

Look for:
- Tasks customers avoid.
- Frequent workarounds.
- Expensive manual processes.
- Repeated customer complaints.
- Decisions customers find confusing.
- Problems that existing products solve poorly.
- Tasks that become possible only when constraints change.

For example, a customer may not need an AI-powered insurance assistant. They need to understand which policy covers a particular treatment and what exclusions apply.

AI may be one way to improve that experience, but the customer outcome should define the problem.

## 3.2 Look for changing constraints

Emerging technologies can make previously unattractive solutions feasible.

A task may have been too expensive because it required expert labor. Better automation might reduce its cost.

A process may have been too slow because data was difficult to access. New integrations might reduce the delay.

A product may have been difficult to personalize because it required extensive manual configuration. New recommendation systems might make personalization practical.

Ask:

- What was previously impossible or prohibitively expensive?
- Which constraint has changed?
- Does the change create meaningful customer value?
- Can the new solution be delivered reliably?
- Is the opportunity large enough to justify investment?

## 3.3 Separate the problem from the proposed solution

Suppose leadership says:

"We need to launch an AI financial advisor."

This is a solution proposal, not yet a validated product opportunity.

A better discovery process asks:
- Which customers need help?
- What decision are they struggling with?
- How do they solve the problem today?
- What errors or costs result?
- Why are current solutions inadequate?
- Which parts of the workflow require judgment?
- What level of accuracy and reliability is necessary?

The eventual solution might be an AI assistant, a rule-based calculator, a better dashboard, a human-assisted service, or a combination of these.

The goal is to find the best solution to the problem, not to justify the original proposal.

# 4. Technology Readiness and Product Readiness

A technology can work in a demonstration but fail in a real product.

Product Managers must distinguish technical capability from production readiness.

## 4.1 Technology readiness

Technology readiness concerns whether a capability works reliably enough for the intended application.

Questions include:
- Does it work outside a controlled environment?
- How often does it fail?
- What inputs does it require?
- How does performance change under load?
- Can failures be detected?
- Can the system recover?
- What does it cost to operate?
- Are there security or privacy limitations?

A successful prototype is evidence of possibility, not proof of production readiness.

## 4.2 Product readiness

Product readiness includes more than technical performance.

A product may also need:
- A clear customer problem.
- A usable experience.
- Reliable onboarding.
- Support and escalation.
- Monitoring and incident response.
- Pricing and packaging.
- Legal and regulatory review.
- Appropriate customer communication.
- Measurable business value.

For example, an AI model might answer many financial questions correctly in testing. A production product still needs to handle uncertainty, explain limitations, protect account data, prevent unauthorized actions, and respond appropriately when it cannot provide a reliable answer.

## 4.3 The prototype-to-production gap

Consider a document-processing AI feature.

A prototype works well on ten clean sample documents.

Production introduces:
- Poor-quality scans.
- Unusual document layouts.
- Missing information.
- Multiple languages.
- Duplicate uploads.
- Unexpected file types.
- Large traffic spikes.
- Sensitive personal information.
- Incorrect extractions that affect customer decisions.

The prototype demonstrates technical potential. Production readiness requires evidence across the actual operating conditions.

## 4.4 A practical readiness checklist

Before scaling a technology-driven product, evaluate five dimensions:

1. **Capability:** Can it perform the required task?
2. **Reliability:** Does it work consistently under realistic conditions?
3. **Usability:** Can customers use it successfully?
4. **Economics:** Can the business deliver the outcome at an acceptable cost?
5. **Governance:** Can risks, permissions, and failures be managed?

A major weakness in any one dimension may prevent the product from being ready to scale.

# 5. Understanding Technology Adoption

Technological capability and market adoption do not evolve at the same speed.

A technology may be powerful long before customers understand it, trust it, or have a reason to adopt it.

## 5.1 The technology adoption lifecycle

A common model describes five broad customer groups.

**Innovators**

Experiment with new technology and tolerate significant uncertainty.

**Early adopters**

Recognize potential strategic benefits and are willing to try new approaches.

**Early majority**

Prefer proven solutions with clear benefits and manageable risks.

**Late majority**

Adopt when solutions become established, accessible, and relatively low-risk.

**Laggards**

Adopt slowly, often because of constraints, preferences, or limited alternatives.

These are useful conceptual categories, not precise predictions about individual customers.

## 5.2 Product implications

A product designed for early adopters may emphasize:
- Flexibility.
- Advanced capabilities.
- Customization.
- Access to new functionality.

A product targeting the broader market may need:
- Clear value propositions.
- Simple onboarding.
- Reliable performance.
- Strong support.
- Integration with existing workflows.
- Evidence of safety and effectiveness.

A common mistake is assuming that enthusiastic feedback from technically sophisticated users proves broad market demand.

## 5.3 The gap between early adopters and the mainstream

Early adopters may accept incomplete workflows because they are excited about the underlying technology.

Mainstream customers are more likely to ask:
- Does this solve a real problem?
- Can I trust it?
- Will it fit my current workflow?
- What happens when it fails?
- Is it worth paying for?
- Who will support it?

To move beyond early adopters, the product often needs to become less dependent on the customer understanding the technology.

The experience should make the benefit obvious.

## 5.4 Timing the market

Launching too early may require educating customers, creating missing infrastructure, and absorbing high operating costs.

Launching too late may mean entering a crowded market after competitors have established strong distribution and customer relationships.

Assess market timing through signals such as:
- Customer demand.
- Changes in operating costs.
- Availability of supporting infrastructure.
- Regulatory clarity.
- Competitor adoption.
- Customer willingness to pay.
- Availability of complementary services.

Avoid treating a technology hype cycle as a reliable launch calendar. Customer behavior and unit economics provide stronger evidence.

# 6. Evaluate Innovation with Desirability, Feasibility, and Viability

A useful innovation framework evaluates three dimensions.

## 6.1 Desirability

Do customers want or need the solution?

Investigate:
- Problem frequency.
- Problem severity.
- Current alternatives.
- Customer willingness to change behavior.
- Willingness to pay.
- Adoption barriers.

Customer enthusiasm for a demo is weaker evidence than repeated use of a solution that addresses a real need.

## 6.2 Feasibility

Can the organization build and operate the solution?

Investigate:
- Technology maturity.
- Data availability.
- Integration requirements.
- Engineering complexity.
- Performance constraints.
- Security.
- Reliability.
- Organizational capabilities.

A solution may be desirable but infeasible within the available budget or timeframe.

## 6.3 Viability

Can the business sustain the solution?

Investigate:
- Revenue potential.
- Customer acquisition costs.
- Operating costs.
- Contribution margin.
- Support requirements.
- Competitive pressure.
- Regulatory obligations.
- Strategic fit.

A technically successful product can still be commercially unviable.

## 6.4 Adding responsibility and risk

For emerging technologies, especially AI and financial infrastructure, the three dimensions are not sufficient by themselves.

Also assess:
- Safety.
- Privacy.
- Fairness.
- Security.
- Legal requirements.
- Operational resilience.
- Reputational impact.

A product should not proceed simply because customers want it and the technology works if the organization cannot manage its risks responsibly.

# 7. Build an Evidence-Based Innovation Process

Innovation should be treated as a sequence of decisions that progressively reduce uncertainty.

A practical process has seven stages.

## Stage 1: Identify an opportunity

Define:
- The target customer.
- The problem.
- The current workaround.
- The expected value.
- Why the opportunity matters now.

Avoid defining the opportunity only as a technology feature.

## Stage 2: Map critical assumptions

List what must be true for the idea to succeed.

Examples:
- Customers experience the problem frequently.
- They will change their behavior.
- The technology can meet the required accuracy.
- The solution can be delivered at acceptable cost.
- The business can operate within relevant regulations.

Rank assumptions by uncertainty and impact.

## Stage 3: Select the cheapest meaningful test

Choose an experiment that tests the riskiest assumption with minimal investment.

Examples:
- Customer interviews.
- A clickable prototype.
- A manual concierge service.
- A small technical benchmark.
- A landing page.
- A limited pilot.
- A controlled experiment.

The right test depends on the uncertainty being investigated.

## Stage 4: Define success and failure criteria

Before running the experiment, determine:
- What will be measured?
- What result supports the hypothesis?
- What result weakens it?
- What sample or evidence is needed?
- What decision follows each outcome?

This reduces the temptation to reinterpret disappointing results as success.

## Stage 5: Test in realistic conditions

A test should resemble the environment where the product will be used.

For example, evaluate a document-processing feature using realistic documents, including difficult and incomplete cases, rather than only clean examples selected by the development team.

## Stage 6: Decide whether to scale

Possible decisions include:
- Scale the solution.
- Continue experimentation.
- Narrow the target segment.
- Modify the solution.
- Change the underlying technology.
- Pause or stop the initiative.

Stopping an idea after useful evidence is gathered is not necessarily a failure. It can prevent much larger waste.

## Stage 7: Monitor after launch

Innovation does not end at launch.

Track:
- Actual customer adoption.
- Repeat usage.
- Outcome quality.
- Operating costs.
- Support burden.
- Risk events.
- Retention.
- Contribution margin.

The goal is to determine whether the expected value survives contact with real usage.

# 8. Experimentation for Emerging Technologies

Traditional product experimentation often measures customer behavior. Technology-driven innovation may also require technical experiments.

Both are necessary.

## 8.1 Customer experiments

These test whether the problem and proposed experience matter to customers.

Examples:
- Do users understand the value proposition?
- Will they complete onboarding?
- Will they use the feature repeatedly?
- Will they pay for the outcome?

## 8.2 Technical experiments

These test whether the technology can deliver the required result.

Examples:
- Can the model reach the required accuracy?
- Can the system process requests within the latency target?
- Can the integration handle expected transaction volume?
- Can the solution operate within a cost ceiling?

## 8.3 Operational experiments

These test whether the organization can operate the product safely and reliably.

Examples:
- Can support resolve errors effectively?
- Can failures be detected and contained?
- Can the system recover from an outage?
- Can the company handle disputes and remediation?
- Can the process meet compliance obligations?

## 8.4 Example: AI-powered financial explanations

Suppose a fintech team wants to explain account transactions using AI.

A staged experiment might be:

**Experiment 1 — Customer value**

Show customers a prototype and determine whether the explanations help them understand transactions more quickly.

**Experiment 2 — Technical quality**

Evaluate whether explanations accurately reflect authoritative transaction records.

**Experiment 3 — Safety**

Test whether the system invents transaction details, exposes unauthorized data, or presents uncertain information as fact.

**Experiment 4 — Operational viability**

Measure inference costs, response time, escalation volume, and support requirements.

**Experiment 5 — Controlled launch**

Release the feature to a limited group and compare comprehension, support contacts, and error rates with an appropriate baseline.

Only after the evidence supports expansion should the team consider a broad rollout.

# 9. Build vs. Buy vs. Partner for Emerging Technology

A new technology opportunity does not automatically justify building proprietary infrastructure.

Product Managers should evaluate three broad options.

## 9.1 Build

Develop the capability internally.

Potential advantages:
- Greater control.
- Customization.
- Internal expertise.
- Potential differentiation.
- Less dependence on a particular supplier.

Potential disadvantages:
- Higher initial cost.
- Longer time to market.
- Maintenance burden.
- Need for specialized skills.
- Risk of investing in a capability that becomes commoditized.

Building is more attractive when the capability is strategically important, differentiated, and difficult to obtain elsewhere.

## 9.2 Buy

Use an existing vendor, platform, or commercial solution.

Potential advantages:
- Faster delivery.
- Access to established capabilities.
- Lower initial investment.
- Reduced maintenance burden.

Potential disadvantages:
- Vendor dependence.
- Limited customization.
- Recurring fees.
- Data and security concerns.
- Limited control over product changes.

Buying is attractive when the capability is relatively standard and the vendor can meet the required quality, security, and cost needs.

## 9.3 Partner

Collaborate with another organization to deliver the experience.

Potential advantages:
- Shared investment.
- Access to distribution.
- Complementary expertise.
- Faster entry into a new market.

Potential disadvantages:
- Shared control.
- Coordination costs.
- Commercial dependencies.
- Conflicting incentives.
- Potential partner competition.

Partnerships can be especially valuable when market access, regulation, or existing infrastructure is difficult to reproduce internally.

## 9.4 A practical decision framework

Evaluate each option against:
- Strategic differentiation.
- Time to market.
- Total cost of ownership.
- Required control.
- Security and compliance.
- Scalability.
- Internal expertise.
- Vendor or partner dependency.
- Reversibility.

The decision should reflect the long-term product strategy, not just the initial implementation cost.

# 10. Emerging Technology Areas and Their Product Implications

The following examples illustrate how Product Managers can evaluate different technologies without assuming that any one of them must be adopted.

## 10.1 Artificial intelligence and machine learning

Potential applications:
- Personalization.
- Fraud detection.
- Forecasting.
- Document processing.
- Search and recommendations.
- Customer-support assistance.
- Decision support.

Key questions:
- Does the technology improve the outcome compared with simpler alternatives?
- What happens when predictions are wrong?
- Can the result be evaluated?
- Are the required data and permissions available?
- Is the operating cost acceptable?

AI is most valuable when it improves a meaningful workflow rather than simply adding a conversational interface.

## 10.2 Blockchain and distributed ledgers

Potential applications may include:
- Shared transaction records.
- Digital asset settlement.
- Tokenization.
- Programmable transactions.
- Coordination among parties that do not fully trust one another.

Key questions:
- Is a shared ledger necessary?
- Who needs to trust whom?
- Would a conventional database be simpler?
- What are the governance and security requirements?
- Are there regulatory or interoperability constraints?
- Does the approach improve the customer outcome?

Using blockchain is not inherently an innovation advantage. The technology must solve a problem that justifies its complexity.

## 10.3 Automation and robotics

Potential applications:
- Repetitive operational tasks.
- Document handling.
- Quality checks.
- Workflow routing.
- Back-office processing.

Key questions:
- How frequent and costly is the manual task?
- How variable are the inputs?
- What error rate is acceptable?
- What exceptions require human judgment?
- Does automation reduce total cost after monitoring and maintenance?

Automating a poorly designed process can make mistakes happen faster. Process quality should be addressed alongside automation.

## 10.4 New payment and financial infrastructure

Potential applications:
- Faster payment processing.
- Improved reconciliation.
- Embedded financial services.
- Better transaction monitoring.
- More reliable integrations.

Key questions:
- Does the new infrastructure improve speed, cost, reliability, or access?
- What dependencies does it introduce?
- How will failures be handled?
- What regulatory obligations apply?
- Can customers understand the change?

Financial infrastructure innovations must be evaluated across the entire transaction lifecycle, not only the happy path.

# 11. Innovation Portfolios and Investment Horizons

A company should not allocate every innovation investment to either immediate improvements or speculative long-term projects.

A portfolio approach helps balance risk and opportunity.

## 11.1 Three horizons

A commonly used planning model separates initiatives into three horizons.

**Horizon 1: Improve the core**

Focus on existing products, customer journeys, reliability, and economics.

Examples:
- Improve payment success rates.
- Reduce insurance purchase abandonment.
- Improve merchant onboarding.

**Horizon 2: Expand into adjacent opportunities**

Reuse current capabilities in new segments, products, or channels.

Examples:
- Offer new financial workflows to existing customers.
- Extend a merchant product into adjacent channels.
- Introduce a new insurance distribution partnership.

**Horizon 3: Explore future opportunities**

Investigate potentially transformative technologies or business models.

Examples:
- Explore a new financial infrastructure model.
- Test an emerging AI-based workflow.
- Evaluate a new service enabled by a developing technology.

These horizons are planning tools, not rigid timelines. A Horizon 3 idea may prove valuable quickly, while a Horizon 1 initiative may require substantial work.

## 11.2 Avoid the innovation theater trap

Innovation theater occurs when a company appears innovative through demonstrations, events, prototypes, or announcements without producing meaningful customer or business outcomes.

Warning signs include:
- Many prototypes with no clear decision.
- No defined target customer.
- No success criteria.
- No path to production.
- No owner after the experiment.
- No budget for operations.
- No evidence of customer value.

An innovation program should be measured by learning quality and validated outcomes, not the number of ideas generated.

## 11.3 Portfolio allocation

There is no universal ideal percentage for each horizon.

The right allocation depends on:
- Business maturity.
- Financial position.
- Competitive pressure.
- Regulatory constraints.
- Technology change.
- Strategic ambition.
- Existing product performance.

A mature company under intense competitive pressure may need significant core investment. A company facing a major market transition may need to protect more resources for experimentation.

The important thing is to make the trade-offs explicit.

# 12. Prioritizing Innovation Opportunities

Innovation initiatives often have uncertain outcomes, so conventional prioritization methods may require adjustment.

## 12.1 Evaluate the opportunity

Consider:
- Customer problem severity.
- Number of affected customers.
- Potential business impact.
- Strategic fit.
- Technical feasibility.
- Time to learn.
- Cost of experimentation.
- Risk.
- Potential for future capabilities.

## 12.2 Prioritize learning as well as delivery

A useful early experiment may not generate immediate revenue, but it can eliminate a major uncertainty.

For example, a small technical test may establish whether a model can accurately extract required fields from real documents.

If the test is inexpensive and the result determines whether a large investment is justified, its value may be substantial.

## 12.3 Consider the cost of waiting

Waiting can be costly if:
- A competitor is establishing a distribution advantage.
- A regulatory deadline is approaching.
- A customer need is growing rapidly.
- An important technical capability is becoming available.

But moving early also has costs.

Estimate both:
- The cost of investing too early.
- The cost of delaying a promising opportunity.

The objective is not to be first at all costs. It is to make a well-informed decision about when to commit.

# 13. Risk Management for Emerging Technology

Emerging technology introduces uncertainty beyond normal product delivery.

## 13.1 Technical risk

The technology may fail to meet quality, latency, scalability, or reliability requirements.

**Response:** Conduct technical evaluations under realistic conditions and define clear thresholds.

## 13.2 Market risk

Customers may not need the product or may not change their behavior.

**Response:** Validate demand before making large investments.

## 13.3 Economic risk

The product may cost more to operate than the value it creates.

**Response:** Estimate unit economics early and update them using real usage data.

## 13.4 Regulatory and legal risk

The product may involve regulated decisions, restricted data, or legal obligations that limit its design.

**Response:** Involve legal, compliance, and risk stakeholders early enough to influence the solution.

## 13.5 Security and privacy risk

New systems may expose data, create new attack surfaces, or introduce unsafe access patterns.

**Response:** Apply security reviews, permission boundaries, data minimization, and appropriate monitoring.

## 13.6 Reputational risk

A visible failure may damage customer trust.

**Response:** Set clear expectations, design escalation paths, and define how customers will be informed and supported when something goes wrong.

## 13.7 Organizational risk

The company may lack the expertise or operational processes needed to sustain the solution.

**Response:** Identify capability gaps and establish ownership, training, and support plans.

# 14. Communicating Innovation to Executives

Executive stakeholders often need to understand why an uncertain investment deserves resources.

A strong innovation proposal should not rely on excitement about the technology.

Use a concise structure.

## 14.1 The opportunity

Describe the customer problem, affected segment, and why the problem matters.

## 14.2 The change

Explain what has changed technologically, economically, or in customer behavior that makes a new approach possible.

## 14.3 The hypothesis

State what you believe will improve and why.

For example:

"We believe automated document extraction can reduce the time customers spend entering insurance application information while maintaining acceptable accuracy."

## 14.4 The evidence

Separate:
- Observed customer behavior.
- Technical test results.
- Financial estimates.
- Assumptions.
- Unknowns.

Avoid presenting projections as established facts.

## 14.5 The experiment

Describe the smallest useful test, its cost, duration, and success criteria.

## 14.6 The decision

Explain what you will do if the experiment succeeds, fails, or produces ambiguous results.

## 14.7 The investment request

Specify the resources needed now and what additional investment would require further evidence.

This approach makes innovation decisions more transparent and reduces the risk of committing to a large project before the main uncertainties are understood.

# 15. Practical Exercise: Innovating an Insurance Purchase Journey

## Scenario

You manage an online platform selling individual supplementary health insurance.

Customers frequently struggle to:
- Understand coverage differences.
- Compare exclusions.
- Estimate which plan suits their circumstances.
- Complete application forms.
- Trust the information presented.

Your team is considering three ideas:

**Idea A:** Build a guided comparison experience with clearer explanations.

**Idea B:** Introduce an AI assistant that answers questions about plan coverage.

**Idea C:** Automate document extraction and application form completion.

## Task 1: Identify the underlying problems

For each idea:
- Define the customer problem.
- Identify the target segment.
- Describe the current workaround.
- Explain the expected customer benefit.
- Identify the evidence needed to establish demand.

Do not assume that every proposed feature solves an equally important problem.

## Task 2: Evaluate feasibility

For each idea, consider:
- Technical complexity.
- Data requirements.
- Accuracy requirements.
- Operational dependencies.
- Security and privacy.
- Maintenance needs.

Identify which uncertainties must be resolved before implementation.

## Task 3: Evaluate viability

Estimate the potential impact on:
- Conversion.
- Completion time.
- Customer support volume.
- Acquisition cost.
- Operating cost.
- Customer trust.

State your assumptions and distinguish measurable facts from estimates.

## Task 4: Design experiments

For each idea, define:
- The main hypothesis.
- The riskiest assumption.
- The smallest useful experiment.
- The primary metric.
- A guardrail metric.
- A decision rule.

For the AI assistant, for example, a useful guardrail is the rate of materially incorrect coverage explanations.

## Task 5: Prioritize

Choose one initiative to test first.

Explain your choice based on:
- Customer impact.
- Confidence in the problem.
- Cost and speed of learning.
- Technical feasibility.
- Risk.
- Strategic fit.

## Task 6: Define the scale-up decision

Specify what evidence would justify:
- Expanding the experiment.
- Reworking the solution.
- Moving to production.
- Stopping the initiative.

# 16. Final Challenge: Innovate a Crypto Exchange Product

You are the Senior Product Manager at a cryptocurrency exchange.

Competition is increasing, and leadership wants to differentiate the product through emerging technology.

Three proposals have been raised:

1. An AI assistant that explains transactions and helps customers navigate the platform.
2. An automated monitoring system that identifies suspicious transaction patterns.
3. A new payment infrastructure integration intended to improve transaction speed and reliability.

You have limited engineering capacity and cannot launch all three initiatives simultaneously.

## Part A: Define the strategic opportunity

Identify the customer or business problem behind each proposal.

Explain whether the problem is:
- A customer experience problem.
- A security or risk problem.
- An operational efficiency problem.
- A competitive positioning problem.

Identify the most important target segment and the outcome the exchange should improve.

## Part B: Evaluate the technology

For each proposal, assess:
- Technology maturity.
- Data requirements.
- Integration complexity.
- Reliability needs.
- Operating cost.
- Security and privacy.
- Regulatory considerations.

Identify the assumptions that could invalidate each initiative.

## Part C: Design the experiments

Create a staged validation plan for each proposal.

Your plan should distinguish:
- Customer validation.
- Technical validation.
- Operational validation.
- Economic validation.

Define success and failure criteria before testing.

## Part D: Prioritize the portfolio

Choose which initiative should receive investment first.

Explain:

# Key Takeaways

1. **Innovation is about creating value, not merely introducing technology.** A new capability matters when it improves a meaningful customer or business outcome.

2. **Different innovation types require different approaches.** Incremental, adjacent, disruptive, and radical innovation involve different levels of uncertainty.

3. **Start with the problem.** Technology should be evaluated as a possible solution to a real customer need.

4. **Technical feasibility is not product readiness.** Reliability, usability, economics, operations, and governance all matter.

5. **Adoption requires more than novelty.** Mainstream customers usually need clear value, reliability, and confidence in the solution.

6. **Test the riskiest assumptions first.** Small, well-designed experiments can prevent expensive mistakes.

7. **Evaluate desirability, feasibility, viability, and risk together.** A solution must work for customers, the organization, and the business.

8. **Build, buy, and partner are strategic choices.** Choose based on differentiation, control, cost, speed, and dependency.

9. **Balance the innovation portfolio.** Improve the core, explore adjacent opportunities, and investigate longer-term possibilities.

10. **Measure outcomes, not demonstrations.** Prototype quality and technology usage are not substitutes for customer value.

11. **Emerging technologies introduce new risks.** Security, privacy, reliability, regulation, and reputation must be considered early.

12. **Innovation is a sequence of evidence-based decisions.** Learn, adapt, scale, or stop based on what the evidence shows.
