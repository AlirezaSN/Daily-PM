# Day 76: Influence Without Authority

# Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Explain why influence without formal authority is a core product management skill.
- Distinguish authority, persuasion, influence, negotiation, and collaboration.
- Identify stakeholders' interests, incentives, concerns, and decision-making power.
- Build credibility and trust across engineering, design, data, business, and executive teams.
- Adapt communication to different audiences without compromising your principles.
- Influence decisions using evidence, framing, storytelling, and well-structured trade-offs.
- Handle resistance, disagreements, competing priorities, and difficult stakeholders.
- Negotiate resources and commitments without relying on positional power.
- Recognize manipulative influence tactics and replace them with ethical alternatives.
- Apply influence techniques to realistic product management and fintech scenarios.

# 1. Introduction: Why Product Managers Need Influence

Product managers are responsible for product outcomes, but they often do not directly manage the people whose work determines those outcomes.

An engineering manager may control engineering capacity. A designer may report to a design leader. A data analyst may support several product teams. Compliance may have independent authority over regulatory decisions. Executives may change business priorities.

Yet the product manager is expected to bring these people together to solve customer problems.

This creates a fundamental challenge:

**You are accountable for outcomes that depend on people you do not directly control.**

Consider a fintech product manager who wants to improve the first-time investment experience.

The proposed improvement requires:
- Engineering to prioritize an integration.
- Design to simplify the investment journey.
- Data to establish a reliable conversion baseline.
- Risk to evaluate potential customer harm.
- Marketing to improve customer education.
- Customer Support to report recurring sources of confusion.

The product manager cannot simply assign work to everyone involved. Each stakeholder has other responsibilities, competing priorities, and their own definition of success.

The PM must create enough shared understanding and commitment for the work to move forward.

That is influence without authority.

## Influence is not manipulation

Influence means helping people understand a situation, evaluate alternatives, and make decisions that support a shared objective.

It does not mean forcing agreement, hiding inconvenient information, exploiting personal relationships, or making people feel unable to say no.

Effective influence combines:
- Credibility.
- Understanding of others' interests.
- Clear reasoning.
- Trust.
- Good timing.
- Mutual benefit.
- Respect for decision rights.

The goal is not to win every argument. It is to improve the quality of decisions and make progress possible.

# 2. Authority, Power, and Influence

These concepts are related, but they are not interchangeable.

## 2.1 Authority

Authority is the formal right to make certain decisions or direct particular activities.

For example:
- An engineering manager may allocate engineers within their team.
- A compliance officer may have authority over specific regulatory controls.
- A product director may decide investment priorities within an agreed portfolio.
- A CEO may approve a major strategic investment.

Authority comes from a role, organizational structure, policy, or delegated responsibility.

## 2.2 Power

Power is the ability to affect decisions, behavior, or outcomes.

It can come from formal authority, but it can also come from expertise, relationships, access to information, control over resources, or the ability to coordinate others.

A staff engineer may have considerable influence over a technical decision without being the manager of the engineers implementing it.

A customer support lead may have significant influence over product priorities because they understand recurring customer problems.

## 2.3 Influence

Influence is the process of changing or shaping how people understand a situation, evaluate options, or act.

It can operate through:
- Evidence.
- Persuasion.
- Relationships.
- Negotiation.
- Shared goals.
- Demonstrated competence.
- Credibility and trust.

Influence can exist without formal authority or significant organizational power.

## Example

You need an engineering team to address a recurring payment failure.

You could try to use authority you do not have:

"Please prioritize this. Product needs it done this sprint."

This is unlikely to work if the engineering manager has other commitments and does not understand the problem's significance.

A more effective approach might be:

"We are seeing a recurring failure that affects customers trying to complete payments. It is generating support contacts and may be contributing to abandonment. Could we review the incident data together, estimate the impact, and compare the fix against the other work planned for this sprint?"

The second approach does not guarantee agreement. It creates a basis for an informed decision.

# 3. Why Influence Is Difficult in Product Management

Influence becomes challenging when people have different goals, information, incentives, and constraints.

## 3.1 Competing priorities

An engineer may prioritize reliability. A marketer may prioritize campaign timing. A product manager may prioritize conversion. A compliance specialist may prioritize control effectiveness.

All may be acting rationally from their own perspectives.

The product manager's job is to help the group understand the trade-offs and determine the best decision for the overall product and business.

## 3.2 Different definitions of success

Consider a new onboarding flow.

- Marketing cares about qualified customer acquisition.
- Product cares about successful activation.
- Engineering cares about reliability and sustainable implementation.
- Risk cares about fraud and appropriate verification.
- Customer Support cares about customer confusion and resolution workload.

A change that improves one team's metric can harm another team's outcome.

Influence requires framing the decision around the broader objective and making the trade-offs visible.

## 3.3 Limited resources

People are often willing to help but lack capacity.

A stakeholder's refusal may reflect a genuine constraint rather than resistance to your idea.

The right response is to understand the constraint and explore alternatives, not assume the person is being uncooperative.

## 3.4 Unequal information

A product manager may understand customer behavior better than an engineering stakeholder, while the engineer understands architectural constraints better than the PM.

Influence works best when each person contributes the information they are best positioned to provide.

## 3.5 Organizational incentives

An individual may be rewarded for local outcomes that conflict with the wider product objective.

For example, an engineering team measured mainly on delivery predictability may resist an urgent change that introduces uncertainty—even when the change could prevent a significant customer problem.

The solution may require changing the prioritization discussion or organizational incentives, rather than improving the PM's presentation.

# 4. Understand Stakeholders Before Trying to Influence Them

One of the most common influence mistakes is preparing a persuasive argument before understanding the other person's perspective.

Before approaching a stakeholder, investigate four dimensions:

1. Their goals.
2. Their concerns.
3. Their constraints.
4. Their decision-making role.

## 4.1 Goals

Ask what the stakeholder is trying to accomplish.

An engineering manager might be responsible for:
- Delivering committed work.
- Maintaining system reliability.
- Reducing technical debt.
- Developing engineers.
- Avoiding excessive context switching.

Understanding these goals helps you frame your request in a way that acknowledges the person's responsibilities.

## 4.2 Concerns

Ask what could go wrong from their perspective.

An engineer might worry about:
- Unclear requirements.
- Hidden dependencies.
- Unstable scope.
- Insufficient testing.
- Long-term maintenance costs.

A compliance stakeholder might worry about:
- Inadequate evidence.
- Unclear ownership.
- Regulatory exposure.
- Inconsistent customer treatment.

These concerns should be addressed directly rather than dismissed as resistance.

## 4.3 Constraints

Understand what limits the stakeholder's choices.

Examples include:
- Capacity.
- Technical dependencies.
- Budget.
- Contractual commitments.
- Regulatory deadlines.
- Availability of data.
- Other executive priorities.

A request that ignores real constraints will rarely gain durable support.

## 4.4 Decision-making role

Determine whether the stakeholder is:
- The decision-maker.
- A required approver.
- An expert adviser.
- An implementation owner.
- A person affected by the decision.
- Someone who can help build broader support.

Not everyone needs to be persuaded in the same way.

## Practical example

Suppose you want engineering to prioritize a new analytics event.

Before making the request, learn:
- Whether the event is necessary for an important decision.
- Whether existing instrumentation can answer the question.
- Whether the event requires a risky application change.
- Whether engineering or data should own implementation.
- Whether the work can be bundled with another change.

The better you understand the actual problem, the more options you can offer.

# 5. Stakeholder Mapping and Influence Planning

For complex initiatives, stakeholder mapping helps identify who needs to participate and how to approach them.

A simple approach considers two dimensions:

- **Decision influence:** How much can this stakeholder affect the outcome?
- **Level of impact or interest:** How much does the decision affect their responsibilities or objectives?

This creates four broad groups.

## High influence, high impact

Examples:
- The executive sponsoring the initiative.
- The engineering leader responsible for a critical dependency.
- The risk leader responsible for a material control decision.

Approach:
- Engage early.
- Understand their objectives.
- Discuss trade-offs directly.
- Involve them in decisions within their remit.
- Avoid surprising them with late escalations.

## High influence, lower direct impact

Examples:
- A senior executive whose support is necessary.
- A platform leader whose capacity affects several product groups.

Approach:
- Communicate the strategic rationale.
- Keep updates concise.
- Explain the decision required.
- Focus on material risks and resource implications.

## Lower formal influence, high impact

Examples:
- Customer Support teams affected by a workflow change.
- Analysts who must maintain the reporting system.
- Operations staff who handle exceptions.

Approach:
- Listen carefully.
- Involve them in discovery and implementation planning.
- Understand practical consequences.
- Ensure their expertise is represented in decisions.

These stakeholders may have more influence than their titles suggest.

## Lower influence, lower direct impact

Approach:
- Keep them informed when appropriate.
- Avoid unnecessary meetings.
- Provide accessible documentation.

This framework is a starting point, not a substitute for understanding people individually. Stakeholders may change roles as the initiative progresses.

## Build an influence map

For a significant initiative, document:

- Stakeholder.
- Relevant goals.
- Main concerns.
- Decision authority.
- Potential contribution.
- Likely objections.
- Best communication approach.
- Next action.

This prevents you from treating influence as a single presentation rather than an ongoing process.

# 6. Build Credibility Before You Need It

Influence is much easier when people already trust your judgment.

Credibility develops through repeated behavior, not a single impressive presentation.

## 6.1 Demonstrate competence

Show that you understand the product, customers, business model, and relevant constraints.

This does not mean pretending to be the expert in every discipline.

A credible PM knows enough to ask good questions, understand the implications of decisions, and recognize when specialist expertise is required.

For example, you do not need to design a payment-processing architecture yourself. You should understand the customer and business problem, the reliability implications, and the questions that need to be resolved with engineering.

## 6.2 Be reliable

If you promise to share research findings, clarify requirements, or follow up on a decision, do so.

Reliability matters because stakeholders often judge future requests based on their previous experience working with you.

Repeatedly changing scope, failing to communicate risks, or missing commitments without explanation damages credibility.

## 6.3 Be honest about uncertainty

Do not present assumptions as facts.

Instead of saying:

"This change will increase conversion by 15%."

Say:

"Our hypothesis is that this change will improve conversion. The current funnel suggests that the problem is significant, but we need an experiment to estimate the effect."

People are more likely to trust your judgment when they understand what you know, what you believe, and what remains uncertain.

## 6.4 Give credit

Recognize the contributions of engineers, designers, analysts, and other stakeholders.

If an engineering team identifies the critical insight behind a solution, acknowledge it.

If a designer improves the approach, do not present the work as your individual achievement.

Influence grows when people see that collaboration benefits the group rather than serving one person's reputation.

## 6.5 Admit mistakes

If you made an incorrect assumption or overlooked a dependency, acknowledge it.

A useful response is:

"I underestimated the complexity of this integration. That affected the plan, and I should have validated the dependency earlier. Let's review the remaining risks and agree on a more reliable approach."

Accountability strengthens credibility when it leads to corrective action.

# 7. Frame the Problem Around Shared Goals

People are more likely to support an initiative when they understand how it relates to outcomes they care about.

The same proposal can be framed differently for different stakeholders without changing the underlying facts.

## Example: Improve payment reliability

### For engineering

Explain:
- Failure patterns.
- System behavior.
- Incident frequency.
- Technical debt.
- Reliability implications.
- Maintenance cost.

### For finance

Explain:
- Failed transaction volume.
- Potential lost revenue.
- Refund or reconciliation costs.
- Support and operational expense.
- Expected investment.

### For customer support

Explain:
- Customer complaints.
- Repeated contact reasons.
- Resolution time.
- Confusing failure messages.
- Manual workarounds.

### For executives

Explain:
- Customer impact.
- Business implications.
- Material risks.
- Strategic relevance.
- Decision required.

The facts should remain consistent. The emphasis changes because the audience has different responsibilities.

## Use a shared-goal statement

A shared-goal statement describes what the group wants to achieve together.

For example:

"We want eligible customers to complete payments reliably while maintaining appropriate fraud controls and avoiding unnecessary operational work."

This creates a common reference point for discussing alternatives.

It also prevents the conversation from becoming a contest between departments.

# 8. Persuasion Through Evidence and Reasoning

Evidence is one of the strongest sources of influence, but data alone does not persuade people automatically.

People may interpret the same evidence differently because they have different assumptions, incentives, and experiences.

A strong product argument combines evidence, interpretation, uncertainty, and a clear recommendation.

## 8.1 Use a structured argument

A useful structure is:

1. **Problem:** What is happening?
2. **Evidence:** What supports this observation?
3. **Impact:** Why does it matter?
4. **Options:** What could we do?
5. **Trade-offs:** What are the costs and risks?
6. **Recommendation:** What do you propose?
7. **Decision:** What do you need from the audience?

### Example

**Problem:** Customers frequently abandon identity verification.

**Evidence:** Funnel data shows a large drop-off at document submission, and customer interviews reveal confusion about acceptable documents.

**Impact:** The problem reduces onboarding completion and creates avoidable support contacts.

**Options:**
- Improve instructions.
- Simplify the document-upload interaction.
- Change verification requirements, if appropriate and permitted.
- Improve recovery from technical failures.

**Trade-offs:** Some options are relatively low risk, while changes to verification requirements may have significant compliance and fraud implications.

**Recommendation:** First improve instructions and error recovery, then evaluate the effect before considering more substantial changes.

**Decision needed:** Agree on engineering capacity for the first experiment and identify the required risk review.

This format makes the reasoning inspectable rather than asking stakeholders to accept your conclusion without context.

## 8.2 Choose evidence appropriate to the decision

Different evidence answers different questions.

- Customer interviews help explain motivations and confusion.
- Funnel analytics help identify where behavior changes.
- Experiments help estimate the effect of a proposed intervention.
- Support data reveals recurring operational problems.
- Financial analysis helps estimate economic consequences.
- Technical analysis clarifies feasibility and system risk.
- Compliance expertise establishes applicable constraints.

No single source necessarily answers every question.

## 8.3 Avoid data dumping

A presentation containing 40 charts may be less persuasive than one that clearly explains three important findings.

Lead with the evidence that changes the decision.

Provide supporting details for people who need them, but make the core argument understandable without requiring the audience to reconstruct it independently.

## 8.4 Acknowledge counterevidence

If evidence weakens your proposal, discuss it openly.

For example:

"Customers report confusion during onboarding, but our data suggests that technical failures account for a substantial portion of abandonment. We should distinguish the two problems before investing in a major redesign."

This makes your recommendation more credible and can prevent costly misdiagnosis.

# 9. Use Storytelling to Make the Problem Concrete

Stories can help stakeholders understand customer experiences that aggregate metrics obscure.

A useful product story connects a real customer problem to broader evidence and a decision.

## Example

"Imagine a customer who has completed registration, verified their identity, and transferred money into their investment account. When they try to make their first investment, they encounter unfamiliar terminology and cannot understand the difference between available investment options. They leave without completing the transaction.

Our research shows that this confusion is recurring, and our funnel data shows substantial abandonment at this stage. We should investigate whether clearer explanations and more transparent comparisons can help customers make informed choices."

The story illustrates the problem, but the quantitative evidence establishes its broader significance.

## Use stories responsibly

- Do not present one customer's experience as representative of everyone.
- Do not invent customer quotes and present them as real.
- Protect personal and financial information.
- Explain the sample and limitations of research.
- Avoid emotionally manipulating stakeholders into ignoring legitimate risks.

Storytelling should clarify the problem, not replace evidence.

# 10. Influence Engineering Without Micromanaging

Engineering collaboration is one of the most important applications of influence without authority.

A PM needs engineering expertise to understand feasibility, trade-offs, dependencies, and technical risks.

Influence is more effective when the PM respects engineering judgment while remaining accountable for product priorities.

## 10.1 Start with the problem, not the prescribed solution

Weak request:

"Build this feature exactly as described in the ticket."

Better request:

"Customers cannot reliably recover from a failed payment. We need to reduce repeated failures and make recovery clearer. Can we explore the possible approaches and their technical implications?"

The second approach gives engineering room to contribute expertise.

This does not mean requirements should always be vague. Once the problem is understood and a solution is selected, clear requirements and acceptance criteria may be essential.

## 10.2 Understand the engineering trade-offs

Engineering may need to balance:
- New functionality.
- Reliability.
- Security.
- Technical debt.
- Architecture.
- Maintainability.
- Operational load.
- Delivery predictability.

A product manager who ignores these concerns may win agreement to a short-term solution that creates long-term problems.

A better approach is to ask:

- What is the simplest viable approach?
- What risks does it introduce?
- What will it cost to maintain?
- What assumptions should we validate?
- Is there a smaller experiment?
- What happens if we delay this work?
- Which parts are reversible?

## 10.3 Negotiate scope, not just deadlines

If engineering cannot deliver the entire proposal in time, consider:

- Reducing scope.
- Splitting the work into stages.
- Testing with a smaller customer segment.
- Reusing an existing capability.
- Delaying lower-value components.
- Changing the launch sequence.
- Running research before committing to implementation.

The goal is to preserve the essential customer outcome while making a realistic commitment.

## 10.4 Respect technical authority

Influence does not mean pressuring engineers to accept unsafe architecture or bypass necessary quality practices.

When a technical disagreement remains unresolved, identify the decision owner and clarify the trade-offs.

The PM should explain customer and business needs. Engineering should explain technical implications. The appropriate decision-maker should resolve trade-offs according to the organization's decision rights.

# 11. Influence Design, Data, Marketing, and Other Functions

Different disciplines have different forms of expertise. Effective influence recognizes that expertise instead of treating other functions as service providers.

## Design

Designers contribute research, interaction design, information architecture, accessibility, and customer experience judgment.

To influence design decisions:
- Clarify the customer problem.
- Share relevant evidence.
- Explain constraints.
- Explore multiple solutions.
- Invite critique before the direction becomes fixed.
- Agree on the desired customer outcome.

Avoid asking design to make a predetermined interface look attractive when the underlying customer problem has not been understood.

## Data and Analytics

Analysts may be responsible for multiple teams and complex data systems.

To gain support:
- Explain the decision the analysis will inform.
- Clarify what is already known.
- Share the deadline and business significance.
- Ask what evidence is feasible.
- Consider reusable instrumentation or self-service analysis.
- Distinguish urgent decisions from interesting but nonessential questions.

"Can you make a dashboard?" is less useful than "We need to understand which step contributes most to first-payment abandonment so we can choose the next experiment."

## Marketing and Sales

Marketing and Sales may prioritize acquisition, positioning, revenue, or customer commitments.

Influence requires:
- Understanding their objectives.
- Sharing product constraints early.
- Aligning promises with actual product capabilities.
- Agreeing on the target customer and value proposition.
- Using customer evidence to resolve disagreements.
- Making launch readiness and dependencies explicit.

Avoid treating Sales requests as automatically correct or dismissing them as distractions. They may reveal valuable customer needs, contractual obligations, or market opportunities.

## Customer Support and Operations

Support and Operations often have detailed knowledge of customer pain, exceptions, and manual work.

Involve them early enough to identify problems before launch.

Useful questions include:
- What are the most common customer complaints?
- Which processes require manual workarounds?
- What happens when the product fails?
- Which exceptions are difficult to handle?
- What would a successful launch require operationally?

Their feedback may reveal risks that are not visible in product analytics.

# 12. Handling Resistance

Resistance is information. It may indicate a weak proposal, a real constraint, competing incentives, lack of trust, or an unresolved decision.

The first step is to understand which situation you are facing.

## 12.1 Diagnose the objection

When someone disagrees, ask:

- What specifically concerns you?
- Which assumption do you think is incorrect?
- What constraint are we overlooking?
- What evidence would change your view?
- Which alternative would you recommend?
- Is the disagreement about the goal, the solution, the timing, or the risk?

Avoid assuming that every objection is irrational or political.

## 12.2 Respond to the underlying concern

### Objection: "We don't have capacity."

Possible response:

"Understood. Could we review the current commitments and compare the impact of this work with the existing priorities? If it is not the right time, I'd like to understand what would need to change for us to address it."

### Objection: "This will create technical debt."

Possible response:

"Let's understand the long-term maintenance cost and whether there is a simpler approach. If the proposed solution creates unacceptable debt, we should change the approach rather than ignore the concern."

### Objection: "The data is not reliable."

Possible response:

"Which part of the measurement is unreliable? Let's identify what we can trust, what needs validation, and whether a smaller decision can be made using the available evidence."

### Objection: "We tried this before."

Possible response:

"What happened last time, and what did we learn? Has the customer problem, implementation approach, or market context changed since then?"

### Objection: "This is not a priority."

Possible response:

"Let's compare the expected impact and urgency against the work already committed. If this should take precedence, we need to make the trade-off explicit."

The goal is not to eliminate objections. It is to turn vague resistance into a specific problem that can be evaluated.

# 13. Negotiation: Reaching Agreements Without Positional Power

Negotiation is the process of reaching an agreement when people have different interests, preferences, or constraints.

Product managers negotiate frequently:
- Engineering capacity.
- Launch scope.
- Timelines.
- Feature requirements.
- Business commitments.
- Research support.
- Cross-team dependencies.
- Acceptable trade-offs.

## 13.1 Separate positions from interests

A position is what someone says they want.

An interest is the underlying reason they want it.

Example:

**Engineering position:** "We cannot take this work into the current sprint."

Possible interests:
- Protecting committed work.
- Avoiding context switching.
- Maintaining delivery predictability.
- Preventing rushed implementation.
- Meeting a critical reliability commitment.

Once the interests are understood, alternative agreements may become possible.

Perhaps the work can be split, a lower-priority item can be removed, or a short technical investigation can establish the real effort required.

## 13.2 Identify your alternatives

Before negotiating, understand what you can do if no agreement is reached.

For example:
- Delay the launch.
- Reduce scope.
- Use an existing capability.
- Run additional research.
- Escalate a genuine portfolio trade-off.
- Choose a different solution.

Your alternative does not need to be ideal. Knowing it helps you avoid accepting an unrealistic agreement simply to maintain momentum.

## 13.3 Make trade-offs explicit

Suppose Sales requests a feature for an important customer commitment, but Engineering says it cannot be delivered by the requested date.

Possible options include:
- Deliver the full feature later.
- Deliver a smaller, safe subset.
- Provide a temporary workaround.
- Negotiate a different customer commitment.
- Reallocate capacity from another initiative.

The negotiation should make the costs and consequences of each option visible.

Avoid treating dates and scope as independent variables when both depend on limited resources.

## 13.4 Aim for durable agreements

A good agreement should clarify:
- What will be done.
- Who owns it.
- By when, or under what conditions.
- What assumptions apply.
- What is excluded.
- What could change the commitment.
- How risks will be communicated.

An agreement that depends on hidden assumptions is likely to create conflict later.

## 13.5 Know when not to compromise

Not every issue should be resolved through compromise.

Security controls, legal obligations, regulatory requirements, and serious customer-safety concerns may impose firm boundaries.

The correct approach is to explore compliant alternatives, involve the authorized decision-makers, and avoid pressuring people to bypass legitimate controls.

# 14. Build Coalitions for Cross-Functional Initiatives

Some initiatives require coordinated support from several stakeholders before a decision can succeed.

A coalition is a group of people who support a shared objective and contribute different forms of expertise, resources, or influence.

For example, improving investment onboarding may require:
- A product sponsor.
- Engineering support.
- Design and research.
- Data analysis.
- Risk and Compliance review.
- Customer Support readiness.

## Build the coalition step by step

### Step 1: Define the shared objective

Make the desired outcome understandable to everyone involved.

### Step 2: Identify necessary stakeholders

Include people who own critical decisions, resources, expertise, or operational consequences.

### Step 3: Engage key stakeholders early

Do not wait until the final presentation to discover that a critical team has unresolved concerns.

### Step 4: Listen and adapt

Incorporate valid feedback into the proposal.

A coalition should not be a group of people who agree with you regardless of evidence.

### Step 5: Clarify commitments

Agree on responsibilities, dependencies, decision rights, and escalation paths.

### Step 6: Maintain alignment

Share meaningful changes, risks, and decisions with the people who need to know.

## Avoid coalition-building as political manipulation

Do not secretly organize people to pressure a stakeholder, misrepresent support, or exclude legitimate decision-makers.

Build support through shared goals, transparent reasoning, and meaningful participation.

The strongest coalition is one in which participants understand why the initiative matters and what they are agreeing to contribute.

# 15. Influence Through Small Experiments

Sometimes the most persuasive argument is a small demonstration.

When stakeholders disagree about an uncertain product opportunity, a limited experiment may be more useful than another meeting.

## Example: Simplifying onboarding

The product manager believes that clearer instructions will reduce abandonment. Engineering worries that a broader redesign will be expensive.

Instead of demanding a complete redesign, the PM proposes:

1. Review the funnel and customer feedback.
2. Identify the most confusing step.
3. Test revised instructions in a limited, appropriate context.
4. Measure completion and relevant guardrail metrics.
5. Evaluate the result.
6. Decide whether broader implementation is justified.

This approach reduces the cost of disagreement because the team can learn before making a larger commitment.

## When experiments are useful

Experiments are particularly valuable when:
- The problem is real but the solution is uncertain.
- The cost of a small test is low.
- The outcome can be measured credibly.
- The test does not create unacceptable customer or operational risk.
- The result could meaningfully change the decision.

Not every decision can be tested through an A/B experiment. Architectural changes, regulatory decisions, and low-frequency high-impact risks may require analysis, simulations, expert review, or other forms of validation.

# 16. Influence in Remote and Distributed Teams

Influence becomes more difficult when people work across time zones, functions, or locations.

Informal conversations are less frequent, context can be fragmented, and disagreements may remain unresolved because people do not share the same meeting schedule.

## Practical techniques

### Write clearly

Document the problem, evidence, options, recommendation, and decision required.

Good written communication reduces the need for repeated meetings.

### Share context before meetings

Give stakeholders enough information to understand the issue and prepare questions.

Do not use a meeting to reveal a complex proposal for the first time if participants need time to analyze it.

### Use asynchronous decisions when appropriate

For low-risk decisions, a clear written proposal and a defined response window may be more efficient than a meeting.

For high-impact or sensitive decisions, synchronous discussion may be necessary.

### Make decision ownership explicit

A discussion is not a decision.

Document who owns the decision, what was agreed, and what remains unresolved.

### Watch for silent disagreement

A lack of objections does not always mean genuine agreement.

Ask stakeholders to identify concerns and unresolved assumptions, especially when the decision affects their teams significantly.

### Follow up without excessive pressure

Use agreed timelines and reminders. If a decision is blocked, clarify what is needed rather than repeatedly sending messages that do not advance the issue.

# 17. Ethical Influence and Common Anti-Patterns

Influence becomes harmful when it relies on deception, coercion, or deliberate exploitation of people.

## Anti-pattern 1: Using false urgency

Claiming that an initiative is urgent when there is no genuine deadline can pressure stakeholders into poor decisions.

**Better approach:** Explain the actual cost of delay and distinguish hard deadlines from preferences.

## Anti-pattern 2: Cherry-picking evidence

Presenting only the data that supports your recommendation can lead to decisions that fail when contrary evidence emerges.

**Better approach:** Present material counterevidence, assumptions, and uncertainties.

## Anti-pattern 3: Invoking executive support inaccurately

Saying "leadership wants this" when no such decision exists undermines trust and creates confusion.

**Better approach:** State who has actually made the decision and what remains open for discussion.

## Anti-pattern 4: Using relationships to bypass legitimate controls

Pressuring a friendly stakeholder to approve something they would otherwise reject can weaken governance.

**Better approach:** Address the underlying concern and follow the appropriate decision process.

## Anti-pattern 5: Winning the argument but damaging the relationship

Humiliating someone in a meeting may establish that your reasoning was stronger, but it can make future collaboration more difficult.

**Better approach:** Challenge the idea, evidence, or assumptions without attacking the person.

## Anti-pattern 6: Treating every disagreement as a persuasion problem

Sometimes the proposal is wrong. Sometimes the other person has better information. Sometimes the decision is genuinely a matter of competing priorities.

**Better approach:** Remain willing to change your own position.

## Anti-pattern 7: Overpromising on behalf of other teams

Committing engineering or operations to dates without their input can create avoidable conflict.

**Better approach:** Negotiate commitments with the people responsible for delivering them.

## Anti-pattern 8: Confusing consensus with success

Trying to make every stakeholder equally enthusiastic can delay decisions indefinitely.

**Better approach:** Seek sufficient alignment, involve the right decision-makers, and respect legitimate decision rights.

# 18. Case Study: Influencing Engineering to Improve Payment Reliability

## Background

You are the product manager for payments at a fintech company.

Customer support reports increasing complaints about failed transfers. Funnel analytics show that a meaningful percentage of customers attempt a transfer but do not complete it.

You believe the team should prioritize payment reliability improvements.

However:
- Engineering is focused on a committed platform migration.
- Finance wants the team to prioritize a revenue-generating feature.
- Operations is concerned about manual reconciliation work.
- Leadership wants the next product release to remain on schedule.

You have no direct authority over these stakeholders.

## Step 1: Establish the problem with evidence

You investigate:
- Transfer attempts and completion rates.
- Failure reasons.
- Customer complaints.
- Manual reconciliation effort.
- The timing and severity of incidents.
- Potential revenue and customer-trust implications.

You distinguish between confirmed findings and assumptions.

For example, you may know that failed transfers have increased, but still need engineering to determine which failures are caused by the platform and which originate from external providers.

## Step 2: Understand stakeholder interests

You meet with each relevant stakeholder.

Engineering explains that the migration addresses a reliability constraint but also carries delivery risk.

Finance explains that the revenue-generating feature is tied to a commercial opportunity.

Operations shows that certain transfer failures create substantial manual work.

Leadership is concerned about changing commitments shortly before the release.

These concerns are legitimate. The decision is not simply between a customer-focused PM and uncooperative stakeholders.

## Step 3: Reframe the decision

Instead of asking engineering to abandon the migration, you propose evaluating the options together.

Possible options include:
- Continue the migration unchanged.
- Add a targeted reliability fix.
- Complete a short technical investigation first.
- Reduce the scope of the revenue feature.
- Sequence the work into separate stages.

You explain the expected customer impact, operational consequences, and uncertainty associated with each option.

## Step 4: Find a practical path forward

Engineering identifies a narrow failure pattern that can be investigated without stopping the entire migration.

Operations helps quantify the manual workload. Finance evaluates the commercial implications of delaying part of the revenue feature.

The group agrees to a short investigation, after which the authorized leaders will make a resource-allocation decision.

This is not guaranteed to produce the outcome you originally preferred. It creates a better basis for choosing.

## Step 5: Make the agreement explicit

Document:
- The investigation scope.
- The responsible engineering owner.
- The expected completion date.
- The evidence required.
- The decision that will follow.
- The existing commitments that remain unchanged.
- The conditions that would trigger escalation.

## Step 6: Follow through

After the investigation, present the findings and recommendation.

If the evidence supports a targeted reliability fix, show the expected benefit and trade-offs. If it does not, update your recommendation.

Once the decision is made, communicate it to affected teams and monitor the results.

## Lessons from the case

- Influence starts with understanding the problem and the interests of others.
- Stakeholders may have legitimate constraints even when they disagree with your priorities.
- Evidence helps frame decisions, but trade-offs still require judgment.
- Small investigations can reduce uncertainty before large commitments.
- Clear ownership and follow-through convert agreement into action.
- Credibility depends on being willing to revise your recommendation when the evidence changes.

# 19. Practical Framework: Preparing for an Important Influence Conversation

Before a high-stakes meeting, answer the following questions.

## A. Define the objective

- What decision or action do I need?
- Why does it matter?
- What outcome should the conversation produce?
- What is the cost of delay?

## B. Understand the audience

- Who is the decision-maker?
- Who provides critical expertise?
- Who will implement the decision?
- What are their objectives and constraints?
- What concerns are they likely to raise?

## C. Prepare the reasoning

- What evidence supports the problem?
- What remains uncertain?
- What alternatives exist?
- What are the major trade-offs?
- What counterarguments deserve attention?
- What is my recommendation?

## D. Plan the conversation

- What context should be shared in advance?
- Which questions should I ask?
- Which decisions are open for discussion?
- Which constraints are non-negotiable?
- What agreement would be acceptable?
- What will I do if no agreement is reached?

## E. Close the loop

- What was decided?
- Who owns each action?
- What are the commitments and assumptions?
- What remains unresolved?
- When should the issue be reviewed?

This preparation is particularly useful for resource negotiations, roadmap disagreements, executive reviews, and cross-team dependencies.

# 20. Your Influence Development Plan

Influence improves through practice and reflection.

Identify the situations in which you struggle most.

Examples:
- Engineering disagrees with your priorities.
- Executives challenge your recommendation.
- Stakeholders ignore your requests.
- You struggle to secure data or design support.
- Meetings end without decisions.
- You avoid difficult conversations.
- You gain agreement but cannot secure follow-through.

Choose one recurring situation and practice a specific behavior.

For example:

**Current pattern:** You repeatedly ask engineering to prioritize your request without discussing the existing commitments.

**New behavior:** Before requesting capacity, prepare the customer impact, expected value, alternative options, and the trade-off required.

Then evaluate whether the conversation becomes more productive.

A simple weekly reflection can help:

1. Which conversation required influence this week?
2. What did I understand about the other person's interests?
3. What evidence or reasoning was most useful?
4. Where did I make an unsupported assumption?
5. Did I listen and adapt when challenged?
6. Was the decision and ownership clear?
7. What would I do differently next time?

The objective is not to become persuasive in every situation. It is to become more effective at helping people reach good decisions together.

# Key Takeaways

- Product managers need influence because product outcomes depend on people they do not directly manage.
- Authority is formal decision-making power; influence is the ability to shape understanding, decisions, and behavior.
- Effective influence begins with understanding stakeholders' goals, concerns, constraints, and decision rights.
- Credibility grows through competence, reliability, honesty, accountability, and giving credit to others.
- Strong product arguments connect customer problems, evidence, business impact, alternatives, trade-offs, and a clear recommendation.
- Adapt communication to the audience without changing the underlying facts.
- Engineering, Design, Data, Marketing, Operations, and Risk each contribute expertise that should shape product decisions.
- Resistance should be investigated rather than automatically treated as obstruction.
- Negotiation works best when people understand one another's underlying interests and make trade-offs explicit.
- Coalitions should be built through shared goals and transparent collaboration, not manipulation.
- Small experiments can help resolve disagreements when uncertainty is high and testing is appropriate.
- Ethical influence requires honesty, respect for legitimate controls, and willingness to change your position.
- The goal is not to win every argument. It is to help the organization make better decisions and deliver meaningful outcomes.
