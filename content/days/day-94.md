# Day 94: Competitive Intelligence & Market Dynamics

# Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- Build a systematic competitive intelligence process.
- Distinguish direct competitors, indirect competitors, substitutes, and the status quo.
- Analyze competitive positioning beyond feature comparisons.
- Understand market structure, industry value chains, and changing sources of power.
- Identify market signals that could affect product strategy.
- Conduct competitor research using public and ethical information sources.
- Evaluate competitor strengths, weaknesses, and likely strategic responses.
- Avoid reactive roadmap decisions driven by competitor announcements.
- Translate competitive insights into actionable product decisions.
- Apply competitive intelligence to fintech, AI products, and digital platforms.

# 1. Why Competitive Intelligence Matters

Product Managers operate in markets where customers have alternatives.

Those alternatives may be other products, traditional service providers, manual processes, or the decision to do nothing.

Understanding these alternatives helps a Product Manager answer important questions:

- Why would a customer choose our product?
- Which customer needs are underserved?
- Where do competitors have a structural advantage?
- What makes our product meaningfully different?
- How might market changes affect our business model?
- Which competitor moves deserve a response?
- Where should we invest to improve our competitive position?

Competitive intelligence is the systematic collection and interpretation of information about competitors, customers' alternatives, and market dynamics to support better decisions.

It is not simply collecting competitor screenshots, tracking feature releases, or creating comparison tables.

**The objective is not to know everything about competitors. It is to make better product and business decisions.**

Consider a cryptocurrency exchange that notices a competitor launching zero-fee trading.

A superficial response would be to remove its own trading fees immediately.

A stronger response investigates:
- Which customer segment is targeted?
- Does the competitor earn revenue through spreads or other services?
- Is the offer temporary or permanent?
- How important are fees relative to liquidity, trust, security, and available assets?
- Would matching the offer damage profitability?
- Could the exchange differentiate through reliability or a superior trading experience?

Competitive intelligence helps the Product Manager understand what the move means before deciding what to do.

# 2. Understand the Competitive Landscape

A competitor is any alternative that can satisfy the same customer need or compete for the same resources, attention, or budget.

The most obvious competitor is not always the most important one.

## 2.1 Direct competitors

Direct competitors target similar customers with comparable products.

Examples:
- Two cryptocurrency exchanges targeting active retail traders.
- Two online insurance platforms selling supplementary health insurance.
- Two SaaS platforms serving Instagram-based merchants.

Direct competitors are usually the easiest to identify because their offerings are visibly similar.

## 2.2 Indirect competitors

Indirect competitors solve the same underlying problem through a different approach.

Examples:
- A digital insurance marketplace versus an insurance broker.
- A crypto exchange versus a brokerage platform offering overlapping investment services.
- A merchant storefront platform versus an order-management system combined with social messaging.

The products may differ, but customers may choose between them when addressing the same need.

## 2.3 Substitutes

A substitute is another way to achieve the desired outcome.

For an online insurance comparison product, substitutes might include:
- Calling several insurance agents.
- Asking friends or family.
- Reading policy documents independently.
- Contacting insurers directly.

Substitutes are important because customers do not always compare products within the same software category.

## 2.4 The status quo

Sometimes the strongest competitor is the customer's current behavior.

Examples:
- Continuing to trade on an existing exchange.
- Delaying the purchase of supplementary insurance.
- Managing Instagram orders manually.
- Keeping financial records in a spreadsheet.
- Asking a human support agent instead of using an AI assistant.

Changing behavior creates friction. A new product must provide enough value to justify that change.

## 2.5 Internal alternatives

Large organizations may have internal tools, teams, or processes that compete with a proposed product investment.

For example, a bank may decide whether to buy an external AI customer-support solution or improve its existing support platform.

The external vendor is not competing only with other vendors. It is also competing with the organization's own development capacity and the option to do nothing.

## 2.6 Build a competitor landscape

Start with a broad map of alternatives.

| Category | Key question | Example: insurance platform |
|---|---|---|
| Direct competitors | Who offers a similar digital purchase journey? | Online insurance marketplaces |
| Indirect competitors | Who solves the same need differently? | Traditional insurance brokers |
| Substitutes | What else can customers do? | Contact insurers individually |
| Status quo | What happens if customers do nothing? | Remain uninsured |
| Internal alternative | Could the organization solve the problem another way? | Existing insurer sales channels |

Do not assume every alternative deserves equal research effort. Prioritize those most relevant to your target segment and strategic decisions.

# 3. Start With the Customer, Not the Competitor

A common competitive-analysis mistake is beginning with a list of companies and their features.

A more useful starting point is the customer problem.

Suppose customers want to buy supplementary health insurance but struggle to compare plan exclusions, deductibles, premiums, and coverage limits.

The competitive question is not simply:

"Which websites sell supplementary insurance?"

It is:

"Which alternatives help customers choose appropriate coverage with sufficient confidence and acceptable effort?"

This framing may reveal competitors that are invisible when searching only for similar websites.

## 3.1 Understand the job customers are trying to accomplish

Use the Jobs-to-be-Done perspective.

Ask:
- What triggers the customer to seek a solution?
- What outcome are they trying to achieve?
- What makes the current process difficult?
- What alternatives have they tried?
- What trade-offs are they willing to accept?
- What makes them trust one option over another?

For a crypto exchange, a customer may not simply want "an exchange with more coins." The underlying job may be to buy a particular asset quickly, at a predictable cost, with confidence that funds can be withdrawn safely.

That distinction changes which competitors and product attributes matter.

## 3.2 Analyze switching behavior

Customers may remain with a competitor even when another product offers better features.

Reasons include:
- Switching costs.
- Existing balances or transaction history.
- Familiarity.
- Loyalty programs.
- Network effects.
- Existing integrations.
- Trust.
- Perceived risk.
- Lack of awareness.

Understanding switching behavior can reveal barriers that feature development alone will not overcome.

# 4. Build a Competitive Intelligence System

Competitive intelligence should be a repeatable process rather than an occasional research project.

A practical process includes six stages.

1. Define the decision.
2. Identify the relevant competitors and alternatives.
3. Collect evidence from appropriate sources.
4. Analyze the implications.
5. Recommend an action.
6. Monitor whether the conclusion remains valid.

## 4.1 Define the decision first

Examples of decisions that may require competitive intelligence:
- Entering a new market.
- Selecting a target customer segment.
- Revising pricing.
- Prioritizing a capability.
- Choosing a distribution channel.
- Responding to a competitor launch.
- Evaluating a partnership.
- Reconsidering product positioning.

The decision determines what information matters.

If you are evaluating a new market entry, company ownership, regulation, distribution access, and local customer behavior may matter more than a detailed comparison of every user-interface feature.

## 4.2 Create a competitor profile

For each important competitor, document:

- Target segments.
- Customer problems addressed.
- Product positioning.
- Core capabilities.
- Pricing and packaging.
- Distribution channels.
- Business model.
- Key partnerships.
- Apparent strengths.
- Apparent weaknesses.
- Customer complaints and unmet needs.
- Recent strategic changes.
- Evidence quality and confidence.

Use the same categories for each competitor so comparisons remain meaningful.

## 4.3 Separate facts from interpretations

A competitor profile should distinguish between observable evidence and your conclusions.

**Fact:** A competitor publicly announced a free basic subscription tier.

**Interpretation:** The competitor may be attempting to accelerate adoption among price-sensitive users.

**Hypothesis:** The competitor may monetize through premium features or adjacent financial services.

**Strategic implication:** We should investigate whether price is the main adoption barrier for our target segment before changing our own pricing.

This discipline prevents speculation from becoming accepted organizational knowledge.

# 5. Sources of Competitive Intelligence

Competitive research can use many sources, each with different limitations.

## 5.1 Public product information

Examples:
- Product websites.
- Pricing pages.
- Help centers.
- Product documentation.
- Public release notes.
- App store listings.
- Public demos.
- API documentation.
- Terms and conditions.

These sources are useful for understanding published capabilities, product positioning, and commercial terms.

However, published information may be incomplete, outdated, or different from the experience of actual customers.

## 5.2 Customer research

Customers can explain how they compare alternatives and why they choose one product over another.

Useful methods include:
- Interviews.
- Win/loss analysis.
- Churn interviews.
- Surveys.
- Customer-support analysis.
- Sales-call analysis.
- Usability tests.

Ask customers about actual behavior rather than hypothetical preferences alone.

For example, "Which platforms did you evaluate before choosing this exchange?" is usually more informative than "Would you use a platform with more features?"

## 5.3 Reviews and public discussions

Public reviews and discussion forums can reveal:
- Repeated complaints.
- Unexpected use cases.
- Trust concerns.
- Pricing frustrations.
- Missing capabilities.
- Switching motivations.

But these sources are biased. People with strong positive or negative experiences may be more likely to post, and individual claims may be inaccurate.

Look for repeated patterns across independent sources rather than treating a single comment as proof.

## 5.4 Company announcements and public filings

Depending on the market, useful sources may include:
- Earnings reports.
- Investor presentations.
- Press releases.
- Partnership announcements.
- Regulatory disclosures.
- Public financial statements.
- Hiring trends.

These sources can help infer strategic priorities and investment areas, but public statements may emphasize the most favorable interpretation of company performance.

## 5.5 Market and industry research

Industry reports, market data, and research publications may help estimate:
- Market size.
- Growth rates.
- Customer segments.
- Adoption trends.
- Regulatory changes.
- Technology costs.
- Industry economics.

Evaluate the methodology, publication date, geographic scope, and definitions before relying on a number.

## 5.6 Ethical and legal boundaries

Competitive intelligence should use legitimate sources and methods.

Avoid:
- Accessing systems without authorization.
- Misrepresenting your identity to obtain confidential information.
- Soliciting trade secrets.
- Violating contractual restrictions.
- Collecting personal data without an appropriate basis.
- Circumventing access controls.

The objective is to understand the market, not to compromise competitors or customers.

# 6. Competitive Feature Analysis

Feature comparison is useful when the features are tied to customer outcomes and strategic positioning.

It is much less useful when teams simply count features.

## 6.1 Create a feature matrix

Suppose you are evaluating three online insurance platforms.

| Capability | Platform A | Platform B | Your product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Premium comparison | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Coverage comparison | Basic | Detailed | Detailed |
| Explanation of exclusions | Limited | Moderate | Detailed |
| Online purchase | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Personalized recommendations | Limited | Yes | Planned |
| Human assistance | Yes | Limited | Available for selected cases |

This matrix shows differences in published capabilities, but it does not tell you which product creates the most value.

The next question is how well each capability serves the target customer.

## 6.2 Add customer importance

Not all features matter equally.

Suppose customers rank the importance of capabilities as follows:

| Capability | Customer importance |
|---|---:|
| Clear premium comparison | High |
| Understanding exclusions | Very high |
| Fast purchase process | High |
| Decorative customization | Low |
| Detailed product recommendations | Medium to high |

A competitor's missing low-importance feature may not be an opportunity.

A competitor's weakness in a high-importance area may be much more strategically significant.

## 6.3 Measure capability quality

A simple yes/no feature matrix hides differences in execution.

Two exchanges may both offer recurring purchases, but one may have clearer setup, better error handling, more payment options, and stronger transaction visibility.

Evaluate:
- Ease of use.
- Reliability.
- Speed.
- Completeness.
- Trust.
- Accessibility.
- Integration quality.
- Customer support.
- Actual outcome quality.

Whenever possible, use direct observation and customer evidence rather than assumptions.

## 6.4 Distinguish parity from differentiation

**Parity capabilities** are necessary to remain competitive.

Examples might include:
- Basic account security.
- Reliable deposits and withdrawals.
- Standard insurance purchase functionality.
- Core reporting features in a SaaS product.

**Differentiating capabilities** create a meaningful advantage for a specific customer segment.

Examples might include:
- Exceptionally clear insurance coverage explanations.
- More reliable transaction-status visibility.
- Specialized tools for a particular trader segment.
- Better onboarding for nontechnical merchants.

Not every differentiating feature becomes a competitive advantage. Customers must value it, and the company must be able to deliver it sustainably.

# 7. Positioning and Competitive Differentiation

Positioning explains why a particular customer should choose your product over relevant alternatives.

A useful positioning statement identifies:

1. The target customer.
2. The customer's important need.
3. The product category or alternative.
4. The distinctive value provided.
5. The reason to believe the claim.

For example:

"For first-time buyers of supplementary health insurance who struggle to compare coverage, our platform provides clear, side-by-side explanations of plan differences so they can make a more informed choice."

This is more useful than saying the product is "the most innovative insurance platform."

## 7.1 Use a positioning map carefully

A positioning map compares competitors across two dimensions that matter to customers.

For example:
- Ease of comparison.
- Depth of coverage explanation.

Or:
- Trading cost.
- Perceived reliability.

A map can help identify areas where competitors cluster and where customer needs may be underserved.

But the axes must represent meaningful customer trade-offs. A map using arbitrary dimensions can produce attractive but misleading conclusions.

Also distinguish between measured performance and perceived positioning. A company may be objectively reliable but poorly trusted because customers lack visibility into its processes.

## 7.2 Identify meaningful differentiation

A useful differentiation opportunity usually satisfies several conditions:

- Customers care about it.
- Current alternatives do not satisfy the need adequately.
- Your organization can deliver it.
- Competitors cannot copy it immediately or cheaply, or your execution can remain better.
- The economics are sustainable.

A capability that is easy to copy may still be worth building, but it is unlikely to create a durable advantage on its own.

# 8. Understand Market Structure and Industry Value Chains

Competitive dynamics are shaped by more than product features.

The structure of a market influences who captures value, who controls distribution, and which capabilities are difficult to replicate.

## 8.1 Map the value chain

Consider a digital insurance purchase journey.

Potential participants include:
- Customers.
- Insurance brokers or distributors.
- Insurance companies.
- Payment providers.
- Customer-service providers.
- Technology platforms.
- Regulatory and supervisory bodies.

Ask:
- Who owns the customer relationship?
- Who controls access to the product?
- Who sets prices and terms?
- Who bears financial risk?
- Who handles claims?
- Who controls the data?
- Who captures the largest share of the economics?

A distributor that owns the customer relationship may have a different competitive position from a technology provider that only supplies infrastructure.

## 8.2 Identify sources of bargaining power

Power may come from:
- Customer access.
- Brand and trust.
- Proprietary data.
- Exclusive distribution.
- Network effects.
- Switching costs.
- Regulatory licenses.
- Economies of scale.
- Technical infrastructure.
- Scarce supply.

For example, a crypto exchange with deep liquidity may attract traders because better liquidity improves execution. More traders can then improve liquidity further, reinforcing the exchange's position.

This dynamic can make competition difficult for a smaller platform, even if the smaller platform has an attractive user interface.

## 8.3 Understand value capture

Creating value and capturing value are different.

A product might provide an excellent customer experience while capturing little economic value because:
- Competitors can copy the feature.
- Customers expect it for free.
- Suppliers control pricing.
- Distribution partners capture most of the margin.
- Operating costs are too high.

Competitive intelligence should investigate not only who creates the best experience, but also who can sustain the business.

# 9. Market Size, Growth, and Attractiveness

A market may be large but unattractive for a particular company.

A useful market assessment considers:
- Customer demand.
- Growth.
- Competition.
- Distribution access.
- Regulation.
- Economics.
- Required capabilities.
- Strategic fit.

## 9.1 TAM, SAM, and SOM

These concepts help structure market sizing.

- **TAM — Total Addressable Market:** The total potential demand if the relevant market could be served.
- **SAM — Serviceable Available Market:** The portion that fits the product's target geography, customer segment, and business model.
- **SOM — Serviceable Obtainable Market:** The portion the company can realistically capture within a defined period, given competition and execution constraints.

Suppose a company estimates that the total annual market for a type of insurance is worth $1 billion.

Its initial product serves only one geography and a particular customer segment, representing an estimated $150 million opportunity.

If the company could realistically capture 2% of that serviceable market over a defined period, the implied annual market share would be $3 million in market value.

That is not automatically company revenue. The business model may capture only a commission or fee from the underlying transaction value.

Market sizing should reflect how the company actually earns money.

## 9.2 Use bottom-up estimates

Whenever possible, estimate opportunity using observable customer and transaction assumptions.

For example:

Eligible customers × Expected purchase frequency × Average revenue per purchase.

This approach forces assumptions into the open.

A top-down industry report can provide useful context, but it should not replace an explanation of how the company expects to acquire and serve customers.

## 9.3 Evaluate market attractiveness relative to capabilities

A market can be attractive in general but unsuitable for your organization.

Consider:
- Existing distribution.
- Brand credibility.
- Technical capabilities.
- Regulatory readiness.
- Capital availability.
- Partnerships.
- Time to market.
- Opportunity cost.

The best market is not always the biggest one. It may be the segment where your company has a credible path to differentiated value.

# 10. Competitive Dynamics and Market Signals

Markets evolve continuously.

Competitive intelligence should help you identify changes that may alter customer behavior, economics, or strategic positioning.

## 10.1 Product and pricing signals

Examples:
- A competitor introduces a lower-cost tier.
- A product removes a major usage limitation.
- A new feature becomes standard across the market.
- A competitor changes transaction fees.
- A product bundles several services into one subscription.

These signals may indicate changing customer expectations or a new competitive strategy.

## 10.2 Distribution signals

Examples:
- A competitor forms a major partnership.
- A new app store or channel changes discovery.
- An insurer opens a new digital distribution channel.
- A financial platform integrates with a popular ecosystem.

Distribution changes can matter more than product changes because they alter access to customers.

## 10.3 Technology signals

Examples:
- A new model substantially improves local-language performance.
- A payment API reduces integration complexity.
- Infrastructure costs fall.
- An open-source tool makes a previously expensive capability more accessible.

Technology changes can reduce barriers to entry, but they may also make existing differentiation easier to copy.

## 10.4 Regulatory signals

Examples:
- New licensing requirements.
- Changes to customer-disclosure obligations.
- Restrictions on data sharing.
- New requirements for financial promotions.
- Changes to digital insurance distribution.

Regulatory signals may alter the cost of serving a segment or make some business models impractical.

## 10.5 Hiring and organizational signals

Public hiring patterns may provide clues about investment priorities.

For example, repeated hiring for enterprise sales, compliance infrastructure, or AI evaluation may suggest areas of strategic focus.

However, hiring alone does not prove that a competitor has successfully built or launched a capability.

Treat it as a hypothesis-generating signal, not a confirmed fact.

# 11. Competitor Response Analysis

A product strategy should consider how competitors might respond to your own moves.

If your exchange reduces fees, competitors might:
- Match the reduction.
- Introduce targeted promotions.
- Improve liquidity.
- Bundle premium services.
- Focus on a different customer segment.

If your insurance platform offers detailed coverage explanations, competitors might:
- Copy the feature.
- Improve their own comparison experience.
- Offer human consultations.
- Compete through brand trust or distribution.

## 11.1 Think in terms of incentives and capabilities

To estimate a competitor's likely response, ask:
- What outcome are they trying to achieve?
- Which customers matter most to them?
- What capabilities do they already possess?
- What resources can they deploy?
- Which business constraints limit their choices?
- How quickly can they respond?
- Would the response damage their own economics?

Do not assume that competitors will respond rationally according to your own priorities. Their incentives and constraints may be different.

## 11.2 Build a response matrix

| Your potential move | Possible competitor response | Strategic consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce fees | Price matching or promotions | Could trigger a margin-damaging price war |
| Improve onboarding | Copy the flow | Advantage may depend on continued execution |
| Launch a niche feature | Ignore or replicate it | Customer value and switching barriers matter |
| Sign a distribution partnership | Secure an alternative partner | Distribution access may become strategically important |
| Offer stronger transparency | Improve messaging or disclosures | Trust must be supported by actual performance |

Use this exercise to understand the range of possible outcomes, not to pretend you can predict competitors perfectly.

# 12. Distinguish Sustainable Advantage From Temporary Differentiation

A feature can differentiate a product today without protecting its position tomorrow.

A sustainable competitive advantage generally depends on something more difficult to replicate than a single visible capability.

Potential sources include:

## 12.1 Network effects

The value of the product increases as more participants join.

Examples:
- A marketplace with more buyers and sellers.
- A trading platform with stronger liquidity.
- A professional network with more relevant participants.

Network effects are not automatic. Growth may also increase congestion, fraud, moderation costs, or low-quality participation.

## 12.2 Switching costs

Customers face meaningful costs when moving to another product.

Examples:
- Rebuilding integrations.
- Migrating historical records.
- Retraining teams.
- Recreating workflows.
- Losing accumulated configuration.

Switching costs can improve retention, but deliberately trapping customers can damage trust and create legal or reputational risks.

## 12.3 Proprietary data and learning

A product may improve through unique data generated by its operations.

For example, an insurance platform may learn which plan attributes customers struggle to understand and use that insight to improve comparison experiences.

However, data is not automatically an advantage. Its value depends on quality, relevance, rights to use it, and the ability to turn it into better outcomes.

## 12.4 Brand and trust

Trust can be especially important in financial services.

Customers may prefer a platform they believe will:
- Protect their funds and data.
- Explain fees clearly.
- Handle problems fairly.
- Provide accurate information.
- Honor commitments.

Trust requires sustained operational performance. Marketing alone cannot create a durable trust advantage.

## 12.5 Operational excellence

A company may build an advantage through:
- Faster delivery.
- Better reliability.
- More efficient operations.
- Stronger customer support.
- Better risk management.
- Lower cost to serve.

These advantages can be difficult to copy because they depend on many coordinated capabilities.

## 12.6 Economies of scale

A larger platform may spread fixed costs across more transactions or users.

Examples include:
- Infrastructure.
- Compliance operations.
- Customer acquisition.
- Data and analytics.
- Liquidity provision.

Scale is valuable only if complexity and operating costs do not grow faster than the benefits.

# 13. Competitive Intelligence for AI Products

AI changes competitive dynamics because access to model capabilities may become easier while differences in execution remain important.

## 13.1 Model access is not automatically differentiation

If multiple competitors can access similar foundation models, merely adding a chatbot may provide limited long-term advantage.

Differentiation may instead come from:
- Proprietary workflows.
- High-quality domain data.
- Reliable integrations.
- Customer trust.
- Evaluation quality.
- Distribution.
- Embedded workflows.
- Cost efficiency.
- Superior problem selection.

## 13.2 Watch the full AI value chain

AI products depend on several layers:
- Hardware and compute.
- Foundation models.
- Model-serving infrastructure.
- Data and retrieval.
- Applications and workflows.
- Distribution and customer relationships.

Ask where value is accumulating and where your company has a credible right to compete.

For example, a fintech company may not need to develop its own foundation model. Its competitive advantage may come from integrating reliable account data, understanding financial workflows, enforcing permissions, and presenting information customers trust.

## 13.3 Evaluate competitor AI claims critically

When a competitor announces an AI capability, investigate:
- Is it generally available or only a demo?
- Which customer segment can use it?
- What tasks does it actually support?
- Is performance measured?
- Does it require human review?
- What are the usage limits?
- Is it integrated into core workflows?
- What customer outcomes have been demonstrated?

Public announcements are useful signals, but they do not establish that the product is effective or economically sustainable.

# 14. Build a Competitive Intelligence Dashboard

A dashboard can make research repeatable and help teams notice important changes.

Avoid building a dashboard that collects large amounts of information without influencing decisions.

## 14.1 Suggested dashboard sections

**Market**
- Market growth.
- Segment trends.
- Regulatory changes.
- Customer behavior shifts.

**Competitors**
- Positioning.
- Pricing.
- Major launches.
- Distribution partnerships.
- Strategic changes.

**Customer alternatives**
- Frequently mentioned competitors.
- Reasons for winning or losing deals.
- Switching motivations.
- Repeated complaints.

**Economics**
- Published pricing changes.
- Distribution costs where observable.
- Business-model changes.
- Evidence of new monetization strategies.

**Technology**
- Relevant platform changes.
- Model capability improvements.
- New integration options.
- Changes in infrastructure economics.

**Implications**
- Strategic hypothesis.
- Confidence level.
- Potential opportunity or threat.
- Recommended action.
- Owner and next review date.

## 14.2 Establish a review cadence

A reasonable starting point might be:
- Weekly: important competitor and market signals.
- Monthly: positioning, customer alternatives, and major product changes.
- Quarterly: broader market structure, strategic assumptions, and investment implications.

Adjust the cadence to the speed of the market and the significance of the decisions.

A rapidly changing exchange market may require more frequent monitoring than a mature enterprise software category.

## 14.3 Use confidence labels

For each insight, record whether it is:
- Confirmed by strong evidence.
- Supported by several independent sources.
- Plausible but unverified.
- Speculative.

This prevents weak signals from driving expensive decisions.

# 15. Turn Intelligence Into Product Decisions

Competitive intelligence is useful only when it changes a decision or improves the reasoning behind one.

A practical decision process is:

1. Identify the observed market change.
2. Determine which customers and outcomes are affected.
3. Validate the evidence.
4. Assess the implications for your positioning and economics.
5. Identify strategic options.
6. Compare the cost and expected value of each option.
7. Choose an action.
8. Define how to evaluate the result.

## 15.1 Example: A competitor launches free trading

Your cryptocurrency exchange sees a competitor introduce zero-fee trading for a large set of assets.

A disciplined response might be:

**Observation:** The competitor introduced zero-fee trading for selected assets.

**Customer question:** Are fees the primary reason our target users leave or avoid our platform?

**Business question:** How much revenue do those assets generate, and what is the effect of matching the offer?

**Competitive question:** Does the competitor have lower costs, different monetization, or a temporary promotional objective?

**Options:**
- Match the offer broadly.
- Introduce a targeted promotion.
- Improve pricing transparency.
- Focus on execution quality or reliability.
- Take no immediate action while collecting evidence.

**Decision:** Choose the option that best fits the target segment, economics, and strategic positioning.

The team may still choose to lower fees. The important point is that the decision follows evidence and strategic reasoning rather than imitation.

# 16. Common Competitive Intelligence Mistakes

## Mistake 1: Copying competitor features

A competitor's feature may serve a different segment or business model.

**Better approach:** Understand the customer problem and strategic rationale before copying.

## Mistake 2: Treating a feature checklist as strategy

A long list of features does not explain why customers choose one product.

**Better approach:** Connect capabilities to customer outcomes and differentiation.

## Mistake 3: Assuming public announcements prove success

Announcements may describe planned capabilities rather than demonstrated results.

**Better approach:** Distinguish availability, adoption, quality, and business impact.

## Mistake 4: Ignoring substitutes and the status quo

A product may compete against manual processes rather than another software product.

**Better approach:** Map alternatives from the customer's perspective.

## Mistake 5: Overreacting to competitors

Frequent roadmap changes can destroy focus and create organizational instability.

**Better approach:** Define the signals that justify a strategic response.

## Mistake 6: Confusing market growth with opportunity

A growing market may still be unprofitable or inaccessible to your company.

**Better approach:** Evaluate economics, distribution, regulation, capabilities, and strategic fit.

## Mistake 7: Treating customer complaints as representative

Reviews may overrepresent unusually positive or negative experiences.

**Better approach:** Combine multiple evidence sources and validate patterns with customer research.

## Mistake 8: Ignoring competitor economics

A competitor's pricing may depend on a business model that is not viable for your organization.

**Better approach:** Understand monetization and cost structure before matching prices.

## Mistake 9: Assuming differentiation is permanent

Competitors can copy visible features.

**Better approach:** Invest in capabilities, trust, distribution, data quality, and operations that support continued advantage.

## Mistake 10: Collecting intelligence without acting

Research becomes wasteful when it is not connected to a decision.

**Better approach:** Define the strategic question before starting research and document the resulting action.

# 17. Practical Exercise: Competitive Analysis for an Insurance Platform

## Scenario

You manage an online platform that helps customers compare and purchase individual supplementary health insurance.

Three types of alternatives exist:
- Online insurance marketplaces.
- Traditional brokers.
- Customers contacting insurers directly.

Your platform differentiates itself through clear plan comparisons and guided purchasing.

You need to decide whether to invest next in AI-powered plan explanations, improved acquisition channels, or a simpler purchase journey.

## Task 1: Map the competitive landscape

Identify:
- Direct competitors.
- Indirect competitors.
- Substitutes.
- The status quo.
- Relevant internal or insurer-owned alternatives.

For each category, explain how it addresses the customer's underlying job.

## Task 2: Build competitor profiles

For at least three alternatives, investigate:
- Target segment.
- Positioning.
- Pricing or monetization.
- Distribution.
- Comparison quality.
- Purchase friction.
- Trust signals.
- Customer complaints.
- Important unknowns.

Use available public evidence and label unsupported assumptions.

## Task 3: Identify customer priorities

List the five most important customer outcomes.

Examples:
- Understanding coverage.
- Comparing premiums.
- Avoiding unexpected exclusions.
- Purchasing with confidence.
- Completing the process efficiently.

Explain how you would validate these priorities with actual customers.

## Task 4: Find a differentiation opportunity

Choose one customer need that appears underserved.

Evaluate:
- Customer importance.
- Competitor performance.

# Key Takeaways

1. **Competitive intelligence supports decisions.** Its purpose is to improve strategy, not to collect information for its own sake.

2. **Start with the customer.** Direct competitors, indirect competitors, substitutes, and the status quo all influence customer choices.

3. **Separate facts from hypotheses.** Public announcements and observed features rarely reveal a competitor's complete strategy or actual performance.

4. **Analyze outcomes, not just features.** Customer importance and execution quality determine whether a capability creates value.

5. **Understand market structure.** Distribution, data, trust, regulation, switching costs, and network effects can matter more than feature breadth.

6. **Evaluate market attractiveness relative to your capabilities.** A large market is not automatically a good opportunity.

7. **Monitor meaningful signals.** Pricing, distribution, technology, regulation, and customer behavior can change competitive dynamics.

8. **Anticipate competitor responses.** Strategic moves should be evaluated in the context of incentives, capabilities, and economics.

9. **Distinguish temporary differentiation from sustainable advantage.** Visible features are often easier to copy than integrated capabilities and trusted customer relationships.

10. **Avoid reactive roadmaps.** Respond to competitor moves when they change customer value, strategic positioning, or business economics.

11. **Use evidence ethically.** Reliable intelligence comes from appropriate sources and disciplined interpretation.

12. **Translate insights into choices.** Every major competitive analysis should lead to a recommendation, an experiment, or a deliberate decision not to act.
