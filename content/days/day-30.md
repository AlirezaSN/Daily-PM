# Introduction: Are We Building the Right Thing?

Imagine your team is extremely efficient.

Engineering ships features quickly.

Design creates beautiful experiences.

QA finds bugs.

The product launches successfully.

But after launch...

Nobody uses it.

What went wrong?

The team may have executed the product **very well**.

But they may have solved the **wrong problem**.

This is one of the central problems Product Discovery tries to solve.

> **Product Discovery helps us understand what we should build before investing heavily in building it.**

---

# Discovery vs Delivery

Product work can broadly be divided into two activities:

```text
Discovery
"What should we build?"

        ↓

Delivery
"How do we build and launch it?"
```

---

# Product Discovery

Discovery focuses on:

- Understanding customers
- Identifying problems
- Finding opportunities
- Testing assumptions
- Exploring solutions
- Reducing uncertainty

---

# Product Delivery

Delivery focuses on:

- Designing the solution
- Building it
- Testing it
- Releasing it
- Monitoring it

---

# Example

Suppose users are abandoning an insurance claim process.

### Discovery

We investigate:

- Why are users abandoning?
- Where do they drop off?
- What are they trying to accomplish?
- What problems do they experience?
- Which problems matter most?

---

### Delivery

Once we understand the problem:

- Design a solution
- Build it
- Test it
- Launch it
- Measure the result

---

# The Double Diamond

A useful way to visualize product discovery is the Double Diamond.

```text
        DISCOVER
           ↓
      ┌─────────┐
      │         │
      │   ↘ ↙   │
      │         │
      └─────────┘
           ↓
         DEFINE
           ↓
      ┌─────────┐
      │         │
      │   ↗ ↖   │
      │         │
      └─────────┘
           ↓
         DELIVER
```

More simply:

```text
Explore broadly
       ↓
Understand the problem
       ↓
Explore solutions
       ↓
Build and launch
```

The important idea is:

> **Diverge before you converge.**

---

# Divergent vs Convergent Thinking

## Divergent Thinking

Generate many possibilities.

Example:

> Why might users abandon claims?

Possible reasons:

- Too many documents
- Confusing instructions
- Technical problems
- Lack of eligibility information
- Long processing time
- Fear of rejection

---

## Convergent Thinking

Narrow down to the most important problem.

Example:

Research shows:

> The biggest problem is that users don't know which documents they need.

Now you can focus.

---

# Why Discovery Matters

Without Discovery, teams can waste resources building:

- Unnecessary features
- Low-value improvements
- Solutions to misunderstood problems
- Features customers don't want
- Features that don't achieve business goals

---

# The Cost of Being Wrong

Consider two scenarios.

## Scenario A

You discover a wrong assumption before development.

Cost:

> 2 days of research.

---

## Scenario B

You discover it after six months of development.

Cost:

> Engineering + Design + QA + Opportunity cost + Customer impact.

Therefore:

> **Discovery is risk reduction.**

---

# The Four Risks of Product Development

A useful framework identifies four major risks.

## 1. Value Risk

> Will customers actually want this?

---

## 2. Usability Risk

> Can customers figure out how to use it?

---

## 3. Feasibility Risk

> Can we technically build it?

---

## 4. Viability Risk

> Does it work for the business?

---

# Example

Suppose you want to build:

> AI-powered insurance claim assessment.

You need to investigate:

### Value

Will users want AI-assisted assessment?

### Usability

Will users understand and trust the result?

### Feasibility

Can the system accurately process the required documents?

### Viability

Does the business and regulatory model allow it?

A strong discovery process investigates all four.

---

# Product Discovery Is About Uncertainty

At the beginning of a product initiative, you often don't know:

- Exactly what users need
- Which problem matters most
- Which solution will work
- How much users will use it
- Whether the technology can support it
- Whether the business case is strong

Discovery reduces these uncertainties.

---

# Discovery Does Not Mean "Do Research Forever"

A common misconception is:

> "We need complete certainty before building."

You never get complete certainty.

The goal is:

> **Reduce the most important risks enough to make a good decision.**

---

# Continuous Discovery

Modern Product Management increasingly treats Discovery as continuous.

Instead of:

```text
Research
   ↓
Build
   ↓
Launch
   ↓
Stop
```

Use:

```text
Discover
   ↓
Build
   ↓
Measure
   ↓
Learn
   ↓
Discover again
```

This creates a continuous learning loop.

---

# Continuous Discovery

A useful mental model:

> **Every product decision is an opportunity to learn.**

You continuously learn about:

- Customers
- Problems
- Solutions
- Behavior
- Market
- Competitors
- Business performance

---

# Problem Discovery

Problem Discovery asks:

> **What problems do customers actually have?**

This is usually where strong product work begins.

---

# Problem vs Solution

Customer:

> "I need an Excel export."

Weak interpretation:

> Build Excel export.

Better interpretation:

> Why do you need an Excel export?

Customer:

> "I need to prepare monthly financial reports."

Now the problem is clearer.

Potential solutions could include:

- Excel export
- Automated reports
- Scheduled reports
- Reporting dashboard

The customer's proposed solution is only one possibility.

---

# The Five Whys

A simple technique for exploring the underlying problem.

Example:

### Customer

> "I need to export my insurance claims."

### Why?

> "I need to send them to finance."

### Why?

> "Finance needs to reconcile payments."

### Why?

> "They need to verify which claims have been reimbursed."

Now the underlying problem is:

> **Payment reconciliation.**

An export might not be the best solution.

---

# Jobs to Be Done

Another useful perspective is:

> What job is the customer trying to accomplish?

Instead of:

> "Users want an export button."

Think:

> "Users want to prepare accurate monthly financial reports."

The job is more stable than the specific feature.

---

# Discovery Questions

Good discovery questions are usually open-ended.

Instead of:

> "Would you use this feature?"

Ask:

> "How do you currently solve this problem?"

---

Instead of:

> "Would automated reports help?"

Ask:

> "Tell me about the last time you prepared this report."

---

Instead of:

> "Do you like this design?"

Ask:

> "What would you expect to happen when you see this screen?"

---

# Why Hypothetical Questions Are Dangerous

Suppose you ask:

> "Would you pay $10 for this feature?"

The customer may say:

> "Yes."

But that doesn't mean they will actually pay.

Behavior is usually stronger evidence than hypothetical opinions.

---

# Stronger Evidence

Compare:

### Weak

> "Would you use this?"

### Better

> "How do you solve this today?"

### Strong

> Observe what they currently do.

### Strongest

> Test actual behavior when possible.

---

# Discovery Research Methods

There are many discovery techniques.

Common ones include:

- User interviews
- Observation
- Surveys
- Customer support analysis
- Analytics
- Usability testing
- Competitor research
- Prototyping
- Experiments
- A/B testing
- Fake-door tests
- Concept testing

Each answers different questions.

---

# User Interviews

Useful for understanding:

- Motivations
- Behaviors
- Problems
- Context
- Frustrations

But remember:

> Interviews tell you what people say.

They don't always predict what people will actually do.

---

# Observation

Observation means watching users perform real tasks.

This can reveal:

- Workarounds
- Confusion
- Hidden behaviors
- Environmental constraints

Sometimes users don't even realize how much effort they're spending on a problem.

---

# Surveys

Useful for:

- Collecting large-scale feedback
- Measuring attitudes
- Segmenting users
- Validating patterns

But surveys are usually weaker for discovering deep problems.

---

# Analytics

Analytics can show:

> What users actually do.

For example:

```text
Signup
100%
 ↓
Profile
85%
 ↓
Verification
62%
 ↓
First transaction
25%
```

Now you know where the biggest drop-off occurs.

But analytics may not tell you **why**.

---

# Combining Quantitative and Qualitative Data

The strongest Discovery often combines:

```text
Quantitative
"What is happening?"

        +

Qualitative
"Why is it happening?"
```

Example:

Analytics:

> 40% of users abandon verification.

Interviews:

> Users don't understand why identity verification is required.

Together:

> You have both the scale and the explanation.

---

# Generative vs Evaluative Research

An important distinction.

## Generative Research

Used to discover:

> Problems, needs, behaviors, opportunities.

Examples:

- Interviews
- Observation
- Diary studies

Question:

> "What problems exist?"

---

## Evaluative Research

Used to evaluate:

> A specific solution or concept.

Examples:

- Usability testing
- Prototype testing
- A/B tests

Question:

> "Does this solution work?"

---

# Example

## Generative

Interview users:

> "Tell me about your last insurance claim."

You discover:

> Users don't understand claim eligibility.

---

## Evaluative

Create a prototype explaining eligibility.

Test it with users.

Question:

> "Can users successfully understand whether they are eligible?"

---

You can think about Discovery as:

```text

      ↓

      ↓

      ↓
Explore Solutions
      ↓
```

# Opportunity Discovery

Not every customer problem is a good product opportunity.

You need to evaluate:

- Frequency
- Severity
- Number of users affected
- Business impact
- Strategic alignment
- Existing alternatives
- Competitive environment

---

# Example

Problem A:

> 1,000 users experience it every month.

Problem B:

> 10 users experience it every day.

Which is more important?

You need more information.

Consider:

- How severe is the problem?
- Who are those users?
- Are they high-value customers?
- How costly is the problem?
- Can we solve it?

Frequency alone is not enough.

---

# Opportunity Solution Tree

One useful framework is the **Opportunity Solution Tree**.

It connects:

```text
Desired Outcome
      ↓
Opportunities
      ↓
Solutions
      ↓
Experiments
```

---

# Example

## Outcome

Increase first-time investment completion.

### Opportunities

- Users don't understand investment options.
- Users don't trust the platform.
- Order flow is confusing.
- Users don't know how much to invest.

### Possible Solutions

For "don't understand options":

- Guided investment flow
- Educational content
- Personalized recommendations

### Experiments

- Prototype test
- Usability test
- Small beta

---

# Why Opportunity Trees Are Useful

They prevent teams from jumping directly from:

> Goal

to:

> Feature.

Instead:

```text
Goal
 ↓
Problem
 ↓
Multiple possible solutions
 ↓
Experiments
```

This keeps teams solution-flexible.

---

# Discovery and Prioritization

Discovery generates opportunities.

Prioritization determines:

> Which opportunities deserve attention?

You can use frameworks from Day 26:

- RICE
- ICE
- Impact vs Effort
- Value vs Risk

But don't prioritize solutions before understanding the problem.

---

# Solution Discovery

Once you understand the problem, explore multiple solutions.

Suppose:

> Users don't understand which investment to choose.

Potential solutions:

1. Educational article
2. Guided questionnaire
3. Personalized recommendations
4. Advisor consultation
5. Simplified product catalog

Don't immediately choose the first idea.

---

# Prototype Before Building

A prototype allows you to test an idea cheaply.

Instead of:

```text
Idea
 ↓
6 months development
 ↓
Launch
```

Try:

```text
Idea
 ↓
Prototype
 ↓
User test
 ↓
Learn
 ↓
Improve
 ↓
Build
```

---

# Prototype Fidelity

Prototypes can be:

### Low Fidelity

- Sketch
- Wireframe
- Paper prototype

Cheap and fast.

---

### Medium Fidelity

- Interactive wireframe
- Basic visual design

---

### High Fidelity

- Almost-real interface
- Functional prototype

More realistic but more expensive.

Use the cheapest prototype that can answer your question.

---

# Discovery Experiments

An experiment is a structured way to test an assumption.

Example assumption:

> Users will complete onboarding more often if we reduce the number of steps.

Experiment:

> Test a simplified onboarding flow with a subset of users.

Metric:

> Completion rate.

Success criterion:

> Completion increases by at least 15%.

---

# Assumption Mapping

Every product idea contains assumptions.

For example:

> AI claims assistant.

Assumptions:

- Users want AI assistance.
- AI can provide accurate answers.
- Users will trust the results.
- Compliance allows it.
- The economics work.

You should identify the riskiest assumptions.

---

# Test the Riskiest Assumption First

Suppose:

> Technical feasibility is highly uncertain.

Don't spend months designing the product first.

Test feasibility early.

Or suppose:

> Users may not trust AI.

Test trust before building the entire system.

---

# Discovery Prioritization

A useful question:

> **What is the most dangerous assumption?**

Then:

> **What is the cheapest way to test it?**

This is a powerful Product Management habit.

---

# Discovery vs Delivery Capacity

A common challenge:

> "We are already busy building features. How can we do Discovery?"

The answer is not necessarily:

> Stop delivery.

Instead, integrate Discovery into the product process.

---

# Dual-Track Product Development

A common model is:

```text
DISCOVERY TRACK
Research
 ↓
Prototype
 ↓
Validate

DELIVERY TRACK
Design
 ↓
Build
 ↓
Test
 ↓
Launch
```

The two tracks run in parallel.

---

# Example

While Engineering is building:

> New onboarding flow

The PM and Designer may already be researching:

> The next activation problem.

This prevents:

> Build → Finish → Start thinking about what's next.

---

# Discovery Doesn't Always Need a Dedicated Team

A Product Manager can incorporate Discovery into normal work:

- Talk to customers regularly
- Review support tickets
- Analyze analytics
- Test prototypes
- Review experiments
- Monitor competitors
- Validate assumptions

Discovery becomes a habit rather than a project.

---

# A Weekly Discovery Habit

For example:

### Monday

Review product metrics.

### Tuesday

Talk to customers.

### Wednesday

Analyze support problems.

### Thursday

Test a prototype.

### Friday

Share insights with the team.

The exact schedule doesn't matter.

The principle does:

> **Keep learning continuously.**

# Common Discovery Mistakes

## Mistake #1: Starting With a Solution

Bad:

> "We need an AI chatbot."

Better:

> "What customer problem are we trying to solve?"

---

# Mistake #2: Asking Leading Questions

Bad:

> "Don't you think this new dashboard is easier?"

Better:

> "What do you think about this experience?"

---

# Mistake #3: Talking Only to Friendly Customers

If you only talk to happy customers, you may miss important problems.

Talk to:

- New users
- Power users
- Churned users
- Frustrated users
- Different customer segments

---

# Mistake #4: Treating Every Customer Request as a Priority

A request is evidence.

It is not automatically a roadmap item.

---

# Mistake #5: Research Without Decisions

Research should help answer decisions.

Don't collect information just because it is interesting.

Ask:

> "What decision will this research help us make?"

---

# Mistake #6: Over-Researching

You don't need perfect certainty.

Once the major risk is sufficiently reduced:

> Move forward and learn from real usage.

---

# Mistake #7: Testing Only the Happy Path

Test:

- Errors
- Edge cases
- Confusion
- Unexpected behavior

---

# Discovery Metrics

Discovery itself can be measured.

Examples:

### Customer Contact

- Customer conversations per week
- Percentage of team participating in research

### Validation

- Number of assumptions tested
- Percentage of ideas validated before development

### Learning

- Problems identified
- Experiments run
- Time from hypothesis to evidence

Don't optimize for the number of interviews.

Optimize for:

> **Decision quality and learning.**

Discovery should produce artifacts such as:

- Problem statements
- Customer insights
- Personas
- Journey maps
- Opportunity maps
- Hypotheses
- Prototypes
- Experiment results
- Product recommendations

But remember:

> The artifact is not the goal.

The goal is:

> **Better product decisions.**

# Product Discovery Framework

When facing a new product opportunity, use this process:

```text
1. Define the desired outcome
            ↓
2. Understand the customer
            ↓
3. Identify problems
            ↓
4. Identify opportunities
            ↓
5. Prioritize opportunities
            ↓
6. Generate multiple solutions
            ↓
7. Identify assumptions
            ↓
8. Test risky assumptions
            ↓
9. Select a solution
            ↓
10. Build and measure
```

# Interview Question

## What is Product Discovery?

### Strong Answer

Product Discovery is the process of understanding customers, identifying important problems and opportunities, exploring potential solutions, and validating key assumptions before investing heavily in development. Its purpose is to reduce value, usability, feasibility, and viability risks and improve the quality of product decisions.

---

# Interview Question 2

## What is the difference between Discovery and Delivery?

### Strong Answer

Discovery focuses on determining what problem we should solve and which solution is most likely to create value. Delivery focuses on building, testing, launching, and operating the chosen solution. Strong product teams run both continuously rather than treating Discovery as a one-time phase before development.

---

# Interview Question 3

## How do you know when Discovery is enough?

### Strong Answer

I don't aim for complete certainty. I aim to reduce the most important risks enough to make a confident decision. Once we understand the problem, have sufficient evidence of customer value, understand major feasibility and viability risks, and have tested the riskiest assumptions, we can move into delivery and continue learning through real-world usage.

# Day 30 Exercise

Choose a real product.

Use the following Discovery exercise.

## Step 1: Define an Outcome

Example:

> Increase successful first-time investment.

---

## Step 2: Identify 5 Customer Problems

Don't propose solutions yet.

For example:

- Users don't understand investment options.
- Users don't trust the platform.
- Users don't know how much to invest.
- Users find the order flow confusing.
- Users are worried about making mistakes.

---

## Step 3: Collect Evidence

For each problem, identify what evidence you would want.

Examples:

- Analytics
- Interviews
- Support tickets
- Surveys
- Observation

---

## Step 4: Prioritize the Problems

Use:

- Frequency
- Severity
- Customer impact
- Business impact
- Strategic alignment

---

## Step 5: Choose One Problem

Write:

> **Problem:** ________

> **Who experiences it:** ________

> **Evidence:** ________

> **Impact:** ________

---

## Step 6: Generate 3–5 Solutions

Do not immediately choose one.

---

## Step 7: Identify Assumptions

For each solution ask:

> What must be true for this to work?

---

## Step 8: Identify the Riskiest Assumption

Ask:

> If this assumption is wrong, does the entire idea fail?

---

## Step 9: Design a Cheap Experiment

Define:

### Hypothesis

> We believe ______.

### Experiment

> We will ______.

### Metric

> We will measure ______.

### Success Criteria

> We will consider this successful if ______.

# Advanced Exercise

Take one feature you currently want to build.

Before building it, answer:

```text
What problem does it solve?
Who has this problem?
How frequently does it occur?
How severe is it?
What evidence do we have?
What assumptions are we making?
What is the riskiest assumption?
How can we test it cheaply?
What alternative solutions exist?
What would make us decide not to build it?
```

This exercise is one of the most valuable habits you can develop as a PM.

---

# PM Reflection

Think about your current product.

Ask:

- Do we spend enough time understanding problems before building solutions?
- Are our roadmap items connected to validated customer problems?
- What assumptions are currently untested?
- Which customer segment do we understand least?
- Are we measuring what customers actually do?
- Do we talk to customers regularly?
- Are we testing risky ideas before investing heavily?
- Are we doing enough Discovery alongside Delivery?

Remember:

> **The goal of Discovery is not to prove that your idea is good.**

The goal is to discover whether the idea is worth pursuing.

---

# Key Takeaways

1. Product Discovery helps teams determine what they should build.
2. Delivery focuses on building and launching the chosen solution.
3. Discovery reduces product risk.
4. The four major risks are value, usability, feasibility, and viability.
5. Start with problems, not solutions.
6. Customer behavior is often stronger evidence than hypothetical opinions.
7. Combine quantitative and qualitative research.
8. Generative research discovers problems; evaluative research tests solutions.
9. Opportunity mapping helps connect outcomes to problems and solutions.
10. Test the riskiest assumptions early.
11. Prototypes allow inexpensive learning before development.
12. Discovery should be continuous.
13. Strong teams run Discovery and Delivery in parallel.
14. The goal of Discovery is better decisions—not more research.
