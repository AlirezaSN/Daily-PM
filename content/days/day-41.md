# Day 41: Product Leadership & Decision-Making

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should understand:

- What product leadership means
- The difference between product management and product leadership
- Why decision-making is one of the most important PM skills
- How to make decisions under uncertainty
- How to distinguish reversible and irreversible decisions
- The concept of one-way and two-way door decisions
- How to use decision frameworks
- The difference between data-driven and data-informed decision-making
- How to make decisions when data is incomplete
- How to handle competing priorities
- How to make difficult product trade-offs
- How to communicate product decisions
- How to gain stakeholder alignment
- How to handle disagreement with senior stakeholders
- How to say "no" effectively
- How to establish decision ownership
- How to build trust as a product leader
- How to develop product judgment

---

# 1. What Is Product Leadership?

Product leadership is the ability to help a team and organization make better product decisions and achieve meaningful outcomes.

It is not simply:

> Having a senior product title.

A PM can demonstrate leadership without being a Head of Product or VP of Product.

Product leadership involves:

- Creating clarity
- Establishing direction
- Making decisions
- Managing trade-offs
- Influencing without authority
- Aligning people
- Taking accountability
- Developing product judgment

A product leader asks:

> "What should we do, why should we do it, and how can we help the organization make the best decision?"

---

# 2. Product Management vs. Product Leadership

Product management focuses heavily on:

- Understanding users
- Defining problems
- Prioritizing opportunities
- Creating solutions
- Managing execution
- Measuring outcomes

Product leadership adds another dimension:

- Creating alignment
- Establishing direction
- Making difficult decisions
- Influencing organizational behavior
- Developing teams
- Managing ambiguity
- Thinking beyond individual features

A useful mental model:

```text
Product Management
        ↓
Manage product problems
        ↓
Product Leadership
        ↓
Create clarity and direction
for people and organization
````

---

# 3. Leadership Doesn't Require Authority

A PM often has responsibility without direct authority.

You may not manage:

* Engineers
* Designers
* Marketing
* Sales
* Operations

Yet you still need to influence their decisions.

This is why product management is often described as:

> **Influence without authority.**

Your influence comes from:

* Expertise
* Context
* Trust
* Communication
* Data
* Reasoning
* Relationships
* Consistency

Not simply from your job title.

---

# 4. Why Decision-Making Matters

PMs make decisions constantly.

Examples:

* Which problem should we solve?
* Which customer segment matters most?
* Should we build this feature?
* Should we launch now?
* Should we delay the release?
* Should we reduce scope?
* Which metric matters most?
* Should we continue an experiment?
* Should we kill a product?
* Should we invest in technical debt?

Some decisions are small.

Others can affect the entire product.

Therefore:

> **Decision quality is one of the most important product management skills.**

---

# 5. Decisions Under Uncertainty

Product decisions are rarely made with complete information.

You may not know:

* Exactly what customers will do
* Whether a feature will succeed
* How competitors will respond
* Whether a market will grow
* Whether an experiment will work
* How large the opportunity really is

Waiting for complete certainty is often impossible.

The PM must ask:

> "Do we have enough information to make a reasonable decision?"

Not:

> "Do we know everything?"

---

# 6. The Cost of Waiting

More information is not always better.

Suppose you have:

> 80% confidence

that a product improvement is valuable.

You could spend another three months researching to reach:

> 90% confidence.

But perhaps the cost of waiting is greater than the benefit of additional certainty.

Decision-making involves balancing:

```text
Information
     +
Confidence
     +
Cost of waiting
     +
Cost of being wrong
```

---

# 7. Reversible vs. Irreversible Decisions

One useful way to think about decisions is:

### Reversible decisions

Can be changed relatively easily.

Examples:

* Button wording
* UI layout
* Small experiment
* Ranking algorithm parameter
* Marketing copy

### Hard-to-reverse decisions

Are expensive or difficult to change.

Examples:

* Major architecture changes
* Entering a new market
* Acquiring another company
* Changing pricing structure
* Closing a product line

The more difficult a decision is to reverse, the more carefully you should evaluate it.

---

# 8. One-Way and Two-Way Doors

A related concept is the **one-way door / two-way door** framework.

### Two-way door

You can make the decision and easily change it later.

Therefore:

> Decide quickly.

### One-way door

The decision is difficult or expensive to reverse.

Therefore:

> Spend more time evaluating it.

The mistake is treating every decision like a one-way door.

This creates:

* Slow execution
* Excessive meetings
* Analysis paralysis
* Bureaucracy

---

# 9. Decision Speed Should Match Decision Risk

A useful model:

```text
Low risk + reversible
        ↓
Decide quickly

High risk + reversible
        ↓
Analyze moderately

Low risk + irreversible
        ↓
Analyze carefully

High risk + irreversible
        ↓
Deep evaluation
```

The goal isn't:

> Always move fast.

The goal is:

> **Move at the appropriate speed for the decision.**

---

# 10. Decision-Making With Incomplete Data

Imagine you are considering a new feature.

You have:

* 20 customer interviews
* Some support tickets
* Competitor evidence
* No large quantitative dataset

Is that enough?

Maybe.

Ask:

* How important is the decision?
* How expensive is the feature?
* What is the downside if we're wrong?
* Can we test it cheaply?
* What information is missing?
* Can we reduce uncertainty with an experiment?

This is better than automatically saying:

> "We don't have enough data."

---

# 11. Data-Driven vs. Data-Informed

These terms are sometimes used interchangeably, but they can represent different mindsets.

### Data-driven

The decision is heavily determined by available data.

Example:

> Conversion dropped 20%, so we immediately change the flow.

### Data-informed

Data is an important input, but not the only input.

You also consider:

* Customer feedback
* Business strategy
* Qualitative research
* Technical constraints
* Market context
* Product judgment

Product management should generally be:

> **Data-informed rather than blindly data-driven.**

---

# 12. Data Has Limitations

Data can be:

* Incomplete
* Biased
* Delayed
* Misinterpreted
* Poorly instrumented
* Correlated rather than causal

For example:

> Users who use Feature A have higher retention.

This doesn't necessarily mean:

> Feature A causes higher retention.

Perhaps highly engaged users are simply more likely to use Feature A.

Always ask:

> "What else could explain this result?"

---

# 13. Correlation vs. Causation

Suppose you observe:

> Users who watch onboarding videos retain 15% more.

Possible explanations:

### A

The videos improve retention.

### B

More motivated users are more likely to watch videos.

### C

Another factor causes both behaviors.

You need experimentation or stronger evidence to establish causality.

This is why PMs should understand the basics of experimentation and analytics.

---

# 14. Product Judgment

Product judgment is the ability to make reasonable decisions when there isn't enough information for a purely analytical answer.

It develops through:

* Experience
* Research
* Customer exposure
* Experimentation
* Pattern recognition
* Understanding business
* Understanding technology
* Learning from failures

Good product judgment does not mean:

> "I trust my intuition."

It means:

> "I can make a reasoned decision despite uncertainty."

---

# 15. Intuition vs. Guessing

These are different.

### Guessing

> "I think users will love this."

No evidence or reasoning.

### Product judgment

> "Based on interviews, usage behavior, competitor research, and the problem's frequency, I believe this is worth testing."

The second is still uncertain, but it is grounded.

---

# 16. A Decision Framework

When facing an important decision, ask:

### 1. What decision are we actually making?

### 2. What outcome are we trying to achieve?

### 3. What information do we have?

### 4. What don't we know?

### 5. What are the options?

### 6. What are the trade-offs?

### 7. What happens if we're wrong?

### 8. Can we test the decision cheaply?

### 9. Is the decision reversible?

### 10. Who owns the decision?

This framework prevents many poorly structured discussions.

---

# 17. Start With the Decision

Meetings often become inefficient because people discuss information without defining the decision.

Weak:

> "Let's talk about the onboarding problem."

Better:

> "We need to decide whether to redesign onboarding this quarter."

Now everyone knows what the conversation needs to accomplish.

---

# 18. Define the Decision Criteria

Suppose you're choosing between two solutions.

Define criteria such as:

* Customer impact
* Business impact
* Strategic alignment
* Development effort
* Technical risk
* Time to market
* Operational complexity

Then evaluate the options against the criteria.

This reduces subjective debate.

---

# 19. Example Decision Matrix

Suppose you have three options:

| Criteria        | Option A | Option B | Option C |
| --------------- | -------: | -------: | -------: |
| Customer impact |     High |   Medium |     High |
| Business impact |     High |   Medium |   Medium |
| Effort          |     High |      Low |   Medium |
| Technical risk  |     High |      Low |   Medium |
| Time to market  |     Slow |     Fast |   Medium |

The best option isn't automatically the one with the highest customer impact.

You need to consider the full trade-off.

---

# 20. Weighted Decision Matrix

For important decisions, you can assign weights.

Example:

| Criteria        | Weight |
| --------------- | -----: |
| Customer impact |    40% |
| Business impact |    30% |
| Effort          |    15% |
| Risk            |    15% |

Then score each option.

This makes assumptions visible.

However:

> A decision matrix is a tool, not a substitute for judgment.

False precision can be dangerous.

---

# 21. Don't Hide Judgment Behind Numbers

Suppose someone says:

> "Option A scored 8.7 and Option B scored 8.5, so A is objectively better."

Not necessarily.

The scores depend on:

* Criteria
* Weights
* Assumptions
* People assigning scores

The value of the framework is that it makes reasoning explicit.

It does not magically make the decision objective.

---

# 22. Prioritization Is a Leadership Activity

Prioritization isn't simply:

> Sorting a backlog.

It involves deciding:

> What should the organization focus on?

This requires balancing:

* Strategy
* Customer value
* Business value
* Risk
* Resources
* Timing

A product leader protects the team's focus.

---

# 23. Competing Priorities

Imagine you have:

### Initiative A

High customer impact.

### Initiative B

High revenue potential.

### Initiative C

Important technical debt.

### Initiative D

Executive request.

You cannot maximize all four simultaneously.

The PM needs to make the trade-off explicit.

For example:

> "We'll prioritize A because it supports our strategic objective and affects our highest-value customer segment. B will follow next quarter. C will be addressed as part of the upcoming platform work."

---

# 24. Saying No

One of the most important PM skills is saying:

> **No.**

But "no" should not mean:

> "I don't like your idea."

It should mean:

> "Given our goals and constraints, this isn't the best use of our resources right now."

A good rejection provides reasoning.

---

# 25. A Good "No"

Instead of:

> "No, we're not doing that."

Say:

> "This is a valuable idea, but our current objective is improving activation. Given our limited engineering capacity, adding this now would delay the onboarding work. Let's capture it and reassess after we evaluate the onboarding results."

This protects the relationship while protecting priorities.

---

# 26. Saying No to Executives

Suppose an executive says:

> "I want this feature next month."

Don't respond defensively.

Clarify:

> "What outcome are we trying to achieve with this feature?"

Then understand:

* Business importance
* Urgency
* Customer impact
* Strategic importance

If it is genuinely important, reprioritize openly.

For example:

> "We can deliver this next month, but we'll need to delay Initiative X by three weeks. Which should take priority?"

This converts a demand into a trade-off.

---

# 27. Escalation

Escalation isn't failure.

Sometimes a decision genuinely requires someone with greater authority.

Escalate when:

* Teams cannot agree
* There is major business risk
* Resources conflict
* Strategic priorities conflict
* The decision exceeds your authority

Good escalation includes:

1. The decision
2. Context
3. Options
4. Trade-offs
5. Your recommendation

Don't simply say:

> "We have a problem. What should we do?"

---

# 28. Recommendation Before Escalation

When escalating, try:

> "We have two options. I recommend A because it provides greater customer impact with lower execution risk. The trade-off is that Initiative B would move by two weeks."

This demonstrates ownership.

You're asking for a decision, not outsourcing your responsibility.

---

# 29. Decision Ownership

Every important decision should have a clear owner.

Without ownership, teams often experience:

```text
PM → "Engineering should decide."
Engineering → "Product should decide."
Design → "We need stakeholder approval."
Stakeholder → "The team should decide."
```

Result:

> Nobody decides.

A clear decision owner prevents this.

---

# 30. Responsibility vs. Decision Ownership

Being responsible for execution doesn't mean owning every decision.

For example:

### PM

May own:

* Product priority
* Customer outcome
* Scope trade-offs

### Designer

May own:

* Interaction design
* Visual design decisions

### Engineer

May own:

* Technical implementation
* Architecture

### Leadership

May own:

* Major strategic investments
* Company-level priorities

The exact boundaries vary by organization.

---

# 31. Consensus vs. Alignment

Teams sometimes try to achieve complete consensus.

That can be expensive.

**Consensus** means:

> Everyone agrees with the decision.

**Alignment** means:

> People understand the decision, even if they would have chosen differently, and commit to moving forward.

You often don't need unanimous agreement.

You need:

> Enough understanding and commitment to execute.

---

# 32. Disagreement Is Healthy

A strong team allows people to say:

> "I disagree."

The problem is not disagreement.

The problem is:

> Disagreement without resolution.

A healthy process is:

```text
Debate
  ↓
Evidence
  ↓
Decision
  ↓
Commit
  ↓
Execute
  ↓
Review results
```

Once a decision is made, the team should move forward.

---

# 33. "Disagree and Commit"

Sometimes a team member may disagree but still support the decision.

For example:

> "I still think Option B is better, but I understand the reasoning and I'll support Option A."

This is valuable.

It prevents endless debate.

However, it should not be used to silence legitimate concerns.

---

# 34. Decision Documentation

Important decisions should be documented.

A simple decision record can contain:

```text
Decision:
What did we decide?

Context:
Why was the decision necessary?

Options:
What alternatives did we consider?

Decision:
What did we choose?

Reason:
Why?

Trade-offs:
What are we giving up?

Owner:
Who made the decision?

Date:
When?

Review:
When should we revisit it?
```

This prevents repeated debates.

---

# 35. Decision Logs

A decision log is particularly useful for:

* Major product decisions
* Strategic choices
* Pricing changes
* Architecture decisions
* Major scope changes
* Launch decisions

It helps teams remember:

> Why did we make this decision?

rather than only:

> What did we decide?

---

# 36. Avoid Decision Reversal Without New Information

Changing a decision isn't inherently bad.

But ask:

> "What new information caused us to reconsider?"

If there is no new information, constantly reopening decisions creates instability.

A useful principle:

> **Change decisions when the evidence or context changes, not simply because someone keeps asking.**

---

# 37. Product Leadership and Strategy

Leadership requires connecting daily decisions to strategy.

Suppose your strategy is:

> Become the easiest trading platform for beginners.

Then decisions should support that direction.

For example:

### Priority

Simplify onboarding.

### Priority

Improve educational content.

### Lower priority

Advanced trading tools for expert traders.

The strategy helps answer:

> "What should we not do?"

---

# 38. Strategic Consistency

Imagine a company says:

> "We focus on simplicity."

But then the product team continuously adds:

* Complex dashboards
* Advanced indicators
* Dozens of settings
* Complex workflows

There is a strategy-execution mismatch.

A product leader identifies these inconsistencies.

---

# 39. Leadership Through Focus

Teams often have too many priorities.

For example:

> "This quarter we need to improve onboarding, retention, monetization, payments, search, performance, AI, and social features."

That's not meaningful prioritization.

A stronger approach:

> "Our primary objective this quarter is improving activation."

Then other initiatives are evaluated against that objective.

Focus is a leadership responsibility.

---

# 40. Creating Clarity

One of the highest-value things a PM can do is turn ambiguity into clarity.

For example:

### Ambiguous

> "Improve customer experience."

### Clearer

> "Increase successful onboarding completion from 62% to 75% for new users."

Now the team understands:

* What?
* Who?
* How much?
* By when?

Clarity enables action.

---

# 41. Accountability

Leadership requires accountability.

If an initiative fails, avoid:

> "Engineering didn't build it correctly."

or:

> "Marketing didn't promote it."

Instead ask:

> "What did we learn, and what could we have done differently?"

Accountability doesn't mean taking blame for everything.

It means:

> Taking ownership of the outcome and learning from it.

---

# 42. Product Leader vs. Feature Manager

A feature manager asks:

> "Did we ship the feature?"

A product leader asks:

> "Did the feature create the intended outcome?"

A feature manager asks:

> "When can engineering finish?"

A product leader asks:

> "What is the most valuable scope we can deliver within the constraint?"

A feature manager asks:

> "What did stakeholders request?"

A product leader asks:

> "Which customer and business problems deserve investment?"

---

# 43. Trust

Product leadership depends heavily on trust.

People need to believe that:

* You understand the problem
* You listen
* You are honest about uncertainty
* You don't hide information
* You make decisions consistently
* You respect expertise
* You take responsibility

Trust takes time to build.

It can disappear quickly.

---

# 44. How to Build Trust

### Be transparent

Say:

> "We don't know yet."

### Keep commitments

If you promise an update Friday, provide it.

### Explain decisions

Don't just announce decisions.

### Admit mistakes

Say:

> "I was wrong about this assumption."

### Give credit

Recognize contributions from others.

### Don't blame

Focus on problems and systems.

---

# 45. Communicating Difficult Decisions

Some decisions will disappoint people.

Examples:

* Cancelling a project
* Delaying a launch
* Reducing scope
* Rejecting an executive request
* Removing a feature
* Changing strategy

Don't hide behind vague language.

Be clear:

> "We are stopping the project because adoption remained below the threshold we established, and the opportunity cost is now too high."

Then explain what happens next.

---

# 46. Product Leadership During Failure

Suppose a major launch fails.

A weak response:

> "The users didn't understand the feature."

A stronger response:

> "Our hypothesis was incorrect. We expected the feature to increase activation, but activation did not improve. We need to understand why before investing further."

This creates a learning culture.

---

# 47. Psychological Safety and Leadership

Teams need to feel safe challenging product decisions.

A PM should encourage:

> "What could we be missing?"

> "What assumption might be wrong?"

> "What would make this fail?"

These questions are more valuable than:

> "Does everyone agree?"

---

# 48. Pre-Mortems

A useful technique is a **pre-mortem**.

Before launching, imagine:

> "The project failed."

Then ask:

> "Why did it fail?"

Possible answers:

* Users didn't understand the feature.
* Performance was poor.
* Adoption was low.
* A dependency failed.
* We underestimated engineering complexity.
* The market changed.

This helps identify risks before they become problems.

---

# 49. Expected Value

For uncertain decisions, you can think about expected value.

Simplified:

```text
Expected Value =
Probability of outcome × Value of outcome
```

Suppose:

* 40% chance of success
* Success creates $1M value

Expected value:

> 0.4 × $1M = $400K

This is not a perfect prediction.

It is a way to reason about uncertainty.

---

# 50. Cost of Failure

Probability alone isn't enough.

Consider:

```text
Decision A
90% chance of success
10% chance of catastrophic failure

Decision B
70% chance of success
30% chance of small failure
```

Decision B might be preferable despite having a lower success probability.

You need to consider:

* Probability
* Impact
* Reversibility
* Cost of failure

---

# 51. Risk Matrix

A simple risk matrix can help:

|                  | Low Impact   | High Impact   |
| ---------------- | ------------ | ------------- |
| Low Probability  | Low priority | Monitor       |
| High Probability | Manage       | High priority |

For example:

> A small UI bug affecting 1% of users

is very different from:

> A payment bug that could incorrectly charge customers.

The second requires much more attention.

---

# 52. Decision Quality vs. Decision Outcome

This is an important distinction.

A good decision can produce a bad outcome.

A bad decision can sometimes produce a good outcome.

Example:

> You launch a well-researched product with a strong hypothesis, but market conditions unexpectedly change.

The outcome is bad.

That doesn't necessarily mean the decision process was bad.

Evaluate:

> **Was the decision reasonable given the information available at the time?**

---

# 53. Avoid Hindsight Bias

After an event happens, people often say:

> "It was obvious this would fail."

But it may not have been obvious beforehand.

Good product organizations evaluate decisions based on:

> What was reasonably knowable at the time?

not simply:

> What we know now.

---

# 54. Learning From Decisions

After an important decision, ask:

* What happened?
* What did we expect?
* What was different?
* Which assumption was wrong?
* What did we learn?
* Would we make the same decision again?

This improves product judgment over time.

---

# 55. Decision-Making Framework

When facing an important product decision, use this process:

```text
1. Define the decision
        ↓
2. Define desired outcome
        ↓
3. Gather relevant evidence
        ↓
4. Identify unknowns
        ↓
5. Generate options
        ↓
6. Evaluate trade-offs
        ↓
7. Assess risk
        ↓
8. Determine reversibility
        ↓
9. Make recommendation
        ↓
10. Decide
        ↓
11. Communicate
        ↓
12. Execute
        ↓
13. Measure
        ↓
14. Learn
```

This turns decision-making into a repeatable process.

---

# 56. Example: Should We Build an AI Support Assistant?

Imagine a fintech company is considering an AI assistant for customer support.

Possible benefits:

* Lower support costs
* Faster answers
* 24/7 availability
* Better customer experience

Possible risks:

* Incorrect answers
* Compliance problems
* Customer trust issues
* Data privacy risks
* Poor escalation handling

A weak decision:

> "AI is popular, so we should build it."

A stronger decision:

### Desired outcome

Reduce support response time while maintaining answer quality.

### Unknowns

* How accurately can the system answer?
* Which questions are suitable?
* How often will human escalation be required?

### Experiment

Start with:

> FAQ questions only.

### Guardrails

Monitor:

* Answer accuracy
* Escalation rate
* Customer satisfaction
* Incorrect-answer rate

This reduces uncertainty before major investment.

---

# 57. Leadership in Resource Constraints

Resources are always limited.

You may have:

* Limited engineers
* Limited budget
* Limited time
* Limited data

Leadership means making choices.

For example:

> We have capacity for only three major initiatives.

You identify:

1. Highest strategic value
2. Highest customer impact
3. Highest business impact

Then consciously deprioritize the rest.

---

# 58. The Opportunity Cost of Every Decision

When you choose:

> Initiative A

you are implicitly choosing:

> Not Initiative B.

This is opportunity cost.

PMs should communicate it.

For example:

> "If we allocate the team to this project, we won't be able to deliver the retention initiative this quarter."

This makes resource allocation more transparent.

---

# 59. The Leadership Habit of Asking "Why?"

When someone proposes a solution:

> "We need a new dashboard."

Ask:

> "Why?"

Maybe:

> "Managers can't understand team performance."

Ask:

> "Why is that important?"

Maybe:

> "They are spending hours manually compiling reports."

Now you may discover the actual opportunity:

> Automate reporting.

The original solution may not be the best solution.

---

# 60. Final Mental Model

Think of product leadership as:

```text
        Product Leadership
                |
      +---------+---------+
      |         |         |
   Clarity   Decisions  Focus
      |         |         |
      +---------+---------+
                |
           Alignment
                |
           Execution
                |
            Outcomes
                |
            Learning
```

The strongest product leaders:

* Create clarity
* Make decisions
* Manage trade-offs
* Protect focus
* Influence without authority
* Build trust
* Take accountability
* Learn from outcomes

They don't need to have all the answers.

They need to help the organization make **better decisions more consistently**.

# Key Takeaways

1. Product leadership is about creating direction, clarity, and better decisions.
2. Leadership does not require formal authority.
3. PMs influence cross-functional teams through context, trust, reasoning, and communication.
4. Product decisions are usually made under uncertainty.
5. You rarely need complete information to make a decision.
6. The cost of waiting should be considered alongside the value of additional information.
7. Reversible decisions can generally be made faster.
8. Difficult-to-reverse decisions deserve more careful analysis.
9. Not every decision needs consensus.
10. Alignment is often more important than unanimous agreement.
11. Data should inform decisions rather than blindly determine them.
12. Correlation does not necessarily mean causation.
13. Product judgment helps PMs make decisions when data is incomplete.
14. Decision frameworks make assumptions and trade-offs visible.
15. Decision matrices are tools for reasoning, not substitutes for judgment.
16. Prioritization is a leadership responsibility.
17. Saying no is necessary when resources are limited.
18. A good "no" explains the trade-off rather than simply rejecting an idea.
19. Escalation should include context, options, trade-offs, and a recommendation.
20. Every important decision should have a clear owner.
21. Decision logs help teams remember why decisions were made.
22. Decisions should be revisited when new information or circumstances justify it.
23. Strategy provides a framework for making daily product decisions.
24. Focus is one of the most valuable things a product leader creates.
25. Trust is built through transparency, consistency, accountability, and respect.
26. Good leaders encourage constructive disagreement.
27. Pre-mortems can help identify risks before launching.
28. Decision quality should be evaluated based on the information available at the time.
29. Good outcomes don't always mean good decisions, and bad outcomes don't always mean bad decisions.
30. Strong product leadership ultimately helps teams make better decisions and achieve better outcomes.

# Practical Exercise

Imagine you are the PM of a fintech trading platform.

Your CEO asks:

> "I want us to build a social trading feature where users can follow successful traders and copy their trades."

You have limited engineering capacity.

At the same time, your team is already working on:

* Improving onboarding
* Reducing payment failures
* Improving portfolio analytics

Answer the following:

### 1. What decision are you actually being asked to make?

### 2. What additional information would you want?

### 3. What customer problem might social trading solve?

### 4. What assumptions would you need to validate?

### 5. What are the potential benefits?

### 6. What are the major risks?

### 7. Is this a reversible or difficult-to-reverse decision?

### 8. What would you need to know before committing significant engineering resources?

### 9. Could you test the idea without fully building it?

### 10. How would you prioritize it against the three existing initiatives?

### 11. If you disagree with the CEO, how would you communicate your recommendation?

### 12. If the CEO insists that it must be built, what trade-off would you make explicit?

The goal is not to find one "correct" answer.

The goal is to practice **structured product decision-making under uncertainty and resource constraints**.
