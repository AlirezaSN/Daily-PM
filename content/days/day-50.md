# 1. What Is Go-to-Market?

**Go-to-Market (GTM)** is the strategy a company uses to bring a product to a target market and generate adoption and business value.

A GTM strategy answers questions such as:

* Who are we selling to?
* What problem are we solving?
* Why should customers choose us?
* How will customers discover the product?
* How will they buy it?
* How will we activate and onboard them?
* How will we support adoption?
* How will we measure success?

A simplified GTM model is:

```text
Target Market
      ↓
Customer Problem
      ↓
Value Proposition
      ↓
Positioning & Messaging
      ↓
Distribution
      ↓
Sales / Conversion
      ↓
Activation
      ↓
Adoption
      ↓
Retention & Expansion
```

GTM is therefore much broader than simply launching a product.

---

# 2. Why GTM Matters

A good product can still fail if customers:

* Don't know it exists
* Don't understand its value
* Can't find it
* Can't purchase it
* Don't trust it
* Don't know how to use it
* Don't reach value quickly

Consider two products:

```text
Product A:
Excellent product
Poor distribution
Poor positioning
Poor onboarding
```

and:

```text
Product B:
Good product
Strong positioning
Strong distribution
Effective sales
Excellent onboarding
```

Product B may outperform Product A.

This is why Product Managers need to understand GTM.

---

# 3. GTM Is Not Just Marketing

Marketing is one component of GTM.

A GTM strategy can involve:

```text
Product
Marketing
Sales
Customer Success
Support
Finance
Operations
Partnerships
Engineering
Design
```

For example, launching an enterprise product may require:

* Product readiness
* Pricing
* Security documentation
* Sales training
* Demo environments
* Contracts
* Customer onboarding
* Support processes

Marketing alone cannot solve these requirements.

---

# 4. GTM vs. Product Launch

A **product launch** is an event or coordinated release.

A **GTM strategy** is the broader system used to successfully bring the product to market.

For example:

```text
Product Launch
     ↓
Announcement
     ↓
Campaign
```

versus:

```text
GTM Strategy
├── Target market
├── ICP
├── Positioning
├── Pricing
├── Distribution
├── Sales
├── Marketing
├── Onboarding
├── Support
└── Success metrics
```

A launch can be one component of a GTM strategy.

---

# 5. Start With the Market

Before deciding how to sell a product, understand the market.

Questions include:

* How large is the market?
* Who are the customers?
* What alternatives exist?
* What problems do customers have?
* How urgent are those problems?
* How frequently do they occur?
* How much are customers willing to pay?
* How do customers currently solve the problem?

This connects GTM to the market research concepts you've already learned.

---

# 6. Target Market

The **target market** is the broader group of customers the product is designed to serve.

For example:

> Small and medium-sized businesses that need accounting software.

But this may still be too broad.

You can narrow it:

```text
Businesses
   ↓
SMBs
   ↓
Technology companies
   ↓
20–100 employees
   ↓
Growing rapidly
   ↓
Need multi-currency accounting
```

The more clearly you understand the market, the easier it becomes to build an effective GTM strategy.

---

# 7. Ideal Customer Profile (ICP)

An **Ideal Customer Profile** describes the type of customer that is most likely to gain significant value from the product.

For B2B products, an ICP may include:

* Company size
* Industry
* Geography
* Revenue
* Technology stack
* Business model
* Growth stage
* Existing tools
* Pain points
* Buying triggers

Example:

```text
ICP:

B2B SaaS companies
50–500 employees
Growing >20% annually
Remote workforce
Multiple engineering teams
Existing project-management software
Experiencing collaboration problems
```

The ICP helps the company focus its resources.

---

# 8. ICP vs. Buyer Persona

These concepts are related but different.

### ICP

Describes the ideal **account or customer organization**.

### Buyer Persona

Describes an individual involved in the buying process.

For example:

```text
ICP:
500-person SaaS company
```

Personas:

```text
CTO
Product Manager
Engineering Manager
Procurement Manager
Finance Manager
```

Different people may influence the same purchase.

---

# 9. Buyer Roles

In B2B products, several people may participate in a purchase.

Common roles include:

### User

Actually uses the product.

### Champion

Actively supports the product internally.

### Decision Maker

Has authority to approve the purchase.

### Economic Buyer

Controls the budget.

### Influencer

Influences the decision.

### Procurement

Handles purchasing processes.

### IT / Security

Evaluates technical and security requirements.

A PM should understand these roles because product requirements can differ significantly between them.

---

# 10. Customer Problem

A GTM strategy should be based on a real customer problem.

Weak positioning:

> "Our platform has 50 powerful features."

Stronger positioning:

> "Our platform helps distributed teams coordinate projects without constant status meetings."

The second statement starts with customer value.

---

# 11. Value Proposition

A **value proposition** explains why a customer should choose the product.

A useful structure is:

```text
For [target customer]

Who [customer problem]

Our product is a [category]

That [key benefit]

Unlike [alternative]

We [key differentiator].
```

Example:

> For distributed product teams who struggle with project coordination, our platform provides a centralized workspace that reduces manual status tracking.

The value proposition should be clear before creating detailed marketing messages.

---

# 12. Positioning

**Positioning** describes how you want customers to perceive your product relative to alternatives.

For example:

```text
Competitor A:
Enterprise-focused

Competitor B:
Developer-focused

Competitor C:
Low-cost

Our product:
Simple collaboration for growing teams
```

Positioning answers:

> **Why this product, for this customer, instead of the alternatives?**

---

# 13. Positioning Is Relative

Customers rarely evaluate a product in isolation.

They compare it with:

* Competitors
* Existing tools
* Internal processes
* Doing nothing
* Manual solutions

Therefore, positioning should consider the competitive landscape.

For example:

```text
Customer problem:
Project coordination

Alternatives:
Email
Spreadsheets
Competitor A
Competitor B
Our product
```

Your product needs a compelling reason to be chosen.

---

# 14. Messaging

**Messaging** translates positioning into language customers can understand.

Example:

### Positioning

> Simple project management for growing teams.

### Messaging

> Plan projects, assign work, and keep everyone aligned without complex enterprise software.

### Feature

> Custom project views.

The hierarchy is:

```text
Positioning
    ↓
Value Proposition
    ↓
Messaging
    ↓
Features
```

Start with value, not features.

---

# 15. Distribution

Distribution answers:

> **How will the product reach customers?**

Possible channels include:

* Direct sales
* Self-service
* Product-led growth
* Partnerships
* Marketplaces
* App stores
* SEO
* Content
* Paid advertising
* Events
* Communities
* Resellers

The right channel depends on the product and target market.

---

# 16. Channel Selection

Suppose you have:

```text
Channel A:
Paid advertising

Channel B:
Direct sales

Channel C:
Partnerships

Channel D:
SEO
```

Don't choose based on popularity.

Evaluate:

* Customer behavior
* CAC
* Conversion
* Sales cycle
* Reach
* Scalability
* Competition
* Required capabilities
* Customer trust

A channel should match how customers actually discover and buy products.

---

# 17. B2C vs. B2B GTM

B2C and B2B GTM can be very different.

### B2C

Often involves:

```text
Marketing
 ↓
Acquisition
 ↓
Signup
 ↓
Activation
 ↓
Purchase
```

The buying process may be relatively short.

### B2B

Often involves:

```text
Awareness
 ↓
Lead
 ↓
Qualification
 ↓
Demo
 ↓
Evaluation
 ↓
Security
 ↓
Procurement
 ↓
Contract
 ↓
Implementation
 ↓
Adoption
```

The cycle can be significantly longer.

---

# 18. Sales-Led GTM

In a **sales-led model**, salespeople play a major role in converting customers.

Typical process:

```text
Lead
 ↓
Qualification
 ↓
Discovery
 ↓
Demo
 ↓
Proposal
 ↓
Negotiation
 ↓
Contract
 ↓
Onboarding
```

This is common in enterprise software.

The product still matters, but the sales organization is a major part of the GTM engine.

---

# 19. Product-Led GTM

In a product-led model:

```text
Customer discovers product
        ↓
Signs up
        ↓
Uses product
        ↓
Experiences value
        ↓
Invites others
        ↓
Upgrades
```

The product itself plays a major role in acquisition, activation, conversion, and expansion.

You've already covered Product-Led Growth in previous days; here the focus is how PLG fits into the broader GTM strategy.

---

# 20. Hybrid GTM

Many companies use a combination of models.

For example:

```text
SMB
→ Self-service

Mid-market
→ Product + Sales

Enterprise
→ Sales-led
```

This is often more practical than trying to force one GTM model across all customer segments.

# 21. Pricing and GTM

Pricing is closely connected to GTM.

Customers may perceive the same product differently depending on its pricing model.

Common models include:

* Subscription
* Usage-based
* Per-seat
* Freemium
* Transaction fee
* One-time purchase
* Tiered pricing

You've already covered monetization and pricing in Day 44.

The GTM question is different:

> **Does our pricing model support the way customers discover, evaluate, buy, and expand the product?**

# 23. Customer Acquisition Cost

**CAC** measures how much it costs to acquire a customer.

A simplified formula:

```text
CAC =
Sales & Marketing Costs
----------------------
Number of New Customers
```

Example:

```text
$100,000 spent
1,000 new customers

CAC = $100
```

But CAC should be analyzed by channel and customer segment.

---

# 24. CAC by Channel

Suppose:

```text
SEO:
CAC = $30

Paid Ads:
CAC = $120

Sales:
CAC = $500
```

You shouldn't automatically conclude:

> "SEO is the best."

You also need to consider:

* Customer quality
* Retention
* Revenue
* LTV
* Growth potential
* Scalability

A $500 CAC customer may be more valuable than a $30 CAC customer.

---

# 25. Customer Lifetime Value

**LTV** estimates the value a customer generates over their relationship with the company.

A simplified subscription model:

```text
LTV ≈
Average Revenue per Customer
×
Gross Margin
×
Average Customer Lifetime
```

The relationship between CAC and LTV is important.

For example:

```text
CAC = $100
LTV = $500
```

may be healthier than:

```text
CAC = $30
LTV = $40
```

The exact economics depend on the business model.

# 26. GTM and Product Readiness

Before launching, ask:

```text

□ Is the target customer clear?
□ Is onboarding ready?
□ Is pricing ready?
□ Is documentation ready?
□ Is support ready?
□ Is analytics implemented?
□ Is sales enablement ready?
```

A launch should not happen simply because development is complete.

# 28. GTM Launch Types

Not every launch needs the same level of effort.

### Soft Launch

Release to a small audience.

Useful for learning.

### Beta

Limited customers test the product.

Useful for validation.

### Phased Launch

Release progressively.

Useful for managing risk.

### Full Launch

Broad availability and coordinated GTM activities.

The launch type should match product risk and organizational capacity.

# 30. Objection Handling

Customers may say:

> "We already use another tool."

The response shouldn't simply be:

> "Our product has more features."

Instead:

```text
Customer objection
      ↓
Understand underlying concern
      ↓
Connect to customer problem
      ↓
Explain relevant value
      ↓
Provide evidence
```

Example:

> "We already use spreadsheets."

Possible response:

> "That works well for basic tracking. Teams usually consider our product when multiple people need real-time coordination and ownership tracking."

---

# 31. Customer Proof

GTM becomes stronger when claims are supported by evidence.

Examples:

* Customer testimonials
* Case studies
* Usage statistics
* Reviews
* Benchmarks
* ROI analysis
* Before/after outcomes

For example:

Weak:

> "Our product saves time."

Stronger:

> "Teams using the workflow reduced manual reporting time by 30%."

Evidence improves credibility.

---

# 32. GTM Experimentation

GTM strategies contain assumptions.

Instead of assuming:

> "Customers will buy through direct sales."

Test it.

Instead of assuming:

> "This segment is our best target market."

Validate it.

Potential experiments:

* Landing page test
* Pricing test
* Messaging test
* Sales script test
* Channel test
* Trial length test
* Demo test
* Onboarding test

This connects GTM with the experimentation principles you've already learned.

---

# 33. GTM Hypothesis

A GTM hypothesis can be structured as:

```text
We believe
[customer segment]

has
[problem]

and will respond to
[value proposition]

through
[channel]

because
[evidence].
```

Example:

> We believe growing SaaS companies will adopt our collaboration product through self-service because they already prefer lightweight SaaS tools and can experience value without sales assistance.

Then test the assumption.

# 36. Funnel Leakage

Suppose:

```text
100,000 visitors
 ↓
10,000 signups
 ↓
2,000 activated
 ↓
500 trials
 ↓
100 paying customers
```

Potential questions:

* Why do only 10% sign up?
* Why do only 20% activate?
* Why do only 25% of activated users start a trial?
* Why do only 20% convert to paid?

The GTM team should identify the biggest constraint.

# 37. GTM by Customer Segment

A company may need different GTM strategies for different segments.

For example:

| Segment     | Acquisition          | Sales        | Onboarding |
| ----------- | -------------------- | ------------ | ---------- |
| Individuals | Content / Ads        | Self-service | Automated  |
| SMB         | Product / Partners   | Inside sales | Guided     |
| Enterprise  | Sales / Partnerships | Field sales  | High-touch |

This is one reason segmentation matters.

# 38. GTM and Partnerships

Partnerships can provide:

* Distribution
* Credibility
* Integration
* Customer access
* Co-marketing
* Reseller channels

For example:

```text
Your product
      +
Partner ecosystem
```

# 39. GTM Risks

Common risks include:

### Wrong market

The product targets customers who don't have enough need.

### Wrong positioning

Customers don't understand the value.

### Wrong channel

The company cannot efficiently reach customers.

### Wrong sales motion

The buying process doesn't match customer complexity.

### Poor onboarding

Customers purchase but never reach value.

### Weak economics

Acquisition costs exceed customer value.

### Organizational misalignment

Product, sales, marketing, and customer success communicate different messages.

# 45. GTM Strategy vs. Product Strategy

These are related but different.

### Product strategy

Answers:

> What product should we build and why?

### GTM strategy

Answers:

> How will we bring that product to customers and create adoption?

For example:

```text
Product Strategy
      ↓
Build analytics platform
      ↓
GTM Strategy
      ↓
Target SaaS companies
      ↓
Position around retention insights
      ↓
Sell through content + sales
      ↓
Onboard product teams
```

The two strategies must be aligned.

# 46. GTM and Product Strategy Alignment

A product strategy might prioritize:

> Enterprise customers.

But the GTM strategy uses:

> Self-service acquisition only.

There may be a mismatch.

Enterprise customers may require:

* Sales
* Security review
* Procurement
* Contracts
* High-touch onboarding

Therefore:

```text
Product strategy
        ↕
GTM strategy
        ↕
Business model
```

should reinforce one another.

# 47. GTM and Product-Market Fit

GTM should not compensate for a fundamentally weak product.

If customers repeatedly say:

> "This doesn't solve an important problem."

Increasing advertising won't solve it.

A useful sequence is:

```text
Problem validation
       ↓
Solution validation
       ↓
Product-market fit signals
       ↓
GTM optimization
       ↓
Scale
```

The exact process differs by product and market.

1. **GTM is the strategy for bringing a product to market and generating adoption and business value.**

5. An ICP identifies the customers most likely to gain significant value.

10. Messaging translates positioning into customer-facing communication.

12. Different products require different distribution channels.
13. B2B GTM often involves multiple stakeholders and longer buying cycles.
14. B2C GTM often relies more heavily on scalable acquisition and self-service.
15. Sales-led, product-led, and hybrid GTM models are all valid.
16. Pricing must support the way customers discover and purchase the product.
17. CAC should be evaluated together with customer quality and LTV.
18. Product readiness is an important part of GTM readiness.
19. Sales enablement helps sales teams communicate product value effectively.
20. Customer proof can significantly strengthen GTM.
21. GTM assumptions should be tested rather than treated as facts.
22. Funnel analysis helps identify GTM bottlenecks.
23. Different customer segments may require different GTM strategies.
24. Partnerships can be powerful distribution channels but introduce dependencies.
25. GTM requires strong cross-functional collaboration.

30. A strong GTM strategy cannot permanently compensate for poor product-market fit.
31. The goal of GTM is not simply to generate awareness or sales.
32. The ultimate goal is to connect the right customers with meaningful product value in a repeatable and economically sustainable way.

# Practical Exercise

Imagine you are the Product Manager for a new B2B SaaS product.

The product helps companies manage employee expenses.

Current situation:

```text
Target market:
Small and medium-sized businesses

Product:
Expense management SaaS

Main problem:
Employees submit expenses manually
and finance teams spend significant time
reviewing and reconciling them.

Competitors:
Traditional accounting software
Spreadsheets
Several specialized expense platforms

Pricing:
$10 per employee/month

Current acquisition:
Mostly founder-led sales

Current conversion:
Demo → Paid = 15%

Main customer complaint:
"Implementation takes too long."
```

You need to create a GTM strategy.

Answer the following:

### Market

1. Who is the target market?
2. Would you narrow the market further?
3. Define an ICP.
4. Which customer segment would you target first?
5. Why?

### Customer

6. Who is the user?
7. Who is the buyer?
8. Who is the decision maker?
9. Who might be the champion?
10. What is the main customer problem?
11. How urgent is the problem?
12. What alternatives do customers currently use?

### Positioning

13. Write a value proposition.
14. Write a positioning statement.
15. What would differentiate the product?
16. What messaging would you use?
17. What objections might customers have?

### Distribution

18. Which acquisition channels would you consider?
19. Which channel would you prioritize?
