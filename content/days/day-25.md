# Introduction: From Strategy to Execution

In the previous days, we learned:

```text
Vision
  ↓
Strategy
  ↓
Goals
  ↓
Roadmap
  ↓
Features
```

Today we focus on the **Goals** layer.

A strategy can be excellent, but if nobody knows how to measure progress, it remains abstract.

For example:

> Strategy: Become the easiest investment platform for beginners.

This sounds good.

But how do we know whether we're succeeding?

We need measurable goals.

---

# What Is a Product Goal?

A product goal describes a meaningful outcome the product team wants to achieve.

Examples:

> Increase successful first-time investments.

> Improve customer retention.

> Reduce onboarding friction.

A good product goal focuses on **outcomes**, not activities.

---

# Output vs Outcome

This is one of the most important distinctions in Product Management.

## Output

What the team produces.

Examples:

- Launch a new onboarding flow
- Build an AI assistant
- Release a mobile app
- Add a new dashboard

---

## Outcome

What changes because of the product work.

Examples:

- More users complete onboarding
- More users make their first investment
- Retention increases
- Customer complaints decrease

---

# Why Outcomes Matter

Imagine your team launches:

> 15 new features.

That sounds productive.

But what if:

- Retention decreased
- Revenue decreased
- Customers are unhappy

The team produced many outputs but failed to create outcomes.

A Product Manager should ask:

> **"What changed for the customer or business?"**

---

# What Are OKRs?

OKR stands for:

**Objectives and Key Results**

It is a framework for setting and measuring goals.

```text
Objective
    ↓
Key Results
    ↓
Initiatives
```

---

# Objective

An Objective describes:

> **What meaningful outcome do we want to achieve?**

It should be:

- Clear
- Meaningful
- Ambitious
- Easy to understand

Example:

> Make first-time investing simple and confidence-building.

---

# Key Results

Key Results describe:

> **How will we know whether we achieved the Objective?**

They should be:

- Measurable
- Specific
- Outcome-oriented
- Time-bound

Example:

Objective:

> Make first-time investing simple and confidence-building.

Key Results:

- Increase first-trade completion from 15% to 30%.
- Reduce time-to-first-trade from 3 days to 1 day.
- Increase first-time investor satisfaction from 65% to 80%.

---

# Objective vs Key Result

Think about it this way:

```text
Objective:
Where do we want to go?

Key Results:
How do we know we've arrived?
```

---

# Objective vs Initiative

Another common confusion.

## Objective

The outcome we want.

> Increase first-time investment success.

---

## Initiative

The work we believe will help achieve it.

Examples:

- Redesign onboarding
- Add investment education
- Create guided investment flow

---

# Complete Example

```text
Objective:
Make investing easier for beginners.

Key Result 1:
Increase first-trade completion from 20% to 35%.

Key Result 2:
Reduce average time to first trade from 2 days to 30 minutes.

Key Result 3:
Increase beginner investor satisfaction from 70% to 85%.

Initiatives:
- Redesign onboarding
- Add educational content
- Launch guided investment flow
```

Notice the difference:

**Key Results measure outcomes.**

**Initiatives describe the work.**

---

# Why OKRs Matter for Product Managers

## 1. Create Focus

Teams cannot solve every problem.

OKRs force teams to prioritize.

---

## 2. Align Teams

Product, engineering, design, marketing, and business teams can work toward the same outcomes.

---

## 3. Measure Progress

Instead of:

> "We're working on improving onboarding."

You can say:

> "We're trying to increase onboarding completion from 60% to 80%."

---

## 4. Encourage Outcome Thinking

OKRs shift conversations from:

> "What are we building?"

to:

> "What are we trying to change?"

---

# Writing a Good Objective

A strong Objective should describe a meaningful change.

### Weak

> Improve onboarding.

Too vague.

### Better

> Make onboarding simple and trustworthy for new investors.

Much clearer.

---

# Characteristics of Good Objectives

## 1. Clear

Everyone understands it.

---

## 2. Inspiring

It gives the team a reason to care.

---

## 3. Outcome-Oriented

It describes the desired change.

---

## 4. Strategic

It connects to company strategy.

---

# Writing Good Key Results

A Key Result should have a measurable starting point and target.

A useful format:

> Increase [metric] from [current state] to [target state] by [time].

Example:

> Increase onboarding completion from 60% to 80% by the end of Q2.

---

# Weak vs Strong Key Results

## Weak

> Launch new onboarding.

This is an initiative/output.

---

## Strong

> Increase onboarding completion from 60% to 80%.

This is an outcome.

---

## Weak

> Build an AI assistant.

---

## Strong

> Increase successful first-trade completion from 20% to 30%.

---

# Leading vs Lagging Indicators

Another important concept.

## Lagging Indicator

Measures the final result.

Examples:

- Revenue
- Retention
- Profit

---

## Leading Indicator

An earlier signal that influences the final result.

Examples:

- Onboarding completion
- Feature adoption
- First successful action

---

# Example

Goal:

Increase customer retention.

Lagging metric:

> 30-day retention.

Leading metrics:

- First-week engagement
- Number of successful transactions
- Onboarding completion

PMs should often monitor both.

---

# OKRs and Product Strategy

OKRs should flow from strategy.

Example:

## Vision

> Make financial opportunities accessible to everyone.

↓

## Strategy

> Focus on simplifying investing for beginners.

↓

## Objective

> Make the first investment simple and confidence-building.

↓

## Key Results

- Increase first-trade completion from 20% to 35%.
- Reduce time to first trade from 2 days to 30 minutes.
- Increase beginner satisfaction from 70% to 85%.

↓

## Initiatives

- Simplify onboarding
- Add guided investing
- Improve educational content

---

# OKRs and Roadmaps

Your roadmap should support your OKRs.

Example:

Objective:

> Improve first-time investment success.

Possible roadmap initiatives:

### Q1

Redesign onboarding.

### Q2

Launch guided investment flow.

### Q3

Improve educational recommendations.

But remember:

The roadmap is **not the goal**.

The goal is the outcome.

---

# OKR Scoring

Some organizations score OKRs.

For example:

```text
0.0 – 0.3   Low progress
0.4 – 0.6   Moderate progress
0.7 – 0.9   Strong progress
1.0         Full achievement
```

However, scoring systems vary between companies.

A score of 0.7 does not necessarily mean failure.

For ambitious OKRs, achieving 70–80% may represent excellent progress.

---

# Should Every OKR Be 100% Achievable?

Not necessarily.

Some organizations intentionally set ambitious goals.

The purpose is to encourage teams to think beyond incremental improvements.

However, there is an important distinction:

> **Ambitious does not mean unrealistic.**

A Key Result should still be grounded in evidence.

---

# Common OKR Mistakes

## Mistake #1: Making Everything an Objective

If everything is important, nothing is important.

Keep the number of Objectives limited.

---

## Mistake #2: Using Outputs as Key Results

Bad:

> Launch a new mobile app.

Better:

> Increase mobile activation by 20%.

---

## Mistake #3: Setting Too Many Key Results

Too many KRs create confusion.

Focus on the metrics that matter most.

---

## Mistake #4: Choosing Vanity Metrics

Bad:

> Increase page views by 50%.

If page views don't create value, the metric is meaningless.

---

## Mistake #5: Setting Goals Without Baselines

Bad:

> Increase conversion.

Better:

> Increase conversion from 10% to 15%.

---

## Mistake #6: Creating OKRs Once and Forgetting Them

OKRs should be reviewed regularly.

For example:

- Weekly: monitor progress
- Monthly: discuss trends
- Quarterly: evaluate results

---

# OKRs vs KPIs

These concepts are related but different.

## KPI

A KPI monitors ongoing business or product health.

Example:

> Monthly active users.

---

## OKR

An OKR defines a specific improvement goal.

Example:

Objective:

> Increase product engagement.

Key Result:

> Increase MAU from 500K to 650K.

Think:

```text
KPI = How are we doing?

OKR = What improvement are we trying to achieve?
```

---

# Interview Question

## Question

What makes a good Key Result?

## Strong Answer

A good Key Result is measurable, specific, outcome-oriented, and connected to a meaningful objective. It should have a clear baseline and target so the team can objectively determine whether progress has been made.

---

# Key Takeaways

1. Goals translate strategy into measurable outcomes.
2. OKR stands for Objectives and Key Results.
3. Objectives describe what we want to achieve.
4. Key Results measure whether we achieved it.
5. Initiatives are the work used to influence Key Results.
6. Good KRs measure outcomes, not outputs.
7. OKRs should connect directly to product strategy.
8. KPIs monitor health; OKRs drive improvement.

# Day 25 Exercise

Choose one product:

- LinkedIn
- Spotify
- Uber
- Amazon
- Banking app
- Stock trading platform

Create one complete product OKR.

## Step 1: Start With Strategy

Write one strategic priority.

Example:

> Improve beginner investor activation.

---

## Step 2: Define the Objective

Write one clear, outcome-oriented Objective.

---

## Step 3: Define Three Key Results

For each KR include:

- Metric
- Baseline
- Target
- Time period

Example:

> Increase first-trade completion from 20% to 30% by the end of Q2.

---

## Step 4: Define Initiatives

List 3–5 initiatives that could influence your KRs.

Remember:

> Initiatives are not Key Results.

---

## Step 5: Identify Leading Indicators

For each KR, ask:

> What early signals would tell me we're moving in the right direction?

---

# PM Reflection

Think about your current product.

Ask:

- Do we have clear product goals?
- Are our goals connected to strategy?
- Do we measure outcomes or outputs?
- Can we clearly explain how our roadmap contributes to our goals?
- Are we measuring the impact of our work?

A strong Product Manager can connect:

> **Strategy → Goals → Metrics → Initiatives → Outcomes**

That connection is one of the foundations of effective Product Management.
