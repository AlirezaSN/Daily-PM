# Introduction: Customers Don't Buy Products

A common mistake in Product Management is focusing on what customers buy.

Example:

A company thinks:

> "People buy drills because they need drills."

But customers don't actually want drills.

They want:

> "A hole in the wall."

Even more deeply:

They may want:

> "A beautiful room with pictures on the wall."

The drill is only the solution.

The customer has a job to accomplish.

---

# What Is Jobs To Be Done (JTBD)?

Jobs To Be Done is a framework that helps Product Managers understand:

> The progress a customer is trying to make in a specific situation.

The central idea:

> Customers don't simply buy products. They hire products to help them make progress.

---

# The "Job" Concept

A job is not a task.

A job is the outcome a customer wants to achieve.

Example:

## Task

"Open a spreadsheet."

## Job

"Understand my business performance and make better decisions."

---

Another example:

## Task

"Use a trading app."

## Job

"Invest my money confidently and grow my wealth."

---

# The JTBD Formula

A common way to describe a job:

> When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome].

Example:

> When I receive my salary, I want to invest part of it easily, so I can build long-term wealth.

---

# Why JTBD Matters for Product Managers

JTBD helps answer important questions:

## Why do customers choose our product?

Not:

> "Which feature do they like?"

But:

> "What progress are they trying to achieve?"

---

## Why do customers switch products?

Example:

Users move from one banking app to another.

The reason may not be:

> "The competitor has more features."

The real reason:

> "I want to feel more confident managing my money."

---

## What opportunities exist?

Understanding jobs reveals unmet needs.

---

# Feature Thinking vs Job Thinking

## Feature Thinking

Question:

> "What feature should we build?"

Example:

Users want a dashboard.

---

## Job Thinking

Question:

> "What problem are users trying to solve?"

Example:

Users want to understand their financial situation quickly.

Possible solutions:

- Dashboard
- Weekly reports
- AI insights
- Notifications

The job comes first.

---

# The Three Types of Customer Jobs

Customers usually have three types of jobs:

1. Functional Jobs
2. Emotional Jobs
3. Social Jobs

---

# 1. Functional Jobs

The practical outcome the customer wants.

Examples:

## LinkedIn

Functional job:

> Find relevant career opportunities.

---

## Uber

Functional job:

> Get from one place to another.

---

## Trading Platform

Functional job:

> Buy and sell financial assets.

---

# 2. Emotional Jobs

How customers want to feel.

Examples:

## LinkedIn

Emotional job:

> Feel confident about my career future.

---

## Uber

Emotional job:

> Feel relaxed because transportation is reliable.

---

## Trading Platform

Emotional job:

> Feel confident and in control of my investments.

---

# 3. Social Jobs

How customers want others to see them.

Examples:

## LinkedIn

Social job:

> Be recognized as a professional.

---

## Luxury Products

Social job:

> Show success and status.

---

## Professional Tools

Social job:

> Appear competent and productive.

# The Forces of Progress Model

JTBD explains why customers adopt or abandon products.

Four forces influence decisions:

```
Push of Current Situation
          ↓
    Desire for Change
          ↓
    New Product Solution
          ↓
    Anxiety of Change
```

Let's examine them.

---

# 1. Push of Current Situation

The current problem.

Example:

"My current banking app is too complicated."

---

# 2. Pull of New Solution

The attraction of the new product.

Example:

"This new app looks simpler."

---

# 3. Anxiety of Change

Fear of switching.

Example:

"Will my money be safe?"

---

# 4. Habit of Current Solution

Attachment to the existing product.

Example:

"I already know how my current app works."

---

# Why Customers Don't Always Choose Better Products

Many companies think:

> "If our product is better, customers will switch."

Not always.

Customers also consider:

- Learning effort
- Risk
- Trust
- Existing habits

Understanding these barriers helps Product Managers design better adoption strategies.

---

# JTBD Interview Techniques

Traditional interview question:

> "What features do you want?"

Usually produces weak answers.

Better JTBD questions focus on situations.

---

# Ask About the Trigger Event

Example:

Instead of:

> "Why do you use a trading app?"

Ask:

> "Tell me about the moment you decided to start investing."

---

# Explore the Previous Solution

Ask:

- How did you solve this before?
- What tools did you use?
- What frustrated you?

---

# Understand the Decision Process

Ask:

- What alternatives did you consider?
- Why did you choose this solution?
- What almost stopped you?

---

# Explore Desired Outcomes

Ask:

- What does success look like?
- What would make this experience better?
- What result are you hoping for?

---

# JTBD Interview Example

Product:

Online learning platform

Bad question:

> "Do you like our video courses?"

Better questions:

> "Tell me about the last time you decided to learn a new skill."

Follow-up:

- What motivated you?
- What alternatives did you consider?
- What challenges did you face?
- How did you measure success?

# JTBD and Product Strategy

JTBD helps Product Managers:

## Define Better Problems

Instead of:

> "Users need better search."

Think:

> "Users need to find the right information quickly."

---

## Discover Competition

Competition is not only similar products.

Example:

A fitness app competes with:

- Gym memberships
- YouTube videos
- Doing nothing

because all compete for the same customer job.

---

## Prioritize Features

A feature is valuable when it helps customers complete important jobs.

---

# Common Mistakes

## Mistake #1

Confusing jobs with features.

Example:

"Users need notifications."

Maybe the real job is:

"Users need confidence they won't miss important events."

---

## Mistake #2

Ignoring emotional needs.

People are not purely rational.

---

## Mistake #3

Assuming customers know the solution.

Customers understand problems better than solutions.

---

## Mistake #4

Defining jobs too broadly.

Bad:

"Users want happiness."

Good:

"Users want to confidently manage their investments."

---

## Mistake #5

Using JTBD without research.

JTBD requires understanding real customer experiences.

---

# Interview Question

## Question

What is the main difference between feature-based thinking and JTBD thinking?

## Strong Answer

Feature-based thinking focuses on what customers request or what products provide, while JTBD thinking focuses on the underlying progress customers are trying to achieve. JTBD helps Product Managers identify deeper customer needs and create solutions that better satisfy those needs.

---

# Mini Case Study

You are the Product Manager of a stock trading platform.

## Possible Functional Job

## Possible Social Job

Feel like a knowledgeable investor.

Possible solutions:

- Better charts
- Educational explanations
- Risk indicators
- Investment insights

The feature request is only one possible solution.

---

# Key Takeaways

1. Customers hire products to make progress.
2. Features are solutions; jobs are customer motivations.
3. Customer jobs include functional, emotional, and social dimensions.
4. JTBD helps discover deeper customer needs.
5. Switching decisions depend on motivation, fear, and habits.
6. Great Product Managers solve customer jobs, not just feature requests.
