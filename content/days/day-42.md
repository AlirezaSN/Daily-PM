# 1. What Is Competitive Analysis?

**Competitive analysis** is the systematic process of understanding the companies, products, and alternatives that compete for the same customer need or budget.

It helps answer questions such as:

- Who are our competitors?
- What alternatives do customers have?
- What are competitors good at?
- Where are they weak?
- How do they position themselves?
- How do they make money?
- Why do customers choose them?
- Where can we differentiate?

Competitive analysis is not simply creating a table of features.

The real objective is:

> **Understand the competitive environment so we can make better product and strategic decisions.**

---

# 2. Why Competitive Analysis Matters

Customers rarely evaluate your product in isolation.

They compare it with:

- Other products
- Existing workflows
- Internal tools
- Manual processes
- Doing nothing
- Switching to another provider

For example, if you build a project-management product, your competition isn't only another project-management application.

Customers might use:

- Spreadsheets
- Email
- Messaging applications
- Documents
- Internal software
- Existing project-management tools

Therefore:

> **The customer's alternative is often broader than your direct competitors.**

---

# 3. Direct Competitors

A **direct competitor** offers a similar product to a similar customer segment.

Example:

Suppose you operate an online stock-trading platform.

Direct competitors could include other trading platforms targeting the same market.

They may compete on:

- Fees
- User experience
- Trading tools
- Asset availability
- Reliability
- Customer support

Direct competition is usually the easiest to identify.

---

# 4. Indirect Competitors

An **indirect competitor** solves the same underlying problem through a different approach.

For example:

> Problem: "I want to invest my money."

Possible alternatives include:

- Stock trading platform
- Mutual fund
- Robo-advisor
- Traditional financial advisor
- Bank investment products
- Real estate
- Keeping money in cash

These products may not look similar.

But they compete for the same underlying customer need or financial resources.

---

# 5. The Status Quo Is a Competitor

One of the most important competitors is:

> **Doing nothing.**

Suppose you want customers to adopt a new financial planning product.

The alternatives may include:

- Using your product
- Using a competitor
- Using a spreadsheet
- Asking an advisor
- Doing nothing

If doing nothing is easier, cheaper, or less risky, customers may choose it.

Therefore, competitive analysis should include:

> **The status quo.**

---

# 6. Identify the Real Competitive Set

Don't define competitors only by product category.

Start with:

> **What job is the customer trying to accomplish?**

For example:

### Product category

"Expense management software"

### Customer job

> "Help my company control and understand spending."

Potential alternatives:

- Expense management platforms
- Accounting software
- Corporate cards
- Spreadsheets
- Finance teams doing manual reporting

This creates a more realistic competitive landscape.

---

# 7. Competitive Landscape

A competitive landscape is a structured view of the major alternatives in a market.

You might categorize competitors by:

- Customer segment
- Price
- Product complexity
- Distribution
- Geography
- Business model
- Target use case

For example:

```text
                    High complexity
                         ↑
                         |
            Enterprise  |  Specialized
                         |
                         |
    ---------------------+--------------------→ Price
                         |
                         |
             Simple     |  Consumer
                         |
                         ↓
                    Low complexity
````

The purpose isn't to create a beautiful chart.

The purpose is to understand:

> **Where does each competitor play?**

---

# 8. Competitor Segmentation

You can classify competitors into categories.

### Category 1: Primary competitors

The products customers compare directly with yours.

### Category 2: Secondary competitors

Products solving related problems.

### Category 3: Emerging competitors

New companies or technologies that could become important.

### Category 4: Substitute solutions

Different ways of solving the same customer problem.

### Category 5: Status quo

Doing nothing or continuing the current workflow.

This classification prevents tunnel vision.

---

# 9. Competitor Positioning

**Positioning** describes how a product is perceived relative to alternatives.

For example:

A product might position itself as:

> "The easiest trading platform for beginners."

Another:

> "The professional trading platform for active traders."

Another:

> "The lowest-cost trading platform."

The products may offer similar functionality.

But their positioning is different.

---

# 10. Positioning Is More Than Marketing

Positioning affects product decisions.

If your strategy is:

> "The easiest platform for beginners."

Then you might prioritize:

* Simplicity
* Education
* Guided onboarding
* Clear explanations
* Reduced complexity

You may deprioritize:

* Highly advanced trading tools
* Complex customization
* Professional analytics

Positioning creates strategic focus.

---

# 11. Feature Comparison

A feature comparison can be useful.

Example:

| Feature              | Our Product | Competitor A | Competitor B |
| -------------------- | ----------- | ------------ | ------------ |
| Mobile app           | Yes         | Yes          | Yes          |
| Fractional investing | Yes         | Yes          | No           |
| Advanced charts      | Basic       | Advanced     | Advanced     |
| Social trading       | No          | Yes          | No           |
| Automated investing  | Yes         | No           | Yes          |

But this is only the beginning.

A feature checklist tells you:

> **What exists.**

It doesn't tell you:

> **Why customers choose the product.**

---

# 12. Don't Stop at Feature Comparison

Suppose:

| Feature | Us  | Competitor |
| ------- | --- | ---------- |
| Search  | Yes | Yes        |

This doesn't tell you:

* Which search is faster?
* Which is easier?
* Which supports more use cases?
* Which users prefer?
* Which produces better results?

Feature parity doesn't mean experience parity.

Two products can both have:

> "Search"

while one is dramatically better.

---

# 13. Compare Customer Experience

Competitive analysis should also evaluate:

### Acquisition

How do customers discover the product?

### Signup

How easy is registration?

### Onboarding

How quickly does the user reach value?

### Core workflow

How easy is the main task?

### Support

How quickly can customers get help?

### Pricing

How understandable is pricing?

### Reliability

How dependable is the product?

### Retention

Why do customers continue using it?

The competitive experience is broader than the feature list.

---

# 14. Compare the Complete Customer Journey

Imagine a customer journey:

```text
Discover
   ↓
Evaluate
   ↓
Sign up
   ↓
Onboard
   ↓
First value
   ↓
Repeated use
   ↓
Support
   ↓
Renew / Continue
```

Analyze competitors across the journey.

You might discover:

> Your product has better functionality, but the competitor has a much simpler onboarding process.

That could explain why the competitor grows faster.

---

# 15. Pricing Analysis

Pricing is a major competitive factor.

Compare:

* Subscription price
* Free tier
* Usage-based pricing
* Transaction fees
* Premium features
* Enterprise pricing
* Discounts
* Contract terms

But don't simply ask:

> "Who is cheaper?"

Ask:

> **What value does each competitor provide at that price?**

---

# 16. Pricing and Perceived Value

A competitor charging more may still be more attractive if customers perceive greater value.

For example:

```text
Product A
$10/month
Basic functionality

Product B
$30/month
Advanced automation
Premium support
Analytics
Integrations
```

Product B is more expensive.

But customers may still consider it cheaper relative to the value they receive.

Therefore:

> Price should be analyzed together with value.

---

# 17. Business Model Analysis

Competitors can have different business models.

Examples:

* Subscription
* Transaction fees
* Advertising
* Marketplace commission
* Usage-based pricing
* Freemium
* Licensing
* Enterprise contracts

Business model differences can create strategic advantages.

For example:

> A competitor with a strong subscription model may prioritize retention differently from a company earning primarily from transactions.

---

# 18. Distribution as a Competitive Advantage

Competition isn't only about the product.

Distribution can be a major advantage.

Two companies may have similar products, but one may have:

* Better partnerships
* Stronger brand
* Larger sales organization
* Better SEO
* More app-store visibility
* Stronger community
* Existing customer base

A great product without distribution can lose to a good product with excellent distribution.

---

# 19. Brand as a Competitive Advantage

Brand can influence:

* Trust
* Awareness
* Customer acquisition
* Perceived quality
* Willingness to pay

This is especially important in areas such as:

* Finance
* Healthcare
* Security
* Enterprise software

When customers are taking significant risks, trust becomes part of the product.

---

# 20. Technology as a Competitive Advantage

Technology can provide advantages through:

* Performance
* Reliability
* Scalability
* Automation
* Data processing
* AI capabilities
* Infrastructure
* Security

However:

> Technology is only a competitive advantage if it creates customer or business value that competitors cannot easily replicate.

Having a sophisticated architecture doesn't automatically create differentiation.

---

# 21. Competitive Advantage

A **competitive advantage** is something that allows a company to perform better than competitors in a meaningful and sustainable way.

Potential advantages include:

* Lower cost
* Better customer experience
* Strong brand
* Network effects
* Proprietary technology
* Distribution
* Data advantages
* Switching costs
* Operational excellence
* Unique partnerships

The important word is:

> **Sustainable.**

---

# 22. Competitive Advantage vs. Feature Advantage

Suppose your product launches:

> "AI-powered recommendations."

If competitors can build the same thing within three months, it may be a feature advantage.

It isn't necessarily a durable competitive advantage.

A stronger advantage might be:

> A proprietary dataset that dramatically improves recommendations and is difficult for competitors to reproduce.

The distinction matters.

---

# 23. Competitive Moat

A **competitive moat** is a durable advantage that makes it difficult for competitors to catch up.

Examples can include:

### Network effects

The product becomes more valuable as more users join.

### Switching costs

Customers face significant costs when moving to another provider.

### Brand

Strong trust and recognition.

### Scale

Larger scale creates lower costs or better economics.

### Data

Unique data improves the product.

### Distribution

Strong channels are difficult to replicate.

Not every company has a moat.

And not every feature needs one.

---

# 24. Network Effects

A network effect exists when the value of a product increases as more participants use it.

For example:

```text
More buyers
     ↓
More sellers
     ↓
More transactions
     ↓
More buyers
```

This can create a reinforcing cycle.

Network effects can be powerful because competitors may struggle to replicate the network itself.

---

# 25. Switching Costs

Switching costs make it harder for customers to move to a competitor.

Examples:

* Data migration
* Employee training
* Integrations
* Workflow changes
* Contract commitments
* Historical data
* Customer habits

High switching costs can improve retention.

But excessive switching costs can also create a poor customer experience or regulatory concerns.

---

# 26. Data as an Advantage

Data can become an advantage when:

* It is unique
* It improves the product
* It compounds over time
* Competitors cannot easily acquire equivalent data

For example:

```text
More users
    ↓
More data
    ↓
Better product
    ↓
More users
    ↓
More data
```

However, data alone is not automatically a moat.

It must translate into meaningful product or business value.

---

# 27. Competitive Research

Competitive research can use:

### Public information

* Websites
* Product pages
* Pricing pages
* Documentation
* App stores
* Press releases
* Job postings
* Investor reports

### Customer research

Ask customers:

* What alternatives did you consider?
* Why did you choose us?
* What do you prefer about competitor X?
* Why did you leave competitor Y?

### Product research

Actually use competitors' products when possible.

This is often more informative than reading marketing pages.

---

# 28. Primary vs. Secondary Research

### Primary research

Information you collect directly.

Examples:

* Customer interviews
* User testing
* Competitor product testing
* Sales conversations

### Secondary research

Information collected by others.

Examples:

* Industry reports
* Public reviews
* Analyst reports
* News
* Competitor documentation

Both are useful.

Primary research tends to provide more direct evidence, while secondary research can provide broader context.

---

# 29. Customer Interviews for Competitive Analysis

Ask:

> "What other products did you consider?"

Then:

> "Why did you consider them?"

Then:

> "Why did you choose us?"

Then:

> "What do you think they do better than us?"

This can reveal competitive strengths and weaknesses that aren't visible from feature comparisons.

---

# 30. Win/Loss Analysis

Sales teams often conduct **win/loss analysis**.

### Won deal

Ask:

> Why did the customer choose us?

### Lost deal

Ask:

> Why did the customer choose the competitor?

Patterns may emerge.

For example:

```text
Won because:
- Better integration
- Easier setup

Lost because:
- Higher price
- Missing enterprise feature
```

This information can directly influence product strategy.

---

# 31. Competitive Reviews

Customer reviews can provide useful signals.

Look for recurring themes:

* Complaints
* Missing features
* Performance issues
* Support problems
* Pricing concerns
* Positive experiences

Don't treat individual reviews as absolute truth.

Look for:

> **Patterns.**

---

# 32. Competitive Intelligence

Competitive intelligence is the ongoing process of monitoring the market.

Track:

* New products
* Pricing changes
* New features
* Partnerships
* Hiring
* Acquisitions
* Market expansion
* Positioning changes

The goal isn't to react to every competitor move.

The goal is to understand:

> **How the market is changing.**

---

# 33. Don't Build a Competitor News Feed

A common mistake is spending too much time monitoring competitors.

You don't need to know:

> Every feature every competitor launches.

Instead focus on strategically relevant signals.

For example:

> A major competitor significantly changes its pricing model.

That may deserve attention.

A minor button redesign probably doesn't.

---

# 34. Competitor Response

Imagine you launch a successful feature.

A competitor copies it.

What should you do?

Not automatically:

> "Build another feature."

Instead ask:

* Why was our feature valuable?
* Is the competitor actually threatening us?
* Can we improve our advantage?
* Is this feature becoming table stakes?
* Should we differentiate somewhere else?

Competition often causes feature convergence.

Your strategy should determine your response.

---

# 35. Feature Parity

Sometimes a feature becomes **table stakes**.

Example:

Initially:

> Mobile app notifications

may differentiate a product.

Later:

> Every competitor has notifications.

It is no longer a differentiator.

Now it may simply be:

> Expected functionality.

PMs should recognize the difference between:

### Differentiators

Why customers choose you.

### Table stakes

What customers expect everyone to have.

---

# 36. Don't Chase Every Competitor Feature

Suppose Competitor A launches:

> Social profiles.

Should you build social profiles?

Only if:

* Your customers need it
* It supports your strategy
* It creates meaningful value
* The economics make sense

A competitor's roadmap is not your roadmap.

This is one of the most important principles in competitive strategy:

> **Don't let competitors define your product strategy.**

---

# 37. Jobs-to-be-Done and Competition

Remember JTBD.

Customers hire products to accomplish jobs.

Therefore, competition can be understood as:

> **All alternatives customers might "hire" to accomplish the same job.**

For example:

### Job

> "Help me understand where my money is going."

Alternatives:

* Budgeting app
* Spreadsheet
* Bank dashboard
* Financial advisor
* Manual tracking

This perspective produces a much broader competitive analysis.

---

# 38. SWOT Analysis

A classic framework is **SWOT**.

### Strengths

What are we good at?

### Weaknesses

Where are we behind?

### Opportunities

What external opportunities can we exploit?

### Threats

What external factors could hurt us?

Example:

|          | Positive      | Negative   |
| -------- | ------------- | ---------- |
| Internal | Strengths     | Weaknesses |
| External | Opportunities | Threats    |

SWOT is useful as a starting framework.

It should not be the entire strategy.

---

# 39. Example SWOT

Imagine a fintech investment app.

### Strengths

* Simple UX
* Strong mobile experience
* Low fees

### Weaknesses

* Limited advanced tools
* Smaller product ecosystem

### Opportunities

* Growing beginner investor market
* Financial education

### Threats

* Large established competitors
* Increasing price competition
* Regulatory changes

The next step is:

> What should we do about these insights?

---

# 40. Positioning Maps

A positioning map visualizes how products are perceived across two dimensions.

Example:

```text
                 Advanced
                    ↑
                    |
        Pro X       |       Pro Y
                    |
                    |
Simple ←------------+------------→ Complex
                    |
        Easy A      |       Easy B
                    |
                    ↓
                 Beginner
```

Possible dimensions include:

* Price vs. quality
* Simplicity vs. complexity
* Consumer vs. enterprise
* General-purpose vs. specialized

The dimensions should reflect meaningful customer perceptions.

---

# 41. Positioning Gaps

A positioning map may reveal an underserved space.

For example:

> There are many products for enterprise customers and many products for very small businesses, but few products designed specifically for growing mid-market companies.

This might represent an opportunity.

But be careful:

> An empty space doesn't automatically mean a good opportunity.

There may be a reason nobody occupies it.

---

# 42. Why Are Certain Spaces Empty?

An underserved market may exist because:

* Customers don't care
* Economics are poor
* Acquisition is expensive
* Technology isn't mature
* Regulation prevents entry
* Competitors tried and failed

Therefore:

> **An empty positioning space is a hypothesis, not proof of opportunity.**

You still need validation.

---

# 43. Market Trends

Competitive strategy should consider broader market changes.

Examples:

* AI adoption
* Regulatory changes
* Consumer behavior
* New technologies
* Economic conditions
* New distribution channels

A competitor may not be your biggest threat.

A new technology or behavior change might be.

---

# 44. Disruption

Disruption often happens when a new approach initially serves:

> An underserved or less attractive segment.

Over time, it improves and moves toward the mainstream.

The lesson for PMs is:

> Don't only monitor today's strongest competitors.

Monitor:

* Emerging technologies
* New business models
* New customer behaviors
* New entrants

---

# 45. Competitive Analysis Should Influence Strategy

The purpose of competitive analysis is not:

> "Create a report."

The purpose is:

> **Improve strategic decisions.**

For example, research might reveal:

> Competitors are all competing on advanced features.

You might decide:

> We will differentiate through simplicity and onboarding.

That is a strategic implication.

---

# 46. From Insight to Action

A useful chain is:

```text
Observation
    ↓
Insight
    ↓
Implication
    ↓
Strategic Decision
    ↓
Product Action
```

Example:

### Observation

Competitors have many advanced features.

### Insight

The market increasingly serves experienced users.

### Implication

Beginners may be underserved.

### Strategic decision

Focus on beginner-friendly investing.

### Product action

Invest in onboarding and education.

This is much more valuable than simply documenting competitor features.

---

# 47. Competitive Advantage Should Connect to Customer Value

A useful test:

> "Does this advantage matter to customers?"

Suppose your backend processes requests 20% faster.

If users cannot perceive the difference, it may not create meaningful competitive advantage.

But if it reduces:

> Transaction completion time from 5 seconds to 1 second,

then customers may notice.

Competitive advantage should ultimately create:

> **Customer value and/or business value.**

---

# 48. Sustainable Differentiation

Good differentiation is:

* Valuable
* Relevant
* Noticeable
* Difficult enough to replicate
* Consistent with company capabilities

Weak differentiation:

> "We have blue buttons."

Strong differentiation:

> "We provide the simplest end-to-end investment experience for first-time investors."

The second can influence the entire product.

---

# 49. Differentiation vs. Uniqueness

Something can be unique without being valuable.

For example:

> A product has an unusual interface nobody else uses.

That doesn't mean customers want it.

Therefore:

> **Unique ≠ better.**

Good differentiation means:

> Different in a way customers value.

---

# 50. Common Competitive Analysis Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Only looking at direct competitors

You miss substitutes.

### Mistake 2: Comparing only features

You miss customer experience and business model.

### Mistake 3: Copying competitors

You lose strategic differentiation.

### Mistake 4: Assuming bigger competitor = better

Large companies can have weaknesses.

### Mistake 5: Treating every competitor move as important

You lose focus.

### Mistake 6: Assuming empty market space is an opportunity

The market may be unattractive.

### Mistake 7: Confusing feature advantage with competitive moat

Competitors may easily replicate the feature.

### Mistake 8: Using outdated information

Competitive environments change.

---

# 52. Competitive Analysis Process

A structured process:

```text
1. Define the customer problem
        ↓
2. Identify alternatives
        ↓
3. Segment competitors
        ↓
4. Research competitors
        ↓
5. Compare products and experiences
        ↓
6. Analyze positioning
        ↓
7. Analyze business models
        ↓
8. Identify strengths and weaknesses
        ↓
9. Identify market trends
        ↓
10. Identify strategic implications
        ↓
11. Make product decisions
        ↓
12. Continue monitoring
```

# 53. Example: Food Delivery Product

Imagine you are launching a food delivery application.

Direct competitors:

* Other food delivery apps

Indirect competitors:

* Restaurant delivery
* Restaurant pickup
* Cooking at home
* Meal kits

Status quo:

> Customer continues using existing routine.

You could compare:

* Delivery speed
* Restaurant selection
* Pricing
* Fees
* UX
* Reliability
* Promotions
* Customer support

But the real strategic question is:

> **Why would customers switch to us?**

---

# 54. Finding the Strategic Opportunity

Suppose research shows:

### Competitors

* Large selection
* Strong discounts
* Complex interfaces

### Customer problem

Users in a specific segment care more about:

> Reliable delivery at predictable times.

Potential strategy:

> Build the most reliable scheduled-delivery experience.

Now your differentiation could influence:

* Restaurant selection
* Delivery operations
* UX
* Notifications
* Customer support
* Pricing

This is stronger than:

> "Let's copy competitor features."

---

# 55. Competitor Analysis in Product Discovery

Competitive analysis is also useful during discovery.

Suppose customers report:

> "I can't manage my subscriptions easily."

Before building:

> Subscription management dashboard

you should investigate:

* Existing alternatives
* Competitor solutions
* Why they work or don't work
* Customer expectations
* Gaps in existing products

This can improve solution design.

---

# 56. Competitive Analysis Is Not a One-Time Project

Markets change.

Therefore competitive analysis should be updated when:

* Entering a new market
* Launching a major product
* Changing strategy
* Competitors change positioning
* New technologies emerge
* Pricing changes
* Customer behavior changes

You don't need to update it every week.

But you should avoid treating an old analysis as permanent truth.

---

# 57. Strategic Questions to Ask

When analyzing competitors, ask:

### Customer

* Who chooses them?
* Why?
* What customer segment do they dominate?

### Product

* What do they do better?
* What do we do better?
* What is table stakes?

### Business

* How do they make money?
* What economics support their model?

### Strategy

* What are they likely to do next?
* What market are they trying to win?

### Opportunity

* Where are customers underserved?
* Where can we differentiate?

---

# 58. Competitive Analysis and Product Roadmaps

Competitive research can influence roadmap priorities.

Suppose:

> Competitors have recently made security a major differentiator.

You might decide:

> Increase investment in security UX.

But don't automatically add:

> Every security feature competitors have.

Instead ask:

> What customer problem are these features solving?

Then decide whether your own solution should be different.

---

# 59. The Competitor Trap

One of the biggest strategic traps is:

```text
Competitor launches feature
        ↓
We copy feature
        ↓
Competitor launches another feature
        ↓
We copy again
        ↓
Competitor changes direction
        ↓
We follow again
```

Eventually:

> The competitor controls your roadmap.

A strong product strategy breaks this cycle.

---

# 60. Final Mental Model

Think about competitive analysis as:

```text
                Market
                  |
       +----------+----------+
       |          |          |
   Customers   Competitors  Trends
       |          |          |
       +----------+----------+
                  |
             Insights
                  ↓
              Strategy
                  ↓
             Differentiation
                  ↓
              Roadmap
                  ↓
              Execution
                  ↓
               Outcomes
```

The goal is not to know everything competitors are doing.

The goal is to understand:

> **Where the market is, where customers are going, where competitors are strong, where they are vulnerable, and where your product can create differentiated value.**

1. Competitive analysis helps teams understand the environment in which their product competes.

4. Feature comparison is useful but insufficient.
5. Customer experience, pricing, business model, distribution, and brand can be major competitive factors.
6. Positioning describes how a product is perceived relative to alternatives.
7. Competitive advantage is valuable only when it creates meaningful customer or business value.
8. A competitive moat is a durable advantage that is difficult to replicate.
9. Network effects, switching costs, scale, data, brand, and distribution can create moats.
10. Not every unique feature is a competitive advantage.
11. Data is not automatically a moat; it must translate into meaningful value.
12. Primary research comes directly from customers or product experiences.
13. Secondary research comes from information collected by others.
14. Win/loss analysis can reveal why customers choose or reject your product.
15. Competitive intelligence should focus on strategically meaningful changes.
16. Not every competitor feature deserves a response.
17. Competitor roadmaps should not become your roadmap.
18. Some features become table stakes as markets mature.
19. Positioning maps can reveal potential opportunities, but empty spaces require validation.
20. SWOT can organize competitive thinking but should not replace strategic reasoning.
21. Competitive analysis should lead to strategic implications and decisions.
22. Differentiation means being different in ways customers value.
23. Competitive strategy should consider emerging technologies and changing customer behavior.
24. Sustainable differentiation should align with both customer needs and company capabilities.
25. The goal of competitive analysis is not to create a report; it is to make better product and strategic decisions.

# Practical Exercise

Imagine you are the Product Manager of a new investment platform targeting beginner investors.

You identify three major competitors:

### Competitor A

* Very simple interface
* Strong educational content
* Higher fees
* Primarily targets beginners

### Competitor B

* Very low fees
* Advanced trading tools
* Complex interface
* Primarily targets experienced traders

### Competitor C

* Large ecosystem
* Strong brand
* Many financial products
* Average user experience

Your company has:

* Limited engineering capacity
* A strong mobile team
* No established brand
* Competitive pricing
* Good customer support

Answer the following:

1. Who are your direct competitors?
2. What indirect competitors or substitutes exist?
3. What is the status quo?
4. How would you position each competitor?
5. What are the table-stakes features?
6. What are the potential differentiators?
7. Which competitor appears strongest and why?
8. What weaknesses do the competitors have?
9. What customer segment appears underserved?
10. Where could your product differentiate?
11. What competitive advantage could you potentially build?
12. Which advantages would be difficult for competitors to copy?
13. What additional research would you conduct?
14. What would you ask customers about their alternatives?
15. What would you avoid copying from competitors?
16. What product strategy would you recommend?
17. How would your competitive analysis affect your roadmap?

The goal is to move from:

> **"What are competitors doing?"**

to:

> **"What does the competitive environment imply about our product strategy?"**
