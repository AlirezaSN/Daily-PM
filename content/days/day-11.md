# Day 11: Surveys & Quantitative Research

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should understand:

- What quantitative research is
- When Product Managers should use surveys
- How surveys differ from interviews
- How to design effective survey questions
- Different survey question types
- Sampling and response bias
- How to analyze survey results
- How to combine surveys with qualitative research

---

# Why Quantitative Research Matters

Customer interviews are powerful.

Talking to 15 users may reveal deep insights.

But Product Managers also need to answer questions like:

- How many users have this problem?
- How frequently does this happen?
- Which customer segment experiences it most?
- Is this problem large enough to prioritize?

This is where quantitative research helps.

---

# What Is Quantitative Research?

Quantitative research uses numerical data to understand patterns, behaviors, and trends.

It answers questions like:

- How many?
- How often?
- How much?
- What percentage?

Examples:

- 65% of users abandon onboarding.
- 40% of customers use Feature A weekly.
- Conversion increased by 12%.

---

# Qualitative vs Quantitative Research

| Qualitative Research | Quantitative Research |
|---|---|
| Understands why | Measures what |
| Small samples | Large samples |
| Interviews | Surveys |
| User stories | Numbers |
| Exploration | Validation |

Great Product Managers use both.

---

# The Research Funnel

A common product research approach:

```text
Broad Understanding
        ↓
Quantitative Research
        ↓
Identify Patterns
        ↓
Qualitative Research
        ↓
Understand Reasons
        ↓
Product Decision
```

Example:

Analytics show:

> 50% of users stop during onboarding.

Survey:

> Users report confusion during account setup.

Interview:

> Users explain exactly which step creates frustration.

---

# What Is a Survey?

A survey is a structured set of questions used to collect information from users.

Surveys are useful when you want to:

- Measure customer opinions
- Understand preferences
- Validate assumptions
- Collect feedback from many users

---

# When Should You Use Surveys?

Surveys are useful when:

## You Need Scale

Example:

You interviewed 20 users.

Now you want to know whether the same problem affects thousands.

---

## You Need Quantification

Example:

How many users struggle with onboarding?

---

## You Need Segmentation

Example:

Do new users and experienced users have different problems?

---

# When NOT to Use Surveys

Surveys are not always the right tool.

Avoid surveys when:

## You Don't Understand the Problem Yet

If you know nothing about the customer problem, start with interviews.

---

## You Need Deep Context

A survey answer:

> "Checkout is difficult."

doesn't explain why.

---

## You Need Behavioral Data

Users may report what they think they do, not what they actually do.

---

# Survey Design Principles

A good survey should be:

- Clear
- Short
- Neutral
- Focused
- Easy to answer

Users are giving you their time.

Respect it.

---

# Principle #1: Define Your Objective

Every survey should have one clear goal.

Bad:

> Create a survey to learn everything about users.

Good:

> Understand why users do not complete onboarding.

A focused survey creates better insights.

---

# Principle #2: Ask One Question at a Time

Bad:

> How satisfied are you with our pricing, features, and customer support?

This contains multiple questions.

Better:

- How satisfied are you with our pricing?
- How satisfied are you with our features?
- How satisfied are you with customer support?

---

# Principle #3: Avoid Leading Questions

Leading question:

> How much do you love our new feature?

The question assumes the user loves it.

Better:

> How would you describe your experience with the new feature?

---

# Principle #4: Avoid Assumptions

Bad:

> How often do you use our advanced analytics feature?

Problem:

The user may not know what "advanced analytics" means.

Better:

> Which of these analytics features have you used?

---

# Common Survey Question Types

## 1. Multiple Choice Questions

Users select from predefined options.

Example:

Why did you stop using the app?

- Too expensive
- Difficult to use
- Missing features
- Found another solution

---

## 2. Rating Scale Questions

Measure satisfaction or agreement.

Example:

How satisfied are you with the onboarding experience?

1 - Very dissatisfied

2

3

4

5 - Very satisfied

---

## 3. Likert Scale Questions

Measure agreement.

Example:

"I can easily find what I need in the app."

Strongly disagree → Strongly agree

---

## 4. Ranking Questions

Users prioritize options.

Example:

Rank these factors when choosing a banking app:

- Security
- Speed
- Price
- Features

---

## 5. Open-Ended Questions

Users write their own answers.

Example:

> What is one thing you would improve about this product?

Useful for discovering unexpected insights.

---

# Net Promoter Score (NPS)

NPS is one of the most common product survey metrics.

The question:

> How likely are you to recommend this product to a friend or colleague?

Scale:

0–10

---

## Categories

### Promoters

Score:

9–10

Happy customers who recommend the product.

---

### Passives

Score:

7–8

Satisfied but not enthusiastic.

---

### Detractors

Score:

0–6

Unhappy customers.

---

## Formula

```
NPS = % Promoters - % Detractors
```

Example:

Promoters: 60%

Detractors: 20%

NPS = 40

---

# Customer Satisfaction Score (CSAT)

CSAT measures satisfaction with a specific interaction.

Example:

> How satisfied are you with your support experience?

Often measured after:

- Support tickets
- Purchases
- Feature usage

---

# Customer Effort Score (CES)

Measures how easy or difficult an experience was.

Example:

> How easy was it to complete this task?

Important because reducing effort often improves retention.

---

# Sampling

A survey is only useful if you ask the right people.

Sampling means selecting participants.

---

# Good Sampling

Example:

For a job platform:

Include:

- Active job seekers
- Recruiters
- New users
- Existing users

---

# Poor Sampling

Example:

Only asking employees:

> "Would customers like this feature?"

Employees are not customers.

---

# Survey Biases

## Response Bias

People who respond may be different from those who don't.

Example:

Only very happy or very unhappy users answer.

---

## Social Desirability Bias

Users provide answers they think are acceptable.

Example:

"I always read the privacy policy."

Probably not true.

---

## Question Bias

Poor wording influences answers.

Example:

"How much do you enjoy our amazing new feature?"

---

## Sampling Bias

Your participants do not represent your users.

---

# Analyzing Survey Results

Numbers alone are not insights.

Look for:

## Patterns

Example:

70% of new users struggle with onboarding.

---

## Segments

Example:

Enterprise customers are more satisfied than individual users.

---

## Correlations

Example:

Users who complete profiles have higher retention.

---

# Combining Surveys With Other Data

The strongest insights come from multiple sources.

Example:

## Analytics

Shows:

> Users abandon checkout.

---

## Survey

Shows:

> Users are worried about payment security.

---

## Interview

Reveals:

> Users don't trust the payment page because security information is unclear.

Now you have a complete picture.

# Case Study: LinkedIn

Problem:

Users are not completing their profiles.

Possible research approach:

## Analytics

Measure:

- Profile completion rate
- Drop-off points

---

## Survey

Ask:

> What prevents you from completing your profile?

Possible answers:

- Don't know what to write
- Too time-consuming
- Don't see the value

---

## Interviews

Understand:

- User motivations
- Emotional barriers
- Expectations

---

Possible Solutions:

- Profile templates
- AI suggestions
- Better onboarding

---

# Common Mistakes

## Mistake #1

Creating long surveys.

Long surveys reduce completion rates.

---

## Mistake #2

Using surveys to discover completely unknown problems.

Start with interviews.

---

## Mistake #3

Asking biased questions.

Neutral wording matters.

---

## Mistake #4

Ignoring user segments.

Different groups have different experiences.

---

## Mistake #5

Making decisions from one survey.

Combine multiple sources of evidence.

---

# Interview Question

## Question

When would you use surveys instead of customer interviews?

## Strong Answer

I would use surveys when I need quantitative validation, want to measure the size of a problem, or need feedback from a larger user population. I would use interviews when I need to understand motivations, behaviors, and the reasons behind customer problems.

---

# Mini Case Study

You are the PM for a stock trading platform.

Users complain:

> "The trading experience is confusing."

Design a survey.

Possible questions:

1. How frequently do you trade?
2. Which part of trading is most difficult?
3. How confident are you when placing an order?
4. Which features would help you trade more effectively?
5. How satisfied are you with the current trading experience?

Then combine survey results with:

- Trading funnel analytics
- Customer interviews
- Support tickets

---

# Key Takeaways

1. Quantitative research measures patterns.
2. Surveys help validate problems at scale.
3. Interviews explain why problems exist.
4. Good surveys require clear objectives.
5. Avoid leading questions and bias.
6. Segment users when analyzing results.
7. Combine surveys with other research methods.

# PM Reflection

Think about a recent product decision.

Ask yourself:

- Did we know how many users had this problem?
- Did we measure the size of the opportunity?
