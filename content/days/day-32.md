# Day 32: Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should understand:

* What Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) is
* Why customers "hire" products
* The difference between a job, a need, and a feature
* Functional, emotional, and social jobs
* Job statements
* Job stories
* Customer motivations
* Forces of progress
* Switching behavior
* Outcome statements
* How JTBD differs from personas
* How PMs use JTBD for product discovery
* How JTBD helps identify product opportunities
* Common JTBD mistakes

---

# 1. What Is Jobs-to-be-Done?

**Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)** is a framework for understanding **why customers use or buy a product**.

The fundamental idea is:

> Customers don't simply buy products. They "hire" products to make progress in a particular situation.

For example:

People don't necessarily want a drill.

They want:

> A hole in the wall.

And even that may not be the ultimate goal.

They may actually want:

> To hang a picture.

So the product is being "hired" to accomplish a job.

---

# 2. The Famous Milkshake Example

One of the most famous examples used to explain JTBD comes from research around milkshakes.

Imagine a fast-food company wants to sell more milkshakes.

A traditional approach might ask:

> "What features should we add to the milkshake?"

Maybe:

* More flavors
* Larger size
* Lower price
* Better packaging

But JTBD asks a different question:

> **"What job are customers hiring the milkshake to do?"**

Research might discover that some customers buy milkshakes in the morning because they need something:

* Convenient
* Filling
* Easy to consume while driving
* That lasts for a long time

The milkshake is competing with other solutions that can perform the same job.

For example:

* Coffee
* Donuts
* Bananas
* Breakfast sandwiches

The insight isn't:

> "Customers love milkshakes."

The insight is:

> **"Customers are using milkshakes to make their morning commute more convenient and enjoyable."**

This changes how you think about the product.

---

# 3. The Core Question

The central JTBD question is:

> **"What job is the customer trying to accomplish?"**

Instead of asking:

> "What product does the customer want?"

Ask:

> "What progress does the customer want to make?"

This distinction is extremely important for Product Managers.

---

# 4. Product vs. Job

Consider a navigation app.

A user might say:

> "I need Google Maps."

But that's not the actual job.

The job might be:

> "Get to my destination efficiently without getting lost."

The user doesn't fundamentally care about:

* Maps
* GPS
* Navigation UI
* Route algorithms

Those are solutions.

The underlying job is:

> **Get somewhere successfully.**

---

# 5. Another Example: Netflix

A user might say:

> "I want Netflix."

But what are they actually trying to accomplish?

Possible jobs:

> "Relax after work."

> "Find something entertaining to watch."

> "Spend time with my family."

> "Escape boredom."

Different customers can use the same product for different jobs.

This is one of the most important JTBD insights.

---

# 6. Products Can Perform Multiple Jobs

A single product may be hired for many different jobs.

For example, LinkedIn can be used to:

* Find a job
* Recruit employees
* Build a professional network
* Learn from professionals
* Share professional knowledge
* Build a personal brand
* Research companies
* Find potential customers

Therefore:

> **One product can serve many jobs.**

This is why simply defining "the user" isn't always enough.

---

# 7. Jobs vs. Features

Suppose you are building a trading platform.

A user asks:

> "Can you add stop-loss orders?"

That's a feature request.

The underlying job may be:

> "Protect my investment from unexpected losses."

The feature is one possible solution.

Other solutions might include:

* Alerts
* Automated risk controls
* Trailing stops
* Position sizing guidance
* Portfolio risk limits

A Product Manager should understand the job before deciding on the solution.

---

# 8. Job → Problem → Solution

A useful framework is:

**Job → Problem/Friction → Desired Outcome → Possible Solutions → Feature**

For example:

**Job:** Protect my portfolio from large losses.

**Problem:** I cannot monitor the market continuously.

**Desired Outcome:** Reduce downside risk automatically.

**Possible Solutions:**

* Stop-loss
* Price alerts
* Automated rules
* Risk limits

**Feature:** Stop-loss order

This prevents you from jumping directly from:

> "User requested feature X"

to:

> "Let's build X."

---

# 9. Functional Jobs

The **functional job** is what the customer is practically trying to accomplish.

Examples:

### Banking

> Transfer money to another person.

### Trading

> Buy an investment.

### Insurance

> Submit a reimbursement claim.

### LinkedIn

> Find a suitable job.

### Uber

> Get from location A to location B.

Functional jobs are usually the easiest to identify.

---

# 10. Emotional Jobs

Customers also have emotional goals.

An emotional job describes:

> **How the customer wants to feel.**

For example:

A trading user may want to:

> Feel confident about an investment decision.

An insurance customer may want to:

> Feel secure that unexpected expenses are covered.

A job seeker may want to:

> Feel confident that their application represents them well.

The functional job and emotional job can coexist.

---

# 11. Social Jobs

A **social job** describes how the customer wants to be perceived by others.

For example:

A professional might use LinkedIn to:

> Be perceived as knowledgeable and successful.

A business might use a premium analytics platform to:

> Demonstrate that it uses sophisticated technology.

A customer might buy an expensive watch to:

> Signal status.

Social jobs are especially important for products involving:

* Identity
* Status
* Professional reputation
* Social interaction
* Self-expression

---

# 12. Functional + Emotional + Social

A single customer can have all three.

For example, consider a professional using LinkedIn.

### Functional job

> Find a suitable job.

### Emotional job

> Feel confident about my career prospects.

### Social job

> Be perceived as a strong professional.

This gives the Product Manager a much richer understanding of the customer.

---

# 13. Job Statement

A good job statement describes the customer's desired progress.

A useful structure is:

> **Verb + Object + Context**

Examples:

> Find a suitable job while considering a career change.

> Transfer money to a friend after splitting a bill.

> Compare insurance plans before choosing coverage.

> Buy a stock after researching its potential.

Avoid turning the job into a feature.

**Bad:**

> Use the job-search feature.

**Better:**

> Find a suitable job.

---

# 14. Job Stories

Another useful format is the **Job Story**.

The structure is:

> **When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome].**

For example:

> When I decide to change jobs, I want to quickly identify relevant opportunities, so I can focus my application effort on the best ones.

Another example:

> When I am about to place a large trade, I want to understand the potential risk, so I can make the decision confidently.

This format is particularly useful for Product Managers because it connects:

**Situation → Motivation → Outcome**

---

# 15. User Story vs. Job Story

You may already know the traditional User Story:

> As a user, I want to save a payment method so that checkout is faster.

A Job Story would be:

> When I purchase something repeatedly, I want checkout to be faster, so I can complete the purchase with minimal effort.

Notice the difference.

The User Story describes:

> **Who + What**

The Job Story emphasizes:

> **Situation + Motivation + Outcome**

JTBD focuses heavily on the latter.

---

# 16. Why Situation Matters

The same person can have different jobs in different situations.

For example, imagine a trader.

### Situation A

Market is calm.

Job:

> Research long-term investment opportunities.

### Situation B

Market suddenly crashes.

Job:

> Protect my portfolio from significant losses.

Same user.

Different situation.

Different job.

Therefore:

> **Context matters.**

---

# 17. Job Context

When studying a job, investigate:

* When does it happen?
* Where does it happen?
* What triggers it?
* What does the customer do before it?
* What happens afterward?
* How frequently does it occur?
* How important is it?
* What makes it difficult?
* What alternatives are available?

This helps you understand the job deeply.

---

# 18. The Trigger

A job often begins because something changes.

Examples:

### Job Search

Trigger:

> I decide I need a new job.

### Insurance

Trigger:

> I have an unexpected medical expense.

### Trading

Trigger:

> I decide to invest some money.

### Food Delivery

Trigger:

> I'm hungry but don't want to cook.

The trigger is important because it tells you:

> **When does the customer start looking for a solution?**

---

# 19. Desired Outcome

Customers don't just have jobs.

They also have desired outcomes.

For example:

**Job:**

> Submit an insurance claim.

**Desired outcomes:**

* Complete it quickly
* Know exactly what documents are required
* Know whether the claim will be accepted
* Track its status
* Receive reimbursement quickly

The job is:

> What they're trying to accomplish.

The outcomes are:

> What "better" looks like.

---

# 20. Outcome Statements

A useful outcome statement can describe:

> **Direction + Object + Measurement**

Examples:

> Minimize the time required to complete a claim.

> Minimize the number of errors when submitting documents.

> Minimize uncertainty about claim status.

> Increase confidence when selecting an investment.

You can use these outcomes to discover opportunities.

---

# 21. Functional Outcome vs. Emotional Outcome

### Functional

> Complete my application quickly.

### Emotional

> Feel confident that I completed it correctly.

Both matter.

A product can be functionally efficient but emotionally poor.

For example:

> A trading app may let users place an order in 10 seconds.

But if users don't understand what they're doing, they may still feel anxious.

---

# 22. Competing Solutions

One of the most powerful ideas in JTBD is:

> **Your competitor isn't always another product in your category.**

Customers compare different ways of accomplishing the same job.

Suppose the job is:

> "Stay informed while commuting."

Possible solutions:

* Podcast
* Radio
* YouTube
* Audiobook
* Newsletter
* Social media

These aren't necessarily direct competitors.

But they compete for the same job.

---

# 23. Competing Solutions for Job Search

Job:

> Find a suitable job.

Possible solutions:

* LinkedIn
* Company career pages
* Recruiters
* Job boards
* Personal network
* Referrals
* Professional communities
* Conferences

A Product Manager should understand all of these alternatives.

---

# 24. Why This Matters for Competition

Imagine you manage a food delivery app.

Your direct competitors are:

> Other food delivery apps.

But customers may also "hire":

> Cooking at home.

> Going to a restaurant.

> Eating leftovers.

> Ordering groceries.

The real competitive question becomes:

> **What alternatives can accomplish the same job?**

---

# 25. The Four Forces of Progress

JTBD often uses the concept of **Four Forces** to explain switching behavior.

They are:

1. Push
2. Pull
3. Anxiety
4. Habit

---

# 26. Push

**Push** is dissatisfaction with the current solution.

Example:

> "My current trading platform has very high fees."

This creates pressure to switch.

**Current Product → Dissatisfaction → Push → Consider Alternative**

---

# 27. Pull

**Pull** is attraction toward a new solution.

Example:

> "This platform offers much better portfolio analytics."

The customer is attracted toward the new product.

**New Product → Attractive Benefits → Pull**

---

# 28. Anxiety

Anxiety is uncertainty about switching.

For example:

> "Will I lose my transaction history?"

> "Is my money safe?"

> "Will the new platform be difficult to use?"

Anxiety prevents switching.

---

# 29. Habit

Habit is attachment to the existing solution.

Examples:

> "I've been using this app for five years."

> "I already know how everything works."

> "My friends are already here."

Habit creates resistance to change.

---

# 30. Switching Decision

A simplified model is:

**Push from Current Solution + Pull from New Solution − Anxiety about Switching − Habit of Current Solution = Likelihood of Switching**

This is extremely useful for understanding competitive behavior.

---

# 31. Example: Switching Trading Platforms

Imagine a customer currently uses Platform A.

### Push

> Fees are too high.

### Pull

> Platform B has better analytics.

### Anxiety

> I'm worried about transferring my portfolio.

### Habit

> I've used Platform A for five years.

The PM of Platform B needs to:

* Increase the pull
* Reduce anxiety
* Make switching easier

---

# 32. Switching Costs

Switching isn't always about money.

Switching costs can include:

### Financial

> Transfer fees.

### Time

> Setting everything up again.

### Learning

> Learning a new interface.

### Emotional

> Losing trust or familiarity.

### Data

> Losing history or information.

### Social

> Leaving a network.

Reducing switching costs can be a powerful product strategy.

---

# 33. Example: LinkedIn

Consider the job-search job.

LinkedIn's advantages might include:

* Large professional network
* Job listings
* Professional identity
* Recruiter ecosystem
* Social proof
* Connections
* Recommendations

But there are also switching costs.

A professional may already have:

* Hundreds of connections
* Years of posts
* Recommendations
* Work history
* Professional reputation

This creates a strong network effect and makes LinkedIn difficult to replace.

# 34. JTBD and Personas

JTBD does not necessarily replace personas.

They answer different questions.

### Persona

> **Who is the customer?**

Example:

> Sarah, 32, software engineer, lives in New York.

### JTBD

> **What is the customer trying to accomplish?**

Example:

> Find a better-paying remote job.

Personas focus on characteristics.

JTBD focuses on motivations and desired progress.

---

# 35. Why Demographics Aren't Enough

Suppose two users are:

* 35 years old
* Software engineers
* Living in the same city
* Similar income

But one wants:

> A remote job with higher salary.

The other wants:

> A startup role where they can learn new skills.

Their demographics are similar.

Their jobs are different.

Therefore:

> **Behavior and motivation can be more useful than demographics.**

---

# 36. JTBD and Segmentation

Traditional segmentation might use:

* Age
* Gender
* Location
* Income
* Industry

JTBD-based segmentation can use:

* Job
* Situation
* Motivation
* Desired outcome
* Frequency
* Importance

For example:

Instead of:

> "Users aged 25–35"

you might define:

> "Professionals actively searching for a career transition."

That can be much more actionable.

---

# 37. How to Discover Jobs

The best way to discover jobs is to talk to customers.

Don't start by asking:

> "What features do you want?"

Instead, explore their behavior.

Ask:

> "Tell me about the last time you did this."

Then investigate the sequence.

---

# 38. The "Last Time" Interview Technique

Suppose you're researching investment behavior.

Don't ask:

> "Would you use an investment recommendation feature?"

That's hypothetical.

Instead ask:

> "Tell me about the last time you decided to invest money."

Then ask:

* What triggered the decision?
* What did you do first?
* What information did you look for?
* What alternatives did you consider?
* What was difficult?
* What happened next?
* How did you feel?
* What did you do after completing the investment?

This produces much richer evidence.

---

# 39. Avoid Leading Questions

Bad:

> "Wouldn't it be easier if we added automatic document verification?"

The customer is likely to say:

> "Yes."

But that doesn't prove the problem exists.

Better:

> "Tell me about the last time you submitted documents."

Then:

> "What was difficult?"

Then:

> "How did you solve it?"

Let the customer describe the experience.

---

# 40. Ask About Behavior, Not Intentions

Bad:

> "Would you use this feature?"

Better:

> "How do you currently solve this problem?"

Why?

Because people are often bad at predicting their future behavior.

Actual behavior is stronger evidence than hypothetical opinions.

---

# 41. The Timeline of a Job

When researching a job, you can map:

**Trigger → Search → Evaluate → Choose → Execute → Evaluate Result**

For example:

**Need a new job → Search LinkedIn → Compare opportunities → Choose jobs to apply for → Submit applications → Wait for response → Evaluate opportunities**

This gives you a complete picture.

---

# 42. Job Dimensions

A job can be described using several dimensions:

### Frequency

How often does it happen?

### Importance

How important is it?

### Difficulty

How difficult is it?

### Satisfaction

How satisfied are customers with current solutions?

### Urgency

How urgently does the customer need a solution?

These dimensions help identify attractive opportunities.

---

# 43. Opportunity Example

Imagine users frequently need to:

> Upload documents.

Suppose:

* Frequency = High
* Importance = High
* Current satisfaction = Low

That's potentially a strong product opportunity.

Compare it with:

> Change profile picture.

Maybe:

* Frequency = Low
* Importance = Low
* Satisfaction = High

Much less important.

---

# 44. Opportunity Scoring

A simple framework:

**Opportunity Score = Importance + Frequency + Pain − Current Satisfaction**

This isn't a universal formula.

The point is to systematically compare opportunities.

More sophisticated frameworks can use normalized scores and weighting.

---

# 45. JTBD in Product Discovery

A typical discovery process might look like:

**Observe Customers → Understand Jobs → Identify Pain Points → Define Desired Outcomes → Identify Opportunities → Generate Solutions → Test Solutions → Build → Measure Outcomes**

JTBD is especially useful in the early stages.

# 46. JTBD in Product Strategy

JTBD can also influence strategy.

Suppose your company thinks:

> "We are a stock trading app."

JTBD may reveal:

> Customers actually hire us to help them make confident investment decisions.

That changes your strategic thinking.

Instead of competing only on:

> Trading execution.

You might compete on:

> **Investment confidence and decision-making.**

That could influence:

* Product roadmap
* Features
* Positioning
* Marketing
* Customer segmentation
* Competitive strategy

# 47. JTBD and Innovation

JTBD can reveal opportunities outside your current product.

Suppose customers have the job:

> "Prepare for retirement."

Your company might currently provide:

> Stock trading.

But the broader job could involve:

* Portfolio management
* Retirement planning
* Tax optimization
* Risk management
* Financial education

The job can reveal opportunities beyond the current product category.

---

# 48. The Danger of Over-Expanding the Job

However, be careful.

If you define the job too broadly:

> "Help customers become financially successful."

It becomes difficult to use.

A useful job should be:

* Specific enough to understand
* Broad enough to avoid prescribing a solution
* Relevant to customer behavior
* Actionable for product decisions

---

# 49. Job vs. Outcome vs. Feature

This distinction is critical.

### Job

> Find a suitable job.

### Desired Outcome

> Minimize the time required to identify relevant jobs.

### Solution

> Personalized job recommendations.

### Feature

> "Jobs you may be interested in" section.

Think in this order:

**Job → Desired Outcome → Opportunity → Solution → Feature**

# 50. Common JTBD Mistakes

## Mistake 1: Defining a Feature as the Job

Bad:

> "The job is to use personalized recommendations."

That's a solution.

Better:

> "Find relevant job opportunities."

## Mistake 2: Making the Job Too Broad

Bad:

> "Improve my life."

Too broad.

Better:

> "Find a job that provides better career growth."

## Mistake 3: Focusing Only on Functional Jobs

Don't ignore:

* Emotional needs
* Social needs
* Trust
* Confidence
* Identity

## Mistake 4: Asking Customers What Features They Want

Customers can describe problems.

But Product Managers should explore the underlying job before deciding on the solution.

## Mistake 5: Relying on Hypothetical Questions

Instead of:

> "Would you use X?"

Ask:

> "Tell me about the last time you experienced this problem."

## Mistake 6: Assuming One Customer Has One Job

Customers can hire the same product for different reasons.

> Too many irrelevant jobs.

> Minimize time spent reviewing irrelevant jobs.

# 55. JTBD and Product-Market Fit

Product-market fit can be thought about through the relationship between:

> Customer jobs

and

> Product value.

If your product solves an important job significantly better than alternatives, customers have a reason to adopt it.

A strong product often has:

**Important Job + Poor Existing Solutions + Strong New Solution = Strong Opportunity**

# 56. JTBD Mental Model

When someone requests a feature, train yourself to think:

> Customer asks: "Can you add X?"

Don't immediately ask:

> "How do we build X?"

Ask:

> "What are they trying to accomplish?"

Then:

> "What problem are they experiencing?"

> "How are they solving it today?"

> "What outcome do they want?"

> "What alternatives exist?"

> "What is the best solution?"

This is one of the most valuable Product Management habits you can develop.

# 57. Interview Questions

## Question 1: What is JTBD?

### Strong Answer

JTBD is a framework for understanding the underlying progress a customer is trying to make in a particular situation. Instead of focusing primarily on product features or customer demographics, it focuses on the job the customer is trying to accomplish and the outcomes they want.

## Question 2: What does it mean to "hire" a product?

### Strong Answer

It means customers choose a product or solution because they believe it will help them accomplish a particular job. The same product can be hired for different jobs, and different products can compete to perform the same job.

## Question 3: What's the difference between a feature request and a JTBD?

### Strong Answer

A feature request describes a proposed solution, while JTBD describes the underlying customer goal or progress. For example, "I want a stop-loss feature" is a solution request, while "I want to protect my portfolio from large losses" describes the underlying job.

## Question 4: How would you discover a customer's JTBD?

### Strong Answer

I would interview customers about real recent experiences rather than hypothetical situations. I would explore what triggered the job, what they did, what alternatives they considered, what problems they encountered, why they chose their current solution, and what outcome they were trying to achieve.

## Question 5: How does JTBD help Product Managers?

### Strong Answer

It helps Product Managers understand the underlying customer problem before choosing a solution. This can prevent feature-driven product development, reveal underserved customer needs, identify non-obvious competitors, and uncover new product opportunities.
