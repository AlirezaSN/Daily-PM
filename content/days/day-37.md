# Day 37: Product Strategy & Vision

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should understand:

- What product strategy is
- Why product strategy matters
- The difference between vision, mission, strategy, goals, and tactics
- How product strategy connects company strategy to product decisions
- How to identify strategic opportunities
- How customer problems influence strategy
- How market and competitive analysis influence strategy
- Product positioning and differentiation
- Strategic pillars
- Strategic bets
- Product principles
- Strategic trade-offs
- How to define product goals and outcomes
- The difference between strategy and roadmap
- How to communicate product strategy
- How to evaluate and update strategy
- Common product strategy mistakes

---

# 1. What Is Product Strategy?

**Product strategy** is the set of choices and decisions that determine:

> **Where the product should go, who it should serve, what problems it should solve, and how it will create value.**

A simple mental model is:

```text
Company Strategy
       ↓
Product Strategy
       ↓
Product Goals
       ↓
Roadmap
       ↓
Features & Initiatives
````

Product strategy connects the company's broader business objectives to concrete product decisions.

---

# 2. Why Does Product Strategy Matter?

Without a clear strategy, teams can become a:

> **Feature factory**

A feature factory continuously ships features without knowing whether those features meaningfully improve the product.

For example:

```text
Stakeholder request
       ↓
Feature
       ↓
Feature
       ↓
Feature
       ↓
Another feature
```

The team may be very busy but have little strategic direction.

A strategy provides a filter:

> **Which opportunities are worth pursuing, and which are not?**

---

# 3. Strategy Is About Choices

Strategy is not simply:

> "We want to grow."

That's a goal.

Strategy requires choices.

For example:

> "We will grow by becoming the easiest investment platform for first-time investors."

Now there is a strategic direction.

This implies choices about:

* Target customers
* Product experience
* Features
* Pricing
* Distribution
* Competitors
* Capabilities

---

# 4. Strategy Requires Trade-offs

One of the most important characteristics of strategy is:

> **You cannot prioritize everything.**

Suppose a company says:

> "We want to be the cheapest, most premium, simplest, most powerful platform for everyone."

That's not a useful strategy.

A real strategy requires deciding:

> Who are we serving?

> What are we optimizing for?

> What will we deliberately not prioritize?

---

# 5. Product Vision

A **product vision** describes the future state you want the product to create.

It answers:

> **What future are we trying to create?**

A vision is usually longer-term.

Example:

> "Make investing understandable and accessible to everyone."

A vision is inspirational and directional.

It doesn't need to explain exactly how you'll get there.

---

# 6. Product Mission

A **mission** describes what the product or organization exists to do.

Example:

> "Help everyday investors make confident financial decisions."

A simple distinction:

### Vision

> Where are we going?

### Mission

> Why do we exist?

---

# 7. Vision vs. Strategy

These are often confused.

### Vision

> Create the simplest investment experience for everyday investors.

### Strategy

> Focus on first-time investors, simplify the trading experience, provide educational guidance, and reduce unnecessary complexity.

The vision describes the destination.

The strategy describes the choices about how to move toward it.

---

# 8. Strategy vs. Goals

### Strategy

> Focus on first-time investors and differentiate through simplicity.

### Goal

> Increase first-time investor activation by 20%.

Strategy explains:

> **How and where we will compete.**

Goals explain:

> **What outcome we want to achieve.**

---

# 9. Strategy vs. Tactics

### Strategy

> Win first-time investors through a simpler investment experience.

### Tactics

* Simplify onboarding
* Add educational explanations
* Improve order confirmation
* Redesign the portfolio screen
* Add contextual guidance

Tactics are actions.

Strategy explains why those actions matter and how they fit together.

---

# 10. The Product Strategy Stack

A useful framework is:

```text
VISION
Where do we want to go?
        ↓
MISSION
Why do we exist?
        ↓
STRATEGY
How will we win?
        ↓
GOALS
What outcomes do we need?
        ↓
INITIATIVES
What major problems/opportunities will we pursue?
        ↓
FEATURES
What will we build?
        ↓
METRICS
How will we know it worked?
```

A strong PM should be able to connect these layers.

---

# 11. Company Strategy vs. Product Strategy

Product strategy should support company strategy.

Imagine the company strategy is:

> Increase revenue by expanding into the SME market.

Product strategy might be:

> Build a self-service financial platform specifically designed for small businesses.

Product initiatives might include:

* SME onboarding
* Business accounts
* Expense management
* Invoicing
* Business reporting

The product strategy translates the business direction into product choices.

---

# 12. Product Strategy Should Not Be Created in Isolation

A PM should consider:

* Company strategy
* Business model
* Customer needs
* Market trends
* Competition
* Technology
* Regulatory environment
* Operational capabilities
* Financial constraints

Product strategy sits at the intersection of these factors.

```text
          Customers
             ↓
Competition → PRODUCT ← Business
             ↑
        Technology
             ↑
        Regulation
```

---

# 13. Start With the Customer

A strategy that ignores customers is dangerous.

Ask:

* Who are our most important customers?
* What problems do they have?
* How important are those problems?
* What alternatives do they currently use?
* Why would they choose us?
* What prevents them from using the product more?

Customer problems provide strategic opportunities.

---

# 14. Customer Problems vs. Feature Requests

Suppose customers say:

> "Add Apple Pay."

That is a feature request.

A PM should ask:

> "What problem are they trying to solve?"

Maybe the actual problem is:

> Customers want a faster and easier payment experience.

Possible solutions might include:

* Apple Pay
* Google Pay
* Saved payment methods
* One-click payment
* Better payment autofill

Strategy should focus on the underlying opportunity.

---

# 15. Strategic Opportunities

An **opportunity** is an area where solving a customer problem could create meaningful value.

Example:

### Observation

Users abandon onboarding.

### Problem

Identity verification is confusing.

### Opportunity

> Simplify identity verification for new customers.

This opportunity can then become part of product strategy.

---

# 16. Opportunity Size

Not every customer problem deserves strategic attention.

Evaluate opportunities based on factors such as:

* Number of affected users
* Problem severity
* Frequency
* Business impact
* Strategic importance
* Competitive importance
* Feasibility
* Risk

A problem affecting 1% of users may still be strategically important if those users generate 30% of revenue.

---

# 17. Market Analysis

Product strategy should consider the market.

Understand:

* Market size
* Growth rate
* Customer segments
* Trends
* Regulations
* Technology changes
* Competitors
* Substitute products
* Barriers to entry

You don't need a 100-page market report.

You need enough understanding to make good strategic decisions.

---

# 18. Competitive Analysis

Ask:

* Who are our direct competitors?
* Who are our indirect competitors?
* What alternatives do customers use?
* What are competitors good at?
* Where are they weak?
* How are they positioned?
* What capabilities do they have?
* What are they likely to do next?

Competitive analysis isn't about copying competitors.

It is about understanding the strategic landscape.

---

# 19. Direct vs. Indirect Competitors

Suppose you're building a stock trading platform.

### Direct competitors

Other stock trading platforms.

### Indirect competitors

* Traditional brokers
* Financial advisors
* Banks
* Investment funds
* Manual investing
* Doing nothing

Your customer doesn't only compare you with companies that have identical features.

They compare you with:

> **Their alternatives.**

---

# 20. Differentiation

Differentiation answers:

> **Why should customers choose us instead of alternatives?**

Possible dimensions:

* Price
* Convenience
* Speed
* Reliability
* Trust
* Simplicity
* Selection
* Quality
* Personalization
* Network effects
* Brand
* Technology

For example:

> "We are the easiest platform for first-time investors."

This creates a potential strategic position.

---

# 21. Differentiation Must Matter to Customers

A company may claim:

> "We have the fastest API."

But if customers don't care, it isn't meaningful differentiation.

Strong differentiation is:

> **Something customers value and competitors cannot easily replicate.**

For example:

```text
Customer Value
      +
Distinctiveness
      +
Defensibility
      ↓
Strong Differentiation
```

---

# 22. Positioning

**Product positioning** describes how you want customers to perceive the product relative to alternatives.

A useful positioning structure:

> For [target customer], who [customer need], [product] is a [category] that [key benefit], unlike [alternative], because [reason to believe].

Example:

> For first-time investors who find traditional trading platforms intimidating, our product is an investment platform that makes investing simple and understandable, unlike complex broker platforms, because it provides contextual guidance throughout the investment journey.

---

# 23. Target Market

A strategy should define who you are prioritizing.

You may choose:

* Mass market
* Enterprise
* SMB
* Students
* Professionals
* Beginners
* Experts
* High-value customers
* Specific geographic markets

Trying to serve everyone often creates weak positioning.

---

# 24. Ideal Customer Profile

The **Ideal Customer Profile (ICP)** describes the customer segment that is especially valuable or strategically important.

For example:

> First-time investors aged 25–40 who have stable income, limited investment experience, and want to start investing independently.

The ICP helps focus product decisions.

---

# 25. Segmentation

Customers can be segmented based on:

### Demographics

* Age
* Location
* Income

### Behavior

* Frequency of usage
* Purchase behavior
* Engagement

### Needs

* Beginner vs. expert
* Price-sensitive vs. convenience-oriented

### Value

* Revenue
* Lifetime value
* Strategic importance

Good strategy often requires deciding:

> Which segment should we prioritize?

---

# 26. Strategic Pillars

Strategic pillars are a small number of major areas that guide product decisions.

For example:

### Strategy

> Become the easiest investment platform for first-time investors.

### Strategic Pillars

1. **Simplify onboarding**
2. **Make investing understandable**
3. **Build trust**
4. **Reduce transaction friction**

Initiatives should connect to one or more pillars.

---

# 27. Why Strategic Pillars Help

Without pillars:

```text
Feature A
Feature B
Feature C
Feature D
Feature E
Feature F
```

With pillars:

```text
Simplify onboarding
    ↓
Feature A
Feature B

Make investing understandable
    ↓
Feature C
Feature D

Build trust
    ↓
Feature E
```

The team can now see the strategic logic behind the work.

---

# 28. Strategic Bets

A **strategic bet** is a significant investment based on a belief about where future value will come from.

Examples:

> "AI-powered financial guidance will become a major driver of customer engagement."

or:

> "Expanding into SMEs will become our primary growth engine."

Strategic bets involve uncertainty.

That's why they require:

* Evidence
* Experiments
* Metrics
* Review points

---

# 29. Strategic Bets vs. Features

A strategic bet:

> Become the leading AI-powered investment assistant.

A feature:

> Add an AI chatbot.

The feature may support the strategic bet.

But the strategic bet is much broader.

You might discover that a chatbot isn't the right way to achieve the goal.

The strategy should survive changes in implementation.

---

# 30. Product Principles

Product principles are decision-making guidelines.

Examples:

### Principle 1

> Make complex financial concepts understandable.

### Principle 2

> Minimize unnecessary steps.

### Principle 3

> Never hide important financial risks.

### Principle 4

> Default to transparency.

These principles help teams make consistent decisions when the PM isn't involved in every detail.

---

# 31. Strategy as a Decision Filter

Suppose the team receives three requests:

### Request A

Large enterprise customer wants a complex customization.

### Request B

Improve onboarding for first-time investors.

### Request C

Add an advanced chart for expert traders.

If the strategy is:

> Become the simplest platform for first-time investors.

Then Request B probably has stronger strategic alignment.

Strategy helps answer:

> **What should we say no to?**

---

# 32. Strategy Requires Saying No

A strategy without exclusions is weak.

If you prioritize:

* Beginners
* Experts
* Enterprise
* SMB
* Students
* High-net-worth customers

you may end up serving nobody particularly well.

A PM should be able to say:

> "This is a good idea, but it isn't aligned with our current strategy."

That's a strategic decision.

---

# 33. Strategy and Trade-offs

Consider two opportunities:

### Opportunity A

Improve beginner onboarding.

Potential impact:

> High

Strategic alignment:

> Very high

### Opportunity B

Build advanced derivatives trading tools.

Potential impact:

> Potentially high

Strategic alignment:

> Low

The PM may choose A even if B sounds attractive.

This is the essence of strategic prioritization.

---

# 34. Product Goals

Product goals translate strategy into measurable outcomes.

Example:

### Strategy

> Become the easiest platform for first-time investors.

### Goal

> Increase successful first-time investor activation from 40% to 55%.

Now the strategy becomes measurable.

---

# 35. Outcome vs. Output

Output:

> Launch a new onboarding flow.

Outcome:

> Increase successful onboarding completion by 15%.

A strategic product team asks:

> "What outcome are we trying to create?"

not simply:

> "What should we build?"

---

Depending on the strategy, useful metrics may include:

### Acquisition

* New users

### Activation

* Time to first value

### Engagement

* Session frequency

### Retention

* D1/D7/D30 retention

* Repeat usage

### Monetization

* Revenue
* ARPU
* Conversion to paid
* LTV

### Product Value

* Task success
* Customer satisfaction
* Customer effort
* Time to value

The metrics should connect to the strategic objective.

---

# 37. Strategy vs. Roadmap

This is one of the most important distinctions for PMs.

### Strategy

> Become the easiest investment platform for first-time investors.

### Roadmap

```text
Q1
Simplify onboarding

Q2
Improve educational guidance

Q3
Improve portfolio experience

Q4
Personalized investment assistance
```

The roadmap describes planned initiatives.

The strategy explains:

> **Why these initiatives matter and how they fit together.**

# 38. A Roadmap Without Strategy

Imagine a roadmap:

```text

Q2 → AI chatbot
Q3 → Referral system
```

A stakeholder asks:

> "Why are we doing these?"

If the answer is unclear, the roadmap is probably disconnected from strategy.

# 41. Current State Analysis

Analyze:

### Customers

* Who uses the product?

### Product

* What works?
* What doesn't?
* Where are users dropping off?

### Business

* Where does revenue come from?

* What trends are changing it?

# 42. SWOT Analysis

A simple strategic framework is **SWOT**.

### Strengths

Internal advantages.

### Weaknesses

Internal limitations.

### Opportunities

External possibilities.

### Threats

External risks.

Example:

|          | Positive      | Negative   |
| -------- | ------------- | ---------- |
| Internal | Strengths     | Weaknesses |
| External | Opportunities | Threats    |

SWOT can be useful as a starting point, but it shouldn't replace deeper analysis.

# 45. Strategic Coherence

A good strategy should tell a consistent story.

Ask:

> Why this customer?

> Why this problem?

> Why now?

> Why us?

> Why this solution?

> Why this metric?

If these answers don't connect, the strategy may be weak.

---

# 46. "Why Now?"

Timing matters.

A strategic opportunity can become more attractive because of:

* Market changes
* Technology changes
* Customer behavior
* Regulation
* Competitor moves
* Cost changes

For example:

> Generative AI becomes dramatically cheaper and more capable.

This may create a new strategic opportunity.

But:

> "AI is trending."

is not enough.

The PM should ask:

> What customer or business problem can this technology now solve significantly better?

---

# 47. Technology as a Strategic Input

Technology can create new product opportunities.

Examples:

* AI
* LLMs
* Real-time data
* Computer vision
* Blockchain
* Edge computing

But technology itself isn't strategy.

Bad:

> "We need an AI strategy."

Better:

> "We can use AI to reduce customer support dependency and improve self-service resolution."

Technology should connect to customer or business value.

---

# 48. Regulation as a Strategic Input

This is especially important in fintech.

Regulation can:

* Create constraints
* Create opportunities
* Change product economics
* Require new capabilities
* Change customer behavior

For example:

> A new regulation requires stronger identity verification.

This may become:

* A compliance requirement
* A product experience challenge
* An opportunity to differentiate through faster verification

---

# 49. Strategy Under Uncertainty

Product strategy is rarely based on complete information.

You may have uncertainty about:

* Market size
* Customer demand
* Technology
* Competition
* Regulation
* Business economics

Therefore, good strategy includes:

> **Learning mechanisms.**

For example:

```text
Strategic Bet
     ↓
Hypothesis
     ↓
Experiment
     ↓
Evidence
     ↓
Continue / Adapt / Stop
```

---

# 50. Strategy Is Not a One-Time Document

Markets change.

Customers change.

Competitors change.

Technology changes.

Therefore:

> Product strategy should evolve.

This doesn't mean changing strategy every week.

It means periodically reviewing whether the assumptions behind the strategy remain valid.

---

# 51. Strategic Review

A strategy review might ask:

### Customer

Are customer needs changing?

### Market

Is the market evolving?

### Competition

Are competitors changing their positioning?

### Business

Are our economics still attractive?

### Product

Are our initiatives producing the expected outcomes?

### Technology

Are new capabilities changing what's possible?

### Assumptions

Which assumptions have been proven or disproven?

---

# 52. Continue, Adapt, or Stop

For each strategic initiative, ask:

### Continue

Evidence supports the strategy.

### Adapt

The opportunity remains attractive, but the approach needs to change.

### Stop

Evidence suggests the opportunity isn't valuable enough.

This prevents teams from continuing initiatives simply because they were already started.

---

# 53. Strategic Bets and Evidence

Suppose you believe:

> AI recommendations will increase user engagement.

You shouldn't immediately invest heavily.

Start with a smaller bet:

> Test personalized AI investment explanations with 5% of users.

Measure:

* Engagement
* User satisfaction
* Retention
* Trust
* Incorrect recommendation rate

Then decide:

> Continue, adapt, or stop.

---

# 54. Strategy Communication

A strategy that exists only in the PM's document isn't useful.

Everyone should understand:

* Where we're going
* Why
* Who we're serving
* What we're prioritizing
* What we're not prioritizing
* How success is measured

A good PM can explain the strategy in:

> 30 seconds

and also:

> 30 minutes.

---

# 55. The One-Sentence Strategy

Try to summarize strategy in one sentence.

For example:

> "We will become the easiest investment platform for first-time investors by simplifying onboarding, providing contextual education, and building trust."

This is much more useful than:

> "We want to improve the product and increase growth."

---

# 56. Strategy Storytelling

When presenting strategy, a useful structure is:

```text
1. Where are we now?
        ↓
2. What problem/opportunity exists?
        ↓
3. Why is it important?
        ↓
4. Where do we want to go?
        ↓
5. What choices will we make?
        ↓
6. What will we not prioritize?
        ↓
7. What outcomes do we expect?
        ↓
8. How will we measure success?
```

This tells a coherent strategic story.

# 57. Common Product Strategy Mistakes

## Mistake 1: Calling a Roadmap a Strategy

A list of features is not strategy.

---

## Mistake 2: Strategy Without Choices

> "Improve customer experience."

Too broad.

---

## Mistake 3: Strategy Without Customers

If you can't explain which customer problem you're solving, reconsider the strategy.

---

## Mistake 4: Strategy Without Business Context

Products need to create sustainable business value.

---

## Mistake 5: Too Many Strategic Priorities

If everything is a strategic priority:

> Nothing is.

---

## Mistake 6: Copying Competitors

Competitor behavior is an input, not a strategy.

---

## Mistake 7: Technology-Led Strategy

> "We should use AI."

Why?

Start with the problem.

---

## Mistake 8: No Metrics

If you can't measure whether the strategy is working, you can't effectively evaluate it.

---

## Mistake 9: Never Revisiting Strategy

A strategy based on outdated assumptions can become a liability.

---

# 62. PM Interview Questions

## Question 1: What is product strategy?

Product strategy is a set of choices about where the product will focus, which customers and problems it will prioritize, how it will create value, and how it will achieve desired business and customer outcomes.

---

## Question 2: What's the difference between product vision and product strategy?

Vision describes the future state the product wants to create. Strategy describes the choices and approach the company will use to move toward that future.

---

## Question 3: What's the difference between strategy and roadmap?

Strategy explains the direction, choices, and rationale behind product priorities. A roadmap communicates the major initiatives and their approximate sequencing or timing.

---

## Question 4: How do you create a product strategy?

I would start by understanding the current state across customers, product performance, market, competition, business economics, and capabilities. Then I would identify important opportunities, define the target customer and desired future state, make strategic choices, define strategic pillars and measurable outcomes, and translate those choices into prioritized initiatives.

---

## Question 5: How do you decide which customer segment to prioritize?

I would evaluate factors such as customer needs, problem severity, market attractiveness, business value, strategic fit, competitive position, and our ability to serve that segment effectively.

---

## Question 6: What makes a good product strategy?

A good strategy is focused, evidence-based, differentiated, connected to customer and business value, explicit about trade-offs, measurable, and adaptable as new information becomes available.

---

## Question 7: What would you do if the CEO asks for a feature that isn't aligned with your strategy?

I would first understand the underlying business objective and urgency. If the request represents a genuinely important strategic opportunity, I would reconsider the strategy or priorities based on evidence. Otherwise, I would explain the trade-off and show where the request fits relative to current strategic priorities.

---

# 63. Key Takeaways

1. **Product strategy is about choices.**
2. Strategy answers where to focus and how to create value.
3. Vision describes the future state you want to create.
4. Mission explains why the product or organization exists.
5. Goals describe measurable outcomes.
6. Roadmaps communicate initiatives and sequencing.
7. Features are implementation-level outputs.
8. Strategy should connect company goals to product decisions.
9. Customer problems are an important source of strategic opportunities.
10. Market and competitive analysis provide strategic context.
11. Differentiation explains why customers should choose you.

```text
Customer
   ↓
Problem
   ↓
Opportunity
   ↓
Strategic Choice
   ↓
Outcome
```

23. The ultimate purpose of product strategy is to help the organization **focus limited resources on the opportunities most likely to create meaningful customer and business value**.

# 64. Final Mental Model

When thinking strategically as a Product Manager, ask:

```text
WHERE ARE WE NOW?
        ↓
WHO ARE WE SERVING?
        ↓
WHAT PROBLEM MATTERS MOST?
        ↓
WHY DOES IT MATTER?
        ↓
WHERE DO WE WANT TO GO?
        ↓
HOW WILL WE WIN?
        ↓
WHAT WILL WE NOT DO?
        ↓
WHAT OUTCOMES DO WE EXPECT?
        ↓
HOW WILL WE MEASURE THEM?
        ↓
WHAT DID WE LEARN?
        ↓
SHOULD WE CONTINUE, ADAPT, OR STOP?
```

The strongest Product Managers don't simply answer:

> **"What should we build?"**

They can explain:

> **"Why this customer, why this problem, why now, why us, why this approach, and how we'll know we're right."**
