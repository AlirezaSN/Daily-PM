# Introduction: From Priorities to a Plan

In Day 26, we learned how to prioritize.

But prioritization answers:

> "What should we work on?"

A roadmap goes one step further:

> "What direction are we taking, and what are we planning to focus on over time?"

The connection looks like this:

```text
Vision
  ↓
Strategy
  ↓
Goals / OKRs
  ↓
Prioritization
  ↓
Roadmap
  ↓
Execution
```

A roadmap should therefore be a **consequence of strategy**, not simply a list of requested features.

---

# What Is a Product Roadmap?

A product roadmap is a communication and planning artifact that shows:

- Where the product is going
- Which outcomes or problems the team is focusing on
- What major initiatives are planned
- Approximately when work is expected to happen

A roadmap should answer:

> **"What are we focusing on, why, and roughly when?"**

---

# What a Roadmap Is NOT

A roadmap is not necessarily:

- A detailed project plan
- A complete list of features
- A development backlog
- A promise that every item will ship
- A fixed schedule for the next 12 months

This distinction is extremely important.

---

# Roadmap vs Backlog vs Release Plan

These three are often confused.

## Product Roadmap

Focus:

> Direction and priorities.

Example:

```text
Q1: Improve activation
Q2: Improve retention
Q3: Expand personalization
```

---

## Product Backlog

Focus:

> Detailed work that could be done.

Example:

- Redesign signup form
- Add social login
- Improve error messages
- Add onboarding checklist

---

## Release Plan

Focus:

> What is expected to be released and when.

Example:

```text
March 10:
New onboarding flow

March 24:
Referral program

April 5:
New dashboard
```

---

# A Simple Mental Model

```text
Roadmap
"What are we trying to accomplish?"

        ↓

Initiatives
"What major things might we do?"

        ↓

Backlog
"What specific work is required?"

        ↓

Release
"What are we actually shipping?"
```

---

# Why Product Roadmaps Matter

## 1. Alignment

A roadmap helps teams understand priorities.

Engineering knows:

> What we're focusing on.

Design knows:

> What problems they're solving.

Leadership knows:

> Where product investment is going.

Sales knows:

> What they can and cannot expect.

---

# 2. Communication

Product Managers constantly communicate with stakeholders.

A roadmap gives everyone a shared view of product direction.

---

# 3. Focus

A roadmap makes trade-offs visible.

It shows:

> These are the things we're focusing on.

Which also means:

> These are the things we're not focusing on right now.

---

# 4. Strategic Alignment

A good roadmap connects strategy to execution.

For example:

## Strategy

> Become the easiest investment platform for beginners.

↓

## Objective

> Improve beginner activation.

↓

## Roadmap Initiative

> Simplify first-investment experience.

↓

## Potential Features

- Guided onboarding
- Investment education
- Simplified order flow

---

# Feature-Based Roadmaps

A traditional roadmap often looks like this:

| Q1 | Q2 | Q3 |
|---|---|---|
| New dashboard | Mobile redesign | AI assistant |
| Social login | Referral system | Advanced analytics |

This is easy to understand.

But it has a problem.

It focuses on:

> **What we're building**

rather than:

> **Why we're building it**

---

# Outcome-Based Roadmaps

A stronger approach is to organize the roadmap around outcomes.

Example:

| Time | Outcome | Initiatives |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | Improve activation | New onboarding |
| Q2 | Improve retention | Personalization |
| Q3 | Increase monetization | Premium features |

This communicates the purpose behind the work.

---

# Feature Roadmap vs Outcome Roadmap

| Feature-Based | Outcome-Based |
|---|---|
| New onboarding | Improve activation |
| AI assistant | Improve support resolution |
| New dashboard | Improve decision-making |
| Referral feature | Increase organic acquisition |

Outcome-based roadmaps encourage teams to think:

> "What result are we trying to create?"

---

# Roadmap Time Horizons

A roadmap usually becomes less detailed as you look further into the future.

A useful model is:

```text
Near Term
Detailed
High confidence

     ↓

Mid Term
Moderate detail
Moderate confidence

     ↓

Long Term
High-level direction
Low confidence
```

---

# Example

## Now

Very likely:

> Redesign onboarding.

---

## Next

Likely but not fully defined:

> Improve activation experience.

---

## Later

Strategic direction:

> Expand personalized investing.

Notice:

The further into the future we go, the less specific we should be.

---

# Now / Next / Later

One of the simplest roadmap formats.

## Now

Currently being worked on.

---

## Next

Likely upcoming priorities.

---

## Later

Potential future opportunities.

Example:

```text
NOW
- Improve onboarding
- Fix payment failures

NEXT
- Personalized recommendations
- Referral improvements

LATER
- AI investment assistant
- International expansion
```

---

# Why Now / Next / Later Is Useful

It avoids false precision.

Instead of saying:

> "The AI assistant will launch on September 17."

You can say:

> "AI-assisted investing is a Later strategic opportunity."

This communicates direction without pretending you know the future.

---

# Roadmap Confidence

Not everything on a roadmap has the same level of certainty.

You can categorize initiatives as:

### Committed

High confidence.

The team expects to do it.

---

### Planned

Strong intention, but details may change.

---

### Exploratory

An opportunity being investigated.

---

# Example

```text
Committed:
Improve payment reliability

Planned:
Redesign onboarding

Exploratory:
AI investment assistant
```

This is much more honest than presenting everything as equally certain.

---

# Roadmap and OKRs

Your roadmap should support your objectives.

Example:

## Objective

> Increase successful first-time investment.

### Key Results

- Increase first-trade completion from 20% to 35%.
- Reduce time to first trade from 2 days to 30 minutes.

### Roadmap Initiatives

- Simplify onboarding
- Add guided investment flow
- Improve order experience

The roadmap answers:

> What will we focus on?

The OKRs answer:

> What outcomes are we trying to achieve?

---

# Roadmap and Prioritization

A roadmap should not be created independently from prioritization.

The process should be:

```text
Identify Opportunities
       ↓
Evaluate Opportunities
       ↓
Prioritize
       ↓
Select Strategic Initiatives
       ↓
Create Roadmap
```

---

# Roadmap Planning Process

## Step 1: Start With Strategy

Ask:

> What are our strategic priorities?

---

## Step 2: Define Outcomes

Ask:

> What should improve?

---

## Step 3: Identify Initiatives

Ask:

> What could help us achieve those outcomes?

---

## Step 4: Prioritize

Evaluate:

- Impact
- Effort
- Confidence
- Strategic alignment
- Risk

---

## Step 5: Sequence

Determine:

> What should happen first?

Dependencies matter.

---

## Step 6: Communicate

Create a roadmap that stakeholders can understand.

---

## Step 7: Revisit

A roadmap should evolve as you learn.

---

# Roadmap Dependencies

Sometimes initiatives depend on other work.

Example:

```text
Data infrastructure
       ↓
Recommendation engine
       ↓
Personalized recommendations
```

You cannot always start the final feature immediately.

Therefore, roadmapping requires sequencing.

---

# Roadmap Dependencies Example

Imagine a trading platform wants:

> Personalized investment recommendations.

But the system first needs:

1. User preference data
2. Portfolio data
3. Recommendation infrastructure
4. Compliance review

Therefore:

```text
Data Collection
      ↓
Recommendation Engine
      ↓
Compliance Validation
      ↓
Personalized Recommendations
```

---

# Roadmap Trade-offs

Suppose you have two options:

### Initiative A

Potential revenue:

$1M

Effort:

6 months

---

### Initiative B

Potential revenue:

$400K

Effort:

1 month

Which should you choose?

There is no automatic answer.

You need to consider:

- Strategic importance
- Confidence
- Opportunity cost
- Dependencies
- Timing
- Risk

---

# Roadmap Changes Are Normal

One of the biggest mistakes is treating a roadmap as a contract.

The market changes.

Customers change.

Competitors change.

Technology changes.

Your assumptions may be wrong.

Therefore:

> **A good roadmap should change when new evidence changes your priorities.**

---

# Example

You planned:

> Build advanced analytics.

Then you discover:

> 30% of payments are failing.

Would you continue the roadmap exactly as planned?

Probably not.

You may prioritize payment reliability instead.

That is not bad Product Management.

That is good Product Management.

---

# Communicating Roadmap Changes

Don't simply say:

> "We changed the roadmap."

Explain:

### What changed?

> Payment reliability became a higher priority.

### Why?

> Failure rates increased and are affecting customer trust.

### What does it affect?

> Advanced analytics will move to a later period.

### What remains unchanged?

> Our strategic focus on improving customer experience remains.

This creates trust.

---

# Stakeholder Roadmaps

Different audiences need different levels of detail.

## Executives

Usually care about:

- Strategic priorities
- Business outcomes
- Major investments
- Risks

---

## Engineering

Usually needs:

- Initiatives
- Dependencies
- Technical considerations
- Relative sequencing

---

## Sales

Usually cares about:

- Customer-facing capabilities
- Major expected improvements
- Timing confidence

---

## Customers

Usually need:

- Relevant improvements
- High-level direction
- Realistic expectations

---

# Should You Have One Roadmap?

Not necessarily.

You can maintain:

- Internal product roadmap
- Executive roadmap
- Customer-facing roadmap

But they should all represent the same underlying strategy.

---

# Common Roadmap Mistakes

## Mistake #1: Turning the Roadmap Into a Feature List

Bad:

```text
Q1:
Button redesign
Dashboard
Notifications
Dark mode
```

There is no strategic context.

---

# Mistake #2: False Precision

Bad:

> Feature X will launch on October 17.

If you don't know, don't pretend.

---

# Mistake #3: Roadmap as a Promise

The roadmap represents current intent.

It should not become an inflexible contract.

---

# Mistake #4: Everything Is "Now"

If everything is a priority:

> Nothing is a priority.

---

# Mistake #5: Ignoring Outcomes

Launching a feature is not the same as creating value.

Always ask:

> What outcome should this initiative produce?

---

# Mistake #6: Never Updating the Roadmap

A roadmap that never changes may indicate:

- You are not learning
- You are ignoring new information
- You are optimizing for predictability instead of impact

---

# Roadmap Example: LinkedIn

Imagine LinkedIn's strategic goal is:

> Increase professional opportunities for members.

A possible outcome-based roadmap:

| Period | Outcome | Initiatives |
|---|---|---|
| Now | Improve job discovery | Job recommendations |
| Next | Increase quality connections | Networking improvements |
| Later | Improve career development | Learning and career tools |

This is more strategic than:

> "Build feature X in Q2."

### Next

**Build investor confidence**

**Personalize investment experience**

Initiatives:

- Personalized recommendations

# Interview Question

## Question

How do you create a product roadmap?

## Strong Answer

I start with the product strategy and business objectives, then identify the customer problems and opportunities that can contribute to those goals. I prioritize initiatives based on expected impact, effort, confidence, strategic alignment, and risk. I then sequence the highest-priority initiatives based on dependencies and capacity. I communicate the roadmap at an appropriate level of detail and revisit it as new evidence becomes available.

---

# Interview Question 2

## Question

How do you handle stakeholders asking to add features to the roadmap?

## Strong Answer

I first try to understand the problem behind the request rather than immediately accepting the proposed solution. I evaluate the opportunity against our strategic goals, customer impact, effort, and existing priorities. If it is not a priority, I explain the trade-off and what we would need to deprioritize to make room for it.

---

# Key Takeaways

1. A roadmap communicates product direction and priorities.
2. A roadmap is not the same as a backlog.
3. Roadmaps should connect directly to strategy and OKRs.
4. Outcome-based roadmaps are generally more strategic than feature-only roadmaps.
5. Near-term plans can be more detailed than long-term plans.
6. Now / Next / Later is a useful way to communicate uncertainty.
7. Roadmaps should evolve when evidence changes.
8. A roadmap represents intent, not an unconditional promise.
9. Different stakeholders need different levels of roadmap detail.
10. A good roadmap communicates not only what you're doing, but also why.

# PM Reflection

Think about your current product.

Ask:

- Does our roadmap reflect our strategy?
- Is it focused on outcomes or features?
- Are we making promises we cannot confidently keep?
- Can stakeholders understand why each major initiative exists?
- What should we remove from the roadmap?
- How often should the roadmap be revisited?

Remember:

> **A roadmap is not a prediction of the future.**

It is your **best current plan for moving toward your product strategy**, based on what you know today.
