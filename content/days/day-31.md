# Introduction: Customers Are Not Just "Users"

One of the most important PM skills is understanding customers.

A Product Manager should be able to answer:

> Who is the customer?

> What are they trying to accomplish?

> What problems do they experience?

> How do they solve those problems today?

> Why do those problems matter?

Without understanding customers, product decisions often become assumptions.

---

# What Is User Research?

User research is the process of systematically learning about:

- Users
- Customers
- Their behaviors
- Their needs
- Their problems
- Their motivations
- Their context
- Their experiences

The purpose is not simply to "talk to users."

The purpose is:

> **To reduce uncertainty and make better product decisions.**

---

# Why PMs Need User Research

Imagine a stakeholder says:

> "Customers want a mobile app."

You could immediately start planning it.

But user research might reveal:

> Customers don't actually need a mobile app. They need faster access to their reports.

The solution changes completely.

---

# Research Before Building

A useful sequence is:

```text
Assumption
   ↓
Research
   ↓
Evidence
   ↓
Insight
   ↓
Product Decision
```

Instead of:

```text
Opinion
   ↓
Feature
   ↓
Development
   ↓
Hope
```

---

# What Can User Research Tell You?

Research can help you understand:

## Behaviors

What users actually do.

## Problems

What frustrates or blocks them.

## Motivations

Why they behave in a certain way.

## Context

Where, when, and under what circumstances they use your product.

## Alternatives

What they use instead of your product.

## Expectations

What they believe should happen.

---

# What User Research Cannot Guarantee

Research does not tell you with certainty:

> "This feature will succeed."

A user can say:

> "I would definitely use this."

and never use it.

Therefore:

> **Research provides evidence, not certainty.**

---

# Qualitative vs Quantitative Research

Two major categories:

## Qualitative

Helps answer:

> **Why?**

Examples:

- Interviews
- Observation
- Usability testing
- Diary studies

---

## Quantitative

Helps answer:

> **How many? How often? How much?**

Examples:

- Analytics
- Surveys
- Experiments
- A/B tests

---

# Example

Analytics:

> 45% of users abandon identity verification.

This tells you:

> **What is happening.**

Interviews:

> Users don't understand why they need to provide certain documents.

This helps explain:

> **Why it is happening.**

Together, they are much more powerful.

---

# When Should You Conduct User Research?

Research can happen throughout the product lifecycle.

### Before building

Understand the problem.

### During design

Evaluate possible solutions.

### Before launch

Test usability.

### After launch

Understand behavior and problems.

### Continuously

Discover new opportunities.

---

# Types of User Research

Common methods include:

- Customer interviews
- User observation
- Surveys
- Usability testing
- Diary studies
- Focus groups
- Customer support analysis
- Product analytics
- Concept testing
- Prototype testing

This lesson focuses primarily on **customer interviews**.

---

# Customer Interviews

A customer interview is a structured conversation designed to understand a user's:

- Experiences
- Behaviors
- Problems
- Goals
- Motivations
- Context

The goal is not to convince the user of your idea.

The goal is to learn.

---

# The Most Important Interview Principle

> **Talk less. Listen more.**

A common mistake is spending 70% of the interview explaining the product.

Instead, aim for the user to do most of the talking.

---

# Interview Mindset

Before the interview, remind yourself:

> "I am here to learn, not to prove that my idea is correct."

This is extremely important.

---

# Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias occurs when you look for evidence that supports what you already believe.

Example:

You believe:

> Users need an AI assistant.

During interviews, you focus on comments such as:

> "Yes, an assistant could be useful."

But ignore:

> "I don't trust automated recommendations."

A good researcher actively looks for evidence that challenges their assumptions.

---

# Research Questions vs Interview Questions

A research question is what you want to learn.

Example:

> Why do users abandon the claims process?

Interview questions are how you investigate it.

For example:

> "Tell me about the last time you submitted a claim."

---

# Start With Research Questions

Before writing interview questions, define:

> **What do I need to learn?**

Example:

### Research Question

Why do users abandon onboarding?

### Areas to investigate

- User expectations
- Difficult steps
- Confusing information
- Emotional concerns
- Technical problems
- Alternatives

Now create questions around these areas.

---

# Interview Preparation

Before an interview, define:

## 1. Research Objective

What do you want to learn?

---

## 2. Target Participant

Who should you talk to?

---

## 3. Interview Questions

What will you ask?

---

## 4. Logistics

- Duration
- Location
- Recording
- Note-taking
- Consent

---

## 5. Analysis Plan

How will you turn the conversations into insights?

---

# Recruiting Participants

Who you interview matters.

Don't randomly interview anyone.

Define the relevant segment.

For example:

> Users who completed at least one insurance claim in the last six months.

This gives you a specific population.

---

# Segment Your Participants

You may want to interview:

- New users
- Experienced users
- Power users
- Churned users
- Inactive users
- High-value customers
- Users with specific problems

Different segments may reveal different problems.

---

# Example: Stock Trading Platform

Suppose you are investigating why beginners don't place their first trade.

Potential participants:

- New users who haven't traded
- New users who traded once
- Experienced traders
- Users who abandoned onboarding

Do not only interview experienced traders.

They may not represent beginners.

---

# How Many Interviews?

There is no universal magic number.

The right number depends on:

- Research objective
- User diversity
- Complexity of the problem
- How much new information you're discovering

For a focused product problem, a small number of interviews can often reveal meaningful patterns.

The important concept is:

> **Look for recurring patterns, not an arbitrary number.**

---

# Interview Structure

A practical 30–45 minute interview can follow:

```text
Introduction
   ↓
Background
   ↓
Recent Experience
   ↓
Problem Exploration
   ↓
Current Solutions
   ↓
Deep Dive
   ↓
Optional Concept Evaluation
   ↓
Closing
```

---

# Part 1: Introduction

Explain:

- Who you are
- Why you're talking to them
- How long it will take
- That there are no right or wrong answers

Example:

> "Thanks for taking the time. I'm interested in learning how you currently manage your insurance claims. There are no right or wrong answers. I'm not evaluating you; I'm trying to understand your experience."

---

# Part 2: Background

Understand the user's context.

Questions:

> "Can you tell me a little about how you currently use the product?"

> "How often do you use it?"

> "What do you typically use it for?"

Avoid spending too much time here.

---

# Part 3: Recent Experience

This is one of the most valuable techniques.

Ask:

> **"Tell me about the last time you..."**

Why?

Because recent real experiences are more reliable than general opinions.

---

# Example

Instead of:

> "Do you usually have trouble submitting claims?"

Ask:

> "Tell me about the last claim you submitted."

Then explore what happened.

---

# Why "Last Time" Questions Work

Compare:

### Weak

> "Do you ever have trouble finding jobs?"

### Better

> "Tell me about the last time you searched for a job."

The second question leads to a real event.

---

# Part 4: Explore the Problem

Ask about:

- What happened?
- What were they trying to accomplish?
- What did they do?
- What was difficult?
- What happened next?

---

# Example

User:

> "The process was confusing."

Don't immediately move on.

Ask:

> "What was confusing?"

Then:

> "What did you expect to happen?"

Then:

> "What did you do instead?"

Then:

> "How did you eventually solve it?"

This is where the real insight often appears.

---

# Follow-Up Questions

Great interviewers don't simply follow a script.

They follow interesting signals.

Useful follow-ups:

> "Can you tell me more about that?"

> "What happened next?"

> "Why was that important?"

> "How did you handle that?"

> "What did you expect?"

> "What made that difficult?"

> "How often does that happen?"

> "Can you give me an example?"

---

# The Power of "Why?"

"Why?" can be useful, but repeated "Why?" can feel like an interrogation.

Instead of:

> Why?

Use:

> "What made you decide to do that?"

or:

> "What was important about that?"

---

# Digging Deeper

Imagine the user says:

> "I don't like the investment recommendations."

You could ask:

> "What don't you like about them?"

User:

> "I don't trust them."

Follow up:

> "What makes you feel that way?"

User:

> "I don't understand how the recommendations are generated."

Now you have a potentially valuable insight:

> Lack of transparency may be reducing trust.

---

# Asking About Behavior

Behavior is often more valuable than opinions.

Ask:

> "What did you do?"

instead of:

> "What would you do?"

---

# Example

Weak:

> "Would you use an automated claim assistant?"

Better:

> "How do you currently find answers when you're unsure about a claim?"

This reveals the current behavior.

---

# Ask About Current Solutions

One of the most important research questions is:

> **How do you solve this today?**

If the problem is important, users usually have some workaround.

Examples:

- Excel spreadsheets
- Phone calls
- Google searches
- Manual processes
- Competitor products
- Internal tools
- Asking colleagues

---

# Why Workarounds Matter

Suppose a customer says:

> "Reporting is difficult."

Then you discover:

> They spend four hours every month manually combining three CSV files.

Now you understand:

- The problem is real.
- It is frequent.
- It has measurable cost.

This is stronger evidence than:

> "Reporting is annoying."

---

# Understanding Problem Severity

Not every problem is equally important.

Ask:

> "How often does this happen?"

> "How much time does it take?"

> "What happens when it goes wrong?"

> "How do you deal with it today?"

> "How important is solving this?"

---

# A Useful Signal: Existing Investment

A strong indicator of problem importance is:

> **What are users already doing to solve it?**

If users:

- Spend money
- Spend significant time
- Create complex workarounds
- Use multiple tools
- Contact support repeatedly

the problem may be significant.

---

# Asking About Frequency

Instead of:

> "Is this a problem?"

Ask:

> "How often does this happen?"

Example:

> "You mentioned that document verification is frustrating. How often do you encounter this?"

Frequency helps establish importance.

---

# Asking About Impact

Ask:

> "What happens when this problem occurs?"

Possible answers:

- User gives up
- Calls support
- Delays the task
- Uses another service
- Makes an error
- Loses money

Impact is often more important than frustration alone.

---

# Avoid Leading Questions

A leading question pushes the participant toward an answer.

Bad:

> "Don't you think the onboarding process is too complicated?"

Better:

> "How would you describe the onboarding process?"

---

# Bad vs Good Questions

| Bad Question | Better Question |
|---|---|
| Would you use this feature? | How do you solve this today? |
| Do you like our design? | What do you think is happening here? |
| Is onboarding too long? | Tell me about your onboarding experience. |
| Would $10 be reasonable? | How do you currently pay for this? |
| Do you trust our recommendations? | What makes you trust or distrust them? |

---

# Avoid Double-Barreled Questions

A double-barreled question asks two things at once.

Bad:

> "Was the application easy and fast?"

What if it was easy but slow?

Better:

> "How easy was the application process?"

Then:

> "How long did it take?"

---

# Avoid Hypothetical Questions

Bad:

> "If we built this feature, would you use it?"

Users often give socially desirable answers.

Better:

> "How do you currently solve this problem?"

---

# Avoid Asking for Solutions Too Early

Bad:

> "What feature should we build?"

Better:

> "Tell me about the last time you experienced this problem."

Let the problem emerge before discussing solutions.

---

# When to Show a Prototype

Prototype evaluation is useful when you need feedback on a specific solution.

But don't show it too early.

First understand:

> The user's current experience.

Otherwise, the prototype can dominate the conversation.

---

# Prototype Interview

A useful sequence:

```text
Current Behavior
      ↓
Current Problem
      ↓
Show Prototype
      ↓
Observe Behavior
      ↓
Ask Questions
```

---

# Observe Before Asking

Suppose the user is testing your prototype.

Instead of immediately saying:

> "Was that easy?"

Watch what they do.

Look for:

- Hesitation
- Wrong clicks
- Confusion
- Searching
- Misinterpretation
- Questions

Behavior can reveal usability problems that users don't mention.

---

# Active Listening

Active listening means:

- Paying attention
- Not interrupting
- Asking relevant follow-ups
- Reflecting what you heard
- Avoiding premature conclusions

---

# Example

User:

> "I don't really use the dashboard."

Bad response:

> "Okay. Our new dashboard will solve that."

Better:

> "What do you usually use instead?"

Now you're learning.

---

# Silence Is Useful

Don't be afraid of silence.

Sometimes after asking a question, wait.

Users may continue talking and reveal something important.

---

# Don't Fill Every Silence

PMs sometimes become uncomfortable and start talking.

Instead:

> Ask the question.

> Wait.

> Listen.

---

# Take Notes

Don't rely entirely on memory.

Capture:

- Exact behaviors
- Problems
- Quotes
- Context
- Emotions
- Workarounds
- Unexpected observations

Avoid writing only your interpretation.

---

# Observation vs Interpretation

Example:

### Observation

> User spent 45 seconds searching for the "Submit Claim" button.

### Interpretation

> The interface may not make the next action obvious.

Keep these separate.

---

# Interview Notes Example

```text
Participant: P05

Context:
Uses platform 2–3 times/month.

Problem:
Claim submission.

Observation:
Checked FAQ before submitting.

Quote:
"I wasn't sure which documents they needed."

Workaround:
Called customer support.

Impact:
Submission delayed by one day.

Potential insight:
Document requirements are unclear.
```

---

# After the Interviews

Don't immediately jump to:

> "Let's build X."

First analyze the evidence.

---

# Affinity Mapping

A simple technique is to group similar observations.

Example:

```text
User 1 → Doesn't understand documents
User 2 → Doesn't understand documents
User 3 → Missing required document
User 4 → Calls support for documents
User 5 → Checks FAQ
```

Possible theme:

> **Document requirements are unclear.**

---

# From Quotes to Insights

Raw data:

> "I didn't know what documents to upload."

Another:

> "I had to call support."

Another:

> "The FAQ wasn't clear."

Potential insight:

> Users lack confidence that they have the correct documentation before submitting a claim.

That's more useful than simply collecting quotes.

---

# Pattern Recognition

One interview is a story.

Multiple interviews can reveal a pattern.

Example:

| Finding | Participants |
|---|---:|
| Confused about documents | 6/8 |
| Called support | 4/8 |
| Abandoned claim | 2/8 |
| Technical error | 1/8 |

Now you have evidence that document clarity may be a major issue.

---

# Don't Treat "6 out of 8" as Statistical Proof

Eight interviews are not necessarily representative of the entire user population.

Use the findings as:

> Qualitative evidence.

Then combine with quantitative data when possible.

---

# Triangulation

Triangulation means looking at a problem using multiple sources.

For example:

### Interviews

Users say document requirements are unclear.

### Support tickets

Many tickets ask which documents are required.

### Analytics

Users frequently revisit the document information page.

Together:

> Stronger evidence of the problem.

---

# Research Repository

For larger teams, maintain a repository of:

- Interview notes
- Research findings
- User quotes
- Themes
- Segments
- Research reports

This prevents the organization from repeatedly rediscovering the same information.

---

# Turning Research Into Product Insights

A useful chain is:

```text
Observation
     ↓
Pattern
     ↓
Insight
     ↓
Problem
     ↓
Opportunity
     ↓
Product Decision
```

---

# Example

### Observation

Users frequently call support during claim submission.

### Pattern

Most calls are related to required documents.

### Insight

Users lack confidence about which documents are required.

### Problem

Users cannot determine documentation requirements easily.

### Opportunity

Help users understand required documents before submission.

### Product Decision

Explore contextual document guidance.

---

# Research Should Change Decisions

This is an important principle.

If research never changes what you do:

> You may not be asking the right questions.

Good research can lead to:

- Building something
- Not building something
- Changing scope
- Changing the target user
- Changing the problem definition
- Delaying a decision
- Running another experiment

---

# Common User Research Mistakes

## Mistake #1: Selling Instead of Learning

Bad:

> "We built this amazing feature. Would you like it?"

You're marketing, not researching.

---

# Mistake #2: Asking What Users Want You to Build

Users are experts in their problems.

They are not necessarily experts in designing your product.

---

# Mistake #3: Asking Only About the Future

Future behavior is uncertain.

Focus on actual experiences.

---

# Mistake #4: Interviewing Only Power Users

Power users may have very different needs.

---

# Mistake #5: Leading the Participant

You want honest evidence, not confirmation.

---

# Mistake #6: Talking Too Much

If you're explaining your product for half the interview, you're losing research time.

---

# Mistake #7: Jumping to Solutions

Don't turn every observation into a feature.

---

# Mistake #8: Ignoring Contradictory Evidence

If one participant says something unexpected, investigate it.

It may reveal a new segment or problem.

---

# Mistake #9: Not Following Up

Sometimes the most valuable insight is hidden behind one sentence.

User:

> "I stopped using it."

Don't move on.

Ask:

> "Can you tell me what happened?"

---

Here is a reusable structure.

## Introduction

> "Thanks for joining. I'd like to understand how you currently handle [activity]. There are no right or wrong answers. I'm interested in your real experience."

---

## Context

> "How do you currently use [product/process]?"

---

## Recent Experience

> "Tell me about the last time you [task]."

---

## Deep Dive

> "What happened next?"

> "What were you trying to accomplish?"

> "What was difficult?"

> "What did you do?"

---

## Current Solution

> "How do you solve this today?"

> "What other tools or methods do you use?"

---

## Impact

# The Golden Rule

One of the most useful rules in customer interviews:

> **Ask about what happened, not what might happen.**

Instead of:

> "Would you use an automated claims assistant?"

Ask:

> "Tell me about the last time you needed help with a claim."

---

# Interview Example: LinkedIn

Suppose you want to understand why users don't apply for jobs.

### Bad interview

> "Would a better job recommendation system make you apply more?"

This assumes the problem is recommendations.

---

### Better interview

> "Tell me about the last time you searched for a job on LinkedIn."

Follow up:

> "What were you looking for?"

> "How did you decide which jobs to open?"

> "Which jobs did you apply to?"

> "Were there jobs you considered but didn't apply to?"

> "Why?"

Now you may discover:

> Users don't apply because they aren't confident they meet the job requirements.

That's a much more valuable insight.

---

# Interview Example: Stock Trading

Research question:

> Why don't beginner users make their first trade?

Start with:

> "Tell me about the last time you considered making an investment."

Follow up:

> "What were you considering?"

> "How did you decide what to invest in?"

> "What information did you look for?"

> "What made you comfortable or uncomfortable?"

> "Did you eventually invest?"

> "Why or why not?"

This can reveal the real barrier.

---

# Interview Example: Insurance

Research question:

> Why do customers abandon claims?

Start with:

> "Tell me about the last time you submitted a claim."

Then explore:

- What happened?
- What information did they need?
- What did they expect?
- Where did they get confused?
- Did they contact support?
- Did they complete the claim?

You are trying to understand the experience, not validate a feature.

```text
        ↓

        ↓
6. Conduct interviews
        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

```

# Interview Quality Checklist

Before the interview:

- Do I know what I want to learn?
- Am I talking to the right users?
- Are my questions open-ended?
- Am I avoiding leading questions?
- Am I focusing on real behavior?

During the interview:

- Am I listening?
- Am I asking follow-ups?
- Am I allowing silence?
- Am I avoiding selling?
- Am I capturing observations?

After the interview:

- What did I learn?
- What patterns appeared?
- What surprised me?
- What assumptions changed?
- What decision does this affect?

# Interview Question

## How do you conduct customer interviews?

### Strong Answer

I start by defining the product decision and research questions I need to answer. Then I recruit participants who represent the relevant user segment. During the interview, I focus primarily on recent real experiences rather than hypothetical opinions. I ask open-ended questions, use follow-ups to understand behavior and context, and avoid leading participants toward a particular answer. Afterward, I look for recurring patterns across interviews and combine those findings with quantitative data to inform product decisions.

---

# Interview Question 2

## How do you know if a customer problem is important?

### Strong Answer

I look at several signals: frequency, severity, impact, the number and type of users affected, and how users currently solve the problem. Existing workarounds are especially useful because they show whether customers are already investing time, money, or effort to address the problem. I also validate qualitative findings with quantitative data when possible.

---

# Interview Question 3

## What would you do if users give conflicting feedback?

### Strong Answer

I wouldn't immediately assume that one group is wrong. I would look for differences in user segments, context, behavior, and frequency of the problem. I would also compare the qualitative feedback with behavioral and quantitative data. Conflicting feedback can sometimes reveal that we're dealing with different user needs rather than a single universal problem.

You are the PM of a digital insurance platform.

# Advanced Exercise

Conduct **5 real customer interviews** if you have access to users.

Do not start by showing them your product idea.

Instead:

1. Define one research question.
2. Interview five relevant users.
3. Ask about recent real experiences.
4. Capture exact observations.
5. Identify recurring patterns.
6. Write three insights.
7. Identify what assumptions changed.
8. Decide what you would investigate next.

The objective is not to collect feature requests.

# PM Reflection

Ask yourself:

- Do I talk to customers regularly?
- Do I ask about real behavior or hypothetical preferences?
- Do I accidentally lead users toward my preferred answer?
- Do I spend more time talking than listening?
- Do I follow up when something interesting appears?
- Do I understand how customers solve problems today?
- Do I distinguish observations from interpretations?
- Do my research findings actually influence product decisions?

Remember:

> **The best customer interview is not the one where the customer agrees with your idea. It is the one where you learn something that changes your understanding of the problem.**

---

# Key Takeaways

1. User research reduces uncertainty.
2. Customer interviews are primarily for understanding behavior, problems, and context.
3. Start with a research question, not a feature.
4. Ask about recent real experiences.
5. Behavior is usually more reliable than hypothetical opinions.
6. Avoid leading and double-barreled questions.
7. Use follow-up questions to uncover deeper insights.
8. Listen more than you speak.
9. Look for patterns across multiple users.
10. Separate observations from interpretations.
11. Combine qualitative and quantitative evidence.
12. Existing workarounds can reveal problem severity.
13. Research should influence product decisions.
14. Good research can prove your assumption wrong—and that's valuable.
15. The goal is not more interviews; it is better product decisions.
