# Introduction: The Problem Comes First

A common mistake in product development:

```text
Idea
 ↓
Feature
 ↓
Development
 ↓
Launch
 ↓
Hope users like it
```

A better approach:

```text
Customer
 ↓
Problem
 ↓
Validation
 ↓
Solution
 ↓
Development
 ↓
Measure Impact
```

Successful products begin with understanding problems.

---

# What Is a Product Problem?

A product problem is a gap between:

- The customer's current situation
- The desired outcome they want to achieve

Example:

Current situation:

> Users spend 20 minutes manually comparing investment options.

Desired outcome:

> Users want to make confident investment decisions quickly.

Problem:

> Users lack simple tools to evaluate investment choices.

---

# Problem vs Symptom vs Solution

Product Managers must distinguish these three concepts.

---

# Symptom

A visible sign that something is wrong.

Example:

> "Users abandon onboarding."

This is not the real problem.

---

# Problem

The underlying reason causing the symptom.

Example:

> "Users do not understand why identity verification is required."

---

# Solution

A possible way to solve the problem.

Example:

> "Add an onboarding explanation screen."

---

## Example

For a trading platform:

### Symptom

Low first-trade completion rate.

### Possible Problems

- Users don't understand trading terms.
- Users fear losing money.
- The order process is confusing.

### Possible Solutions

- Educational content.
- Guided trading.
- Simplified order flow.

---

# Why Problem Validation Matters

Imagine a team spends six months building:

- New dashboard
- AI recommendations
- Advanced analytics

After launch:

Nobody uses them.

Why?

The team solved a problem customers did not consider important.

Problem validation helps answer:

- Does this problem exist?
- Who experiences it?
- How painful is it?
- How often does it happen?
- Are customers already trying to solve it?

---

# The Problem Discovery Process

A practical process:

```text
Observe Users
      ↓
Identify Problems
      ↓
Understand Root Causes
      ↓
Write Problem Statement
      ↓
Validate Importance
      ↓
Prioritize Problems
```

---

# Step 1: Observe Users

Problems can come from many sources.

## Customer Interviews

Example:

Users say:

> "I don't know which stock to buy."

---

## Analytics

Example:

Data shows:

> 70% of users leave before making their first trade.

---

## Support Tickets

Example:

Many customers ask:

> "How do I withdraw money?"

---

## Reviews

Example:

App store reviews reveal:

> Users complain about account verification.

---

## Sales Feedback

Sales teams hear customer objections directly.

---

# Step 2: Identify Customer Problems

A good problem should describe:

- Who has the problem
- What they struggle with
- Why it matters

---

## Weak Problem Statement

> "Users need a better dashboard."

Why is it weak?

Because it already suggests a solution.

---

## Strong Problem Statement

> "Beginner investors struggle to understand portfolio performance, causing uncertainty and reducing their confidence in investing."

This describes:

- User
- Problem
- Impact

---

# Problem Statement Template

Use this format:

> [User type] struggles with [problem] when [situation], causing [negative impact].

Example:

> New investors struggle to choose investments when they first enter the market, causing hesitation and low first-trade completion.

---

# Step 3: Find Root Causes

The first problem you hear is often not the real problem.

Use the:

# Five Whys Technique

Example:

Problem:

Users do not complete their first trade.

---

Why?

Because they are unsure what to buy.

---

Why?

Because they do not understand investment options.

---

Why?

Because financial information is too complex.

---

Why?

Because the product is designed for experienced investors.

---

Root cause:

The product does not support beginner investors.

---

# Step 4: Validate the Problem

Before solving, test whether the problem is real.

Ask:

## 1. Does the problem exist?

Are customers actually experiencing it?

---

## 2. How frequent is it?

Does it happen once or repeatedly?

---

## 3. How painful is it?

Does it create frustration or business impact?

---

## 4. Are customers already trying to solve it?

Existing solutions indicate importance.

---

# Problem Validation Methods

## 1. Customer Interviews

Best for:

- Understanding motivations
- Discovering pain points

Example questions:

- Tell me about the last time you experienced this problem.
- How did you solve it?
- What was frustrating?
- What alternatives did you try?

---

## 2. Surveys

Best for:

- Measuring problem frequency

Example:

> How often do you struggle with choosing investments?

---

## 3. Analytics

Best for:

- Measuring behavior

Example:

- Drop-off points
- Conversion rates
- Feature usage

---

## 4. Support Data

Best for:

- Finding recurring frustrations

Example:

1000 customers ask about the same issue.

---

# Measuring Problem Importance

Not every problem deserves solving.

A useful evaluation:

---

# Reach

How many users experience the problem?

Example:

10% of users vs 80% of users.

---

# Frequency

How often does it happen?

Daily problems are often more valuable than occasional problems.

---

# Severity

How painful is it?

A small inconvenience is different from a major blocker.

---

# Business Impact

Does solving it improve:

- Revenue?
- Retention?
- Acquisition?
- Customer satisfaction?

---

# Strategic Fit

Does solving this problem support company goals?

---

# Problem Opportunity Score

A simple framework:

```text
Problem Score =

Reach × Frequency × Severity × Strategic Fit
```

The exact numbers are less important than structured thinking.

---

# Example: LinkedIn

Business goal:

Increase job applications.

---

## Observation

Many users view jobs but don't apply.

---

## Possible Problems

Problem 1:

Applications take too long.

Problem 2:

Users don't know if they are qualified.

Problem 3:

Job descriptions are unclear.

---

## Validation

Research shows:

- 60% of users abandon long applications.
- Many users hesitate because they don't understand requirements.

---

## Opportunity

Improve confidence and reduce application effort.

---

# Example: Stock Trading Platform

Business goal:

Increase active investors.

---

## Observation

Users create accounts but never trade.

---

## Possible Problems

- Fear of losing money.
- Lack of investment knowledge.
- Complex trading interface.

---

## Research Findings

Users say:

> "I don't know where to start."

---

## Validated Problem

Beginner investors need guidance before making their first investment decision.

---

## Possible Solutions

- Educational onboarding.
- Investment simulator.
- Guided portfolio creation.

# Problem Prioritization Frameworks

## Impact vs Effort Matrix

```text
High Impact
    |
    |   Quick Wins
    |
    |
    |              Big Projects
    |
    |
    |________________________
          Low Effort
```

Prioritize:

- High impact
- Reasonable effort

---

# RICE Framework

A popular prioritization method:

```text
RICE Score =

Reach × Impact × Confidence
--------------------------
Effort
```

Used by many product teams.

# Common Problem Discovery Mistakes

## Mistake #1

Starting with solutions.

Bad:

> "We need an AI assistant."

Better:

> "What problem would an AI assistant solve?"

---

## Mistake #2

Confusing customer requests with problems.

Customer:

> "Add dark mode."

Problem:

> "The app is uncomfortable to use at night."

---

## Mistake #3

Assuming every problem is valuable.

Some problems are real but not important.

---

## Mistake #4

Ignoring evidence.

A problem should be supported by:

- Research
- Data
- Customer behavior

---

## Mistake #5

Solving problems for everyone.

Focus on specific users first.

---

# Interview Question

## Question

How do you validate whether a customer problem is worth solving?

## Strong Answer

I validate problems by understanding who experiences them, how frequently they occur, how painful they are, and what impact they have on customers and the business. I use customer interviews, analytics, surveys, and other evidence sources before deciding whether the problem deserves investment.

---

# Mini Case Study

You are the PM of a food delivery app.

The CEO says:

> "We should add a subscription plan."

Before building it:

Answer:

1. What customer problem might a subscription solve?
2. Who experiences this problem?
3. How would you validate the problem?
4. What alternatives already exist?
5. What evidence would convince you to build it?

---

# Key Takeaways

1. Great products solve important problems.
2. Problems are different from symptoms and solutions.
3. Validate problems before building solutions.
4. Use research and data to understand customer pain.
5. Prioritize problems based on impact and importance.
6. Avoid falling in love with solutions.
7. Strong PMs are problem finders, not feature builders.
